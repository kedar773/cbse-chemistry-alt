1.1 · Why Bonds Form — The Electrostatic & Quantum Origin

Atoms bond because the bonded state is energetically lower than the isolated-atom state. The driving forces are deeply quantum mechanical, not merely "atoms wanting full shells":

  • Electrostatic potential energy (V = kq₁q₂/r) — when oppositely charged species (nuclei and electrons) come close, attractive Coulombic terms scale as −1/r, dominating the nuclear–nuclear repulsion (+1/r) at equilibrium bond length.
  • Kinetic energy lowering — in the bonded state, electrons occupy lower-energy, more delocalized molecular orbitals (MO theory), reducing their kinetic energy per the Virial Theorem (2⟨T⟩ = −⟨V⟩ for bound systems).
  • Exchange & correlation energy — electrons of like spin avoid each other (Fermi hole), which paradoxically reduces repulsion when bonds form.

1.2 · Kossel–Lewis Approach (1916)

Foundational Postulates

  1. Noble gas configuration drive: Atoms achieve ns² np⁶ (octet) by losing, gaining, or sharing electrons — a heuristic, not a law (it fails for H, Be, B, expanded octets of period 3+).
  2. Ionic bond = full electron transfer; covalent bond = sharing (Lewis's key insight, refined by Heitler–London in 1927).
  3. Lewis dot symbols represent valence electrons as dots; bonds as shared pairs (lines).

⚡ Octet Rule & Its Exceptions High Yield

Exception Type Examples Reason
Incomplete octet BeCl₂, BF₃, AlCl₃ Central atom has fewer than 8 e⁻; p-block elements of period 2 cannot expand octet (no d-orbitals accessible at that energy).
Expanded octet PF₅, SF₆, IF₇, H₂SO₄ Period 3+ atoms can use empty 3d orbitals; not actually d-orbital participation (modern view: ionic 3c–4e bonds), but octet concept still useful.
Odd-electron species NO, NO₂, ClO₂ Cannot satisfy octet; bond order fractional.
Formal charge instability CO (best Lewis with FC, not octet) Triple bond C≡O minimizes formal charges.

Formal Charge — The "Best Lewis" Selector High Yield

FC = (valence e⁻ of free atom) − (non-bonding e⁻) − ½(bonding e⁻)

Rule for choosing dominant resonance structure: the structure with (i) minimum formal charges, (ii) negative FC on the most electronegative atom, and (iii) charges closest to zero wins. 📌 See Q 4.7

1.3 · Bond Parameters — The Quantitative Backbone

① Bond Length (pm)

Equilibrium internuclear distance. Order:

Triple < Double < Single
H–F < H–Cl < H–Br < H–I
As bond order ↑, bond length ↓

② Bond Enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹)

Energy to break 1 mol of bonds (gas phase, 298 K). Higher bond order ⇒ higher bond enthalpy. Average values used for polyatomic molecules (e.g., O–H in H₂O differs in H₂O₂).

③ Bond Angle (°)

Influenced by central atom hybridization, lone pair repulsion, electronegativity of substituents. Deviation from ideal is the key VSEPR diagnostic.

④ Dipole Moment (μ, Debye) High Yield

μ = q × d    (1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m)

Vector quantity. Symmetrical molecules (BF₃, CO₂, CCl₄, SF₆) have μ = 0 even with polar bonds, because bond dipoles cancel. Asymmetrical (H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃) have net μ.

Resonance & Resonance Energy

When no single Lewis structure adequately represents a molecule, the true structure is a resonance hybrid of contributing forms. Resonance energy = experimental bond energy − calculated from any single structure. Example: benzene, C₆H₆, has resonance energy ~150 kJ mol⁻¹ (this is why all C–C bonds are equal at 139 pm, between single 154 pm and double 134 pm).

Polarity & Partial Ionic Character Electronegativity

Pauling's electronegativity (χ) scale: F = 4.0, O = 3.5, N = 3.0, C = 2.5, H = 2.1. Δχ between bonded atoms predicts bond polarity:

  • Δχ < 0.5 → essentially nonpolar covalent
  • 0.5 ≤ Δχ < 1.7 → polar covalent (e.g., H–Cl: Δχ = 0.9)
  • Δχ ≥ 1.7 → ionic (e.g., NaCl: Δχ = 2.1)

Even "ionic" NaCl is ~80% ionic / 20% covalent in reality (Fajans' rules): small high-charge cation + large polarizable anion → covalent character.

1.4 · VSEPR Theory — Gillespie–Nyholm (1957)

Core Principle

The shape of a polyatomic molecule is determined by minimizing the electrostatic repulsion among electron pairs (both bonding and lone) in the valence shell of the central atom. The geometry is set by the steric number VSEPR number = (σ-bonds + lone pairs).

Ideal Geometries Reference Table

Steric # BP LP Geometry Ideal Angles Examples
2 2 0 Linear 180° BeCl₂, CO₂
3 3 0 Trigonal planar 120° BF₃, SO₃
3 2 1 Bent (V-shape) <120° SO₂, O₃
4 4 0 Tetrahedral 109.5° CH₄, NH₄⁺
4 3 1 Trigonal pyramidal ~107° NH₃
4 2 2 Bent ~104.5° H₂O
5 5 0 Trigonal bipyramidal 90°, 120° PCl₅
5 4 1 See-saw — SF₄
5 3 2 T-shaped — ClF₃
6 6 0 Octahedral 90° SF₆
6 5 1 Square pyramidal — BrF₅
6 4 2 Square planar — XeF₄

Repulsion Hierarchy (the "why" of anomalies)

lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp

Mechanistic reason: A lone pair is held by one nucleus, so its electron cloud spreads out more (occupies more angular space) than a bonding pair shared between two nuclei. This extra electron density pushes other pairs closer, compressing bond angles below the ideal.

Numerical evidence:
  • CH₄: 109.5° (4 bp, 0 lp — no compression)
  • NH₃: 107° (3 bp, 1 lp — lp–bp repulsion shrinks bp–bp angle by 2.5°)
  • H₂O: 104.5° (2 bp, 2 lp — both lp–bp and lp–lp compress angle by 5°)

2.1 · Valence Bond Theory (VBT) — Heitler–London (1927), Pauling (1931)

Quantum Roots

When two H atoms approach, their 1s atomic orbitals overlap. Solving the Schrödinger equation for H₂ yields a bonding MO E = −458 kJ mol⁻¹ (constructive interference of wavefunctions, in-phase) and an antibonding MO E = +0.05 kJ mol⁻¹ (destructive interference, out-of-phase). The bonding state is lower because electrons accumulate between the nuclei, where they are simultaneously attracted to both positive charges (a Coulombic win that beats the nuclear–nuclear repulsion).

Two Covalent Bond Types

σ (Sigma) Bond

  • Formed by axial (head-on) overlap
  • Cylindrically symmetric about the internuclear axis
  • Strongest type of covalent bond
  • Examples: s–s, s–p, p–p (head-on), all hybrid–hybrid
  • Single bonds are always σ

π (Pi) Bond

  • Formed by lateral (sideways) overlap of p-orbitals
  • Two electron-cloud lobes above & below the internuclear axis (nodal plane contains the axis)
  • Weaker than σ (less effective overlap)
  • Restricted rotation → cis/trans (geometric) isomerism
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π; Triple = 1σ + 2π

2.2 · Hybridization — Pauling's Construct

Conceptual Framework

To explain observed equivalent bond lengths in CH₄ (all four C–H are 109 pm), Linus Pauling proposed that the central atom's valence orbitals mathematically mix (linear combination) to form new, equivalent hybrid orbitals oriented to maximize mutual separation. The number of hybrids = number of orbitals mixed in.

Hybridization is a model, not a physical reality; it is the simplest single-determinant description that reproduces molecular geometry. It depends on the observed geometry, not the other way around — a common exam trap.

Hybridization Catalogue

Type Orbitals Mixed Geometry Angle Example
sp 1s + 1p Linear 180° BeCl₂, C₂H₂ (C atoms), CO₂
sp² 1s + 2p Trigonal planar 120° BF₃, C₂H₄ (C atoms), SO₃
sp³ 1s + 3p Tetrahedral 109.5° CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, CCl₄
sp³d 1s + 3p + 1d Trigonal bipyramidal 90°/120° PCl₅, SF₄, ClF₃
sp³d² 1s + 3p + 2d Octahedral 90° SF₆, BrF₅, XeF₄

Counting σ-bonds + lone pairs to find hybridization High Yield

H = ½ × (V + M − C + A)

Where V = valence e⁻ of central atom, M = monovalent atoms, C = cation charge, A = anion charge. H = 2 → sp, 3 → sp², 4 → sp³, 5 → sp³d, 6 → sp³d², 7 → sp³d³.

The d-orbital Debate (Modern View)

Important nuance for advanced study: Modern computational chemistry (Gilheany, Reed–Weinhold) shows that period-3 elements like S in SF₆ use not 3d orbitals (which are too high in energy), but rather ionic 3-center-4-electron (3c-4e) bonds — a hypervalent description without d-orbital participation. For CBSE Class 11, the sp³d² explanation is still expected.

2.3 · Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) — Hund, Mulliken, Herzberg

Core Idea

In MOT, atomic orbitals of all atoms in a molecule combine to form molecular orbitals that belong to the whole molecule. Each MO can hold 2 electrons (with opposite spins, per Pauli). Electrons are filled following the Aufbau principle, just like in atoms.

Constructive vs. Destructive Interference (g/u Symmetry)

  • g (gerade, "even"): wavefunction unchanged under inversion about the center. Bonding MOs are usually g.
  • u (ungerade, "odd"): wavefunction changes sign under inversion. Antibonding MOs are usually u.
  • Rule: g × g = g; u × u = g; g × u = u (parity multiplication). A σ bond from s–s is σg; σ* is σu*.

Bond Order — The Master Diagnostic

Bond Order (BO) = ½ × (Nb − Na)

where Nb = electrons in bonding MOs, Na = electrons in antibonding MOs.

  • BO = 0 → molecule doesn't exist (e.g., He₂)
  • BO = 1 → single bond (H₂, F₂, B₂)
  • BO = 2 → double bond (O₂, C₂)
  • BO = 3 → triple bond (N₂, CO)
  • BO fractional → ions like O₂⁺ (2.5), NO (2.5)

Magnetic Behavior

Paramagnetic High Yield

Has unpaired electrons. Attracted to a magnetic field. MO theory successfully predicts O₂ as paramagnetic (2 unpaired e⁻ in π*2p), which VBT could not explain!

Diamagnetic

All electrons paired. Weakly repelled by magnetic field. Examples: N₂, CO, BF₃.

Energy Level Diagrams — Two Orderings

Critical for CBSE:
  • For O₂, F₂, Ne₂ (and beyond): σ2pz < π2px = π2py < π*2px = π*2py < σ*2pz
  • For B₂, C₂, N₂ (and lighter): π2px = π2py < σ2pz < π*2px = π*2py < σ*2pz
The reversal happens because for small atoms, p-orbital overlap is poor and s–p mixing is significant, destabilizing σ2p relative to π2p.

2.4 · Hydrogen Bonding High Yield

A special dipole–dipole attraction (not a true "bond" in the covalent sense) between an H atom bonded to a highly electronegative atom (F, O, N) and a lone pair on another F/O/N. Conditions required:

  1. High ΔEN between H and donor atom (only F–H, O–H, N–H qualify strongly)
  2. Small size of donor atom (concentrates the partial positive on H)
  3. Lone pair on acceptor atom for directionality

Effects: abnormally high boiling points (H₂O > H₂S, HF > HCl), ice floats (open hexagonal lattice in solid, denser in liquid), DNA base pairing, protein secondary structure.

In-text & Exercise Questions

Q 4.1 · Explain the formation of a chemical bond.

A chemical bond forms when two or more atoms come close enough for their valence electrons and nuclei to interact in a way that lowers the total energy of the system. The lowering comes from:

  1. Electrostatic attraction between nuclei and shared/gained electrons
  2. Quantum mechanical exchange energy and delocalization
  3. Achieving a lower-energy electronic configuration (often a noble-gas configuration as a heuristic)

Bond formation releases energy (∆H < 0); breaking requires an equivalent input (bond enthalpy).

Q 4.2 · Write the Lewis dot symbols for: P, S, Br, I, Xe, As, NO, NO⁺, NO⁻, SO₂, SO₃, H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄

Lewis dot symbols show valence electrons as dots around the element symbol:

  • P (Group 15): ·P···· with 1 lone pair + 3 single electrons (5 total)
  • S (Group 16): ··S·· with 2 lone pairs + 2 single electrons (6 total)
  • Br, I (Group 17): 3 lone pairs + 1 single electron (7 total)
  • Xe (Group 18): 4 lone pairs (8 total)
  • As (Group 15): similar to P with 5 dots

For molecules/ions we draw the full Lewis structure with shared pairs and lone pairs; e.g. NO (11 e⁻) has one unpaired e⁻ and a bond order 2.5; NO⁺ is isoelectronic with N₂ and has BO = 3; NO⁻ is isoelectronic with O₂ and has BO = 2 with 1 unpaired e⁻ (paramagnetic).

Q 4.3 · Why does H₂O have a bent structure while BeF₂ is linear?

Hybridization + VSEPR determine the geometry:

  • BeF₂: Be has 2 valence e⁻ + 2 from 2 F = 4 electrons around Be → steric # = 2 → sp hybridization → linear (180°). No lone pairs on Be.
  • H₂O: O has 6 valence e⁻, forms 2 bonds → 2 BP + 2 LP → steric # = 4 → sp³ → tetrahedral electron geometry, but the 2 lone pairs compress the H–O–H angle to 104.5° → bent shape.

So even though both have 2 bonds, the lone pairs on O force a non-linear arrangement. The underlying physics: lone pairs occupy more angular volume than bonding pairs (lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp repulsion).

Q 4.4 · Explain why CO₂ is linear, SO₂ is bent.

Same reasoning: count lone pairs on the central atom.

  • CO₂: C has 4 valence e⁻, forms 2 double bonds, 0 lone pairs on C → sp → linear (180°). Each O has 2 lone pairs but they're on terminal atoms.
  • SO₂: S has 6 valence e⁻, forms 2 bonds (often drawn with one double), with 1 lone pair on S → sp² → bent (~119°).
Q 4.5 · Why is NH₃ pyramidal while BF₃ is planar?
  • BF₃: B has 3 valence e⁻, forms 3 B–F bonds, 0 lone pairs → sp² → trigonal planar (120°). Boron is an incomplete-octet exception.
  • NH₃: N has 5 valence e⁻, forms 3 N–H bonds, 1 lone pair → sp³ → tetrahedral e⁻ geometry but molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal (~107°).

Key insight: BF₃ has an empty p-orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane (Lewis acid character — accepts a lone pair from NH₃ to form the F₃B←:NH₃ adduct).

Q 4.7 · The "best" Lewis structure of CO — apply formal charge rules. 📌 (cross-referenced from Tab 1)

Carbon monoxide has 14 valence electrons total. Three plausible structures:

  1. :C=O: with lone pair on each → C has incomplete octet
  2. :C≡O: triple bond + 1 lone pair on C and 1 on O
  3. :C⁻=O⁺: (carbenoid form)

Compute FC for structure (ii):

C: FC = 4 − 2 − 3 = −1
O: FC = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1

Although this places negative FC on C (less electronegative than O), it is still the dominant structure because the alternative (i) violates the octet rule. The molecule is in fact slightly polar with a small negative end on C (μ = 0.112 D, with C as the δ⁻ end) — this is why CO binds to metal centers through C, not O.

Q 4.8 · Which has higher dipole moment: NH₃ (1.47 D) or NF₃ (0.24 D)? Explain. High Yield

Both are pyramidal (sp³, 1 LP). On paper, they should have similar μ. The huge difference comes from direction of individual bond dipoles:

  • NH₃: N is more electronegative than H (χ: 3.04 vs 2.20), so each N–H dipole points toward N. Combined with the lone pair dipole (which adds), the net μ is large (1.47 D).
  • NF₃: F is more electronegative than N (χ: 3.98 vs 3.04), so each N–F dipole points away from N. These three dipoles point opposite to the lone pair dipole on N. The result: partial cancellation, μ = 0.24 D only.

Key learning: In NH₃, bond dipoles and lone pair dipole reinforce; in NF₃ they oppose. Both are sp³ with 1 LP, but EN of substituents flips the outcome.

Q 4.9 · Compare bond order and stability of O₂, O₂⁺, O₂⁻ (superoxide), O₂²⁻ (peroxide). High Yield

From MO filling (O₂ has 16 e⁻; add/remove e⁻ in the π*2p antibonding MOs):

Species Total e⁻ π* e⁻ BO Magnetism
O₂⁺ (dioxygenyl) 15 1 2.5 Paramagnetic (1 unpaired)
O₂ 16 2 2.0 Paramagnetic (2 unpaired)
O₂⁻ (superoxide) 17 3 1.5 Paramagnetic (1 unpaired)
O₂²⁻ (peroxide) 18 4 1.0 Diamagnetic

Stability trend: O₂⁺ > O₂ > O₂⁻ > O₂²⁻. As electrons are added to antibonding MOs, BO drops, bond length increases, and stability decreases. Peroxide O–O bond is anomalously long (~149 pm) and weak, matching the MO prediction.

Q 4.10 · Use MO theory to explain why He₂ does not exist, but H₂ does.

Both have 2 electrons total filling σ1s. For H₂: 2 e⁻ in σ1s, 0 in σ*1s → BO = ½(2−0) = 1. Stable.

For He₂: σ1s² σ*1s² → BO = ½(2−2) = 0. The antibonding electrons exactly cancel the bonding stabilization, leaving a residual van der Waals attraction (very weak). No true He₂ molecule at ordinary conditions.

This is a beautiful MO prediction that VBT could not even formulate.

Q 4.11 · What is the hybridization of each C in ethyne (C₂H₂)? In ethene (C₂H₄)?
  • C₂H₂ (acetylene): each C forms 1 σ to H + 1 σ to other C + 2 π to other C. Steric # = 2 → sp. Linear geometry, 180° H–C–C.
  • C₂H₄ (ethylene): each C forms 2 σ to H + 1 σ to C + 1 π to C. Steric # = 3 → sp². Trigonal planar, 120° angles. Restricted C=C rotation → cis/trans isomerism.
  • C₂H₆ (ethane): each C forms 3 σ to H + 1 σ to C. Steric # = 4 → sp³. Free rotation about C–C single bond.
Q 4.12 · Why is H₂O liquid at room temperature but H₂S a gas? High Yield

Both are Group 16 hydrides (H₂X, X = O, S, Se, Te). Their molecular masses are similar (18 vs 34 g/mol — actually H₂S is heavier so should have higher BP if only mass mattered). But the boiling points are: H₂O 100°C, H₂S −60°C, H₂Se −41°C, H₂Te −2°C. Water is anomalously high, not H₂S anomalously low.

Cause: Hydrogen bonding. H₂O has the H bonded to highly electronegative, small O, so molecules associate via O–H···O bridges. H₂S lacks strong H-bonding (S is large, less electronegative). Extra energy (~40 kJ/mol) is needed to break H-bonds in H₂O, raising its BP dramatically.

Q 4.13 · Predict shapes of SF₄, ClF₃, BrF₅, XeF₄ using VSEPR.
  • SF₄: S has 6 e⁻, 4 bonds, 1 LP → sp³d → see-saw. Two distinct F environments: 2 axial (90° to LP) and 2 equatorial (120° to each other). Bond lengths: axial S–F ≈ 164 pm, equatorial S–F ≈ 154 pm.
  • ClF₃: Cl has 7 e⁻, 3 bonds, 2 LPs → sp³d → T-shaped. The 2 lone pairs occupy equatorial positions (to minimize lp–lp repulsion), leaving 2 equatorial and 1 axial F → T shape, with ~87.5° F–Cl–F angles.
  • BrF₅: Br has 7 e⁻, 5 bonds, 1 LP → sp³d² → square pyramidal. LP occupies one octahedral position; 5 F form the rest. F–Br–F angles slightly < 90° due to LP compression.
  • XeF₄: Xe has 8 e⁻, 4 bonds, 2 LPs → sp³d² → square planar. Both LPs trans to each other (180° apart) to minimize lp–lp repulsion. All four F at 90° to LP axes. μ = 0 D (perfectly symmetrical cancellation).

Q 4.15 · What is resonance? Draw resonance structures of benzene, CO₃²⁻, O₃.

Resonance describes molecules whose bonding cannot be captured by a single Lewis structure. The actual structure is a weighted hybrid of all valid contributors. Resonance structures are not real isomers that interconvert — they are a limitation of the Lewis model.

Benzene (C₆H₆): 5 equivalent structures (Kekulé 2 + 3 Dewar). The real structure has all C–C = 139 pm (between 154 single and 134 double) and resonance energy 150 kJ/mol.

CO₃²⁻: 3 equivalent structures with C=O at each position. Average: each C–O is 1.33 bond order, 129 pm.

O₃: 2 equivalent structures (O=O–O⁻ ↔ ⁻O–O=O). Average: each O–O bond is 1.5, length 128 pm.

Q 4.16 · Predict whether the following are diamagnetic or paramagnetic: O₂, N₂, B₂, H₂.
  • H₂: σ1s². All paired → diamagnetic.
  • B₂: 2 unpaired in π2p → paramagnetic.
  • N₂: all electrons paired in σ2p and π2p → diamagnetic.
  • O₂: 2 unpaired in π*2p → paramagnetic.
Q 4.17 · Why is bond angle in PH₃ (93.6°) much less than in NH₃ (107°)?

Both are sp³ with 1 LP. Why the difference?

The central atom in PH₃ uses essentially pure p-orbitals for bonding, leaving the lone pair in a nearly pure s-orbital. Pure s has spherical symmetry (no directional preference), so the bonds can collapse to ~90° (the natural angle between two p-orbitals on the same atom). In NH₃, the central N is small enough that the bonding pair and lone pair are more equivalent in energy, so true sp³ hybridization occurs.

This is an example of Drago's rule: heavier p-block hydrides avoid hybridization, with bonds formed from pure p-orbitals.

Q 4.18 · Explain the structure of H₃PO₄ and P₂O₅.

H₃PO₄: P has 5 valence e⁻, 4 bonds to oxygen (one P=O, three P–O–H). Steric # = 4 → sp³. Tetrahedral geometry around P. Note: P can have expanded octet in period 3.

P₂O₅ (P₄O₁₀): the actual molecular form is the cage structure P₄O₁₀ with 4 P atoms at corners of a tetrahedron, 10 O atoms bridging. Each P is sp³ (4 σ bonds to O). The structure has 4 terminal P=O and 12 bridging P–O–P.
