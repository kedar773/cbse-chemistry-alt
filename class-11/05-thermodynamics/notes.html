State Variables (p, V, T, n, U, H) describe the state of the system. ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

1.2 Internal Energy & the First Law of Thermodynamics

Internal Energy (U): Total energy stored within a system (translational, rotational, vibrational KE + PE of molecules, bonds, electrons).

First Law of Thermodynamics

\[ \Delta U = q + w \]

ΔUChange in internal energy (J or kJ)
qHeat exchanged (J) — +q = heat absorbed (system gains), −q = heat released
wWork done on/by the system (J) — +w = work done on the system, −w = work done by the system

Sign Convention (IUPAC): Both q and w are positive when energy enters the system, negative when energy leaves.

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation

The First Law is a statement of energy conservation. If a system absorbs heat q and work w is done on it, the total energy of the system increases by (q + w). Since energy cannot be created or destroyed:

\[ \Delta U_{\text{system}} + \Delta U_{\text{surroundings}} = 0 \]

For the system alone: \[ \Delta U = q + w \]

Cyclic Process: If the system returns to its initial state, ΔU = 0, so q = −w.

🔥 HOTS Application Strategies:
1. Sign Analysis: If a gas expands (−w) and absorbs heat (+q), ΔU = q − w. If the system is insulated (q = 0), then ΔU = w — all energy change is via work (adiabatic).
2. Cyclic Engines: In a cyclic process (ΔU = 0), the net heat absorbed equals the net work done: q = −w. This is the basis of all heat engine analysis.
3. Isolated System: If both q = 0 and w = 0, then ΔU = 0 — internal energy is constant.

1.3 Work: Pressure-Volume (PV) Work

Mechanical work done by/on a gas as it expands or contracts against an external pressure:

General PV Work

\[ w = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} P_{\text{ext}} \, dV \]

📐 Reversible Work Derivation

Derivation: Reversible Isothermal Work for an Ideal Gas

Step 1: For a reversible process, Pext = Pgas ± dP ≈ Pgas at every instant.

Step 2: From the ideal gas law: \[ P = \frac{nRT}{V} \]

Step 3: Substitute: \[ w_{\text{rev}} = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV = -nRT\int_{V_i}^{V_f}\frac{dV}{V} \]

Step 4: Integrate: \[ w_{\text{rev}} = -nRT\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i} = -2.303\,nRT\log\frac{V_f}{V_i} \]

SI Units: n (mol), R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T (K) → w in Joules.

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. Maximum work is obtained in a reversible expansion (area under curve is maximal).
2. For compression, the reversible process requires minimum work input.
3. Exam Tip: Compare wrev vs wirrev — for the same ΔV, |wrev| > |wirrev|.
📐 Irreversible Work Derivation

Derivation: Irreversible (Constant Pext) Work

If the external pressure is constant throughout the expansion:

\[ w_{\text{irrev}} = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} P_{\text{ext}}\,dV = -P_{\text{ext}}(V_f - V_i) = -P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V \]

For an ideal gas expanding irreversibly against constant atmospheric pressure: \[ w_{\text{irrev}} = -P_{\text{atm}}\Delta V \]

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. Free expansion (vacuum): Pext = 0 ⇒ w = 0. If also adiabatic (q = 0), then ΔU = 0 and ΔT = 0.
2. In a two-step irreversible process, the total work is the sum of work from each step.
3. Exam link: NCERT Q6.3 and Q6.4 deal directly with these calculations.

1.4 Enthalpy ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

Enthalpy is defined as: \[ H = U + pV \]

Most chemical reactions occur at constant pressure (open to atmosphere), not constant volume. Enthalpy accounts for the PV work automatically.

At constant pressure: \[ q_p = \Delta H \]

ΔH–ΔU Relationship

\[ \Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT \]

ΔHEnthalpy change (kJ mol⁻¹)
ΔUInternal energy change (kJ mol⁻¹)
Δng(moles of gaseous products) − (moles of gaseous reactants)
📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation: H = U + PV

At constant pressure: qp = ΔU + PΔV (from First Law, since w = −PΔV and ΔU = qp − PΔV)

Define H = U + PV: ΔH = ΔU + Δ(PV). At constant P: ΔH = ΔU + PΔV.

For reactions with gaseous species: PΔV = ΔngRT (assuming ideal gas behavior)

Therefore: \[ \Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT \]

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. If Δng > 0 (more gas moles produced), then ΔH > ΔU — the system has done work expanding, so extra heat is needed.
2. If Δng = 0, then ΔH = ΔU (e.g., N₂ + O₂ → 2NO).
3. If Δng < 0 (gas consumed, e.g., 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O(l)), ΔH < ΔU — surroundings do work on the system.
4. Real context: Combustion of methane: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). Δng = 1 − 3 = −2, so ΔH = ΔU − 2RT, meaning ΔH is more negative than ΔU.

1.5 Heat Capacity ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

Specific Heat Capacity (c): Heat required to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 K (J g⁻¹ K⁻¹).

Molar Heat Capacity (C): Heat required to raise 1 mol by 1 K (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).

\[ q = m c \Delta T = n C \Delta T \]

\[ C_p = \text{Molar heat capacity at constant pressure} \]

\[ C_v = \text{Molar heat capacity at constant volume} \]

Mayer's Relation (for ideal gases)

\[ C_p - C_v = R \]

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation: Cp − Cv = R

Step 1: At constant volume, dU = Cv dT (all heat goes to internal energy).

Step 2: At constant pressure, dH = Cp dT. But H = U + PV.

Step 3: dH = dU + d(PV) = CvdT + d(RT) = CvdT + RdT = (Cv + R)dT

Step 4: Comparing: Cp dT = (Cv + R) dT ⇒ Cp − Cv = R.

For monatomic ideal gas: Cv = (3/2)R, Cp = (5/2)R. For diatomic: Cv = (5/2)R, Cp = (7/2)R.

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. γ = Cp/Cv is the adiabatic index. For monatomic gas γ = 1.67, diatomic γ = 1.40, polyatomic γ ≈ 1.33.
2. In adiabatic processes: PVγ = constant, TVγ−1 = constant, TP(1−γ)/γ = constant.
3. Exam trick: For an ideal gas undergoing an adiabatic process, w = CvΔT = ΔU.

1.6 Calorimetry

Bomb Calorimeter (Constant Volume)

\[ q_V = C_{\text{cal}} \Delta T = \Delta U \]

📌 [See Solved Q 6.8] — direct NCERT application.

Coffee-Cup Calorimeter (Constant Pressure)

\[ q_p = m c \Delta T = \Delta H \]

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation: Bomb Calorimeter — ΔU from Temperature Rise

Step 1: In a bomb calorimeter, V = constant ⇒ w = 0, so ΔU = qV.

Step 2: The heat released by the combustion is absorbed by the calorimeter assembly (water + bomb + stirrer + thermometer).

Step 3: If the calorimeter has total heat capacity Ccal (in J K⁻¹), then qV = Ccal × ΔT.

Step 4: The internal energy change of the reaction is ΔU = −qV (negative because heat is released).

Step 5: To convert to per mole: ΔU = −CcalΔT / nfuel.

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. To find Ccal, burn a standard substance with known ΔU (e.g., benzoic acid).
2. Once Ccal is known, any unknown fuel's ΔU can be determined.
3. Exam link: If the water equivalent is given (W in g), then Ccal = W × 4.184 J K⁻¹.

1.7 Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

"The total enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is the same regardless of whether it occurs in a single step or multiple steps."

\[ \Delta_r H = \Delta_r H_1 + \Delta_r H_2 + \Delta_r H_3 + \cdots \]

📌 [See Solved Q 6.12 & Q 6.14]

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Conceptual Foundation

Enthalpy is a state function — ΔH depends only on initial and final states, not the path taken.

If reaction A → D can proceed via: (i) A → D directly (ΔH₁), or (ii) A → B → C → D (ΔH₂ + ΔH₃ + ΔH₄), then ΔH₁ = ΔH₂ + ΔH₃ + ΔH₄.

Standard Enthalpy of Reaction from Formation Enthalpies:

\[ \Delta_r H^\circ = \sum \nu_i \Delta_f H^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \nu_j \Delta_f H^\circ(\text{reactants}) \]

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. Bond Enthalpy Method: ΔrH° = Σ(Bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(Bond enthalpies of bonds formed).
2. Born-Haber Cycle: Apply Hess's Law to calculate lattice enthalpy from ionization, electron affinity, sublimation, and dissociation data.
3. Exam strategy: Write the target equation, then manipulate given equations (reverse = flip sign, multiply = scale) to cancel intermediates.

2.1 Spontaneity — What Makes a Reaction Happen? ⚡ CBSE Expected — Very High Yield

A spontaneous process occurs without external intervention once started. Not the same as "fast" — spontaneity is about thermodynamic feasibility, not kinetics.

Real-world examples:

  • ✅ Ice melting at 25°C — spontaneous (endothermic!)
  • ✅ Sugar dissolving in water — spontaneous
  • ✅ Rusting of iron — spontaneous but slow (months)
  • ❌ Water freezing at 25°C — non-spontaneous

Why ΔH alone is not a criterion: Ice melting is endothermic (ΔH > 0) but spontaneous above 0°C. Both enthalpy and entropy must be considered.

2.2 Entropy (S) — The Measure of Disorder ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

Entropy is a state function that quantifies the degree of randomness/disorder in a system.

Entropy Change (Reversible Process)

\[ \Delta S = \frac{q_{\text{rev}}}{T} \]

ΔSEntropy change (J K⁻¹)
qrevHeat exchanged in a reversible process (J)
TAbsolute temperature (K)
📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Conceptual Foundation

For a reversible isothermal process: ΔS = qrev/T.

Why reversible? Entropy is a state function, so ΔS is path-independent. We evaluate ΔS by imagining a reversible path between the same initial and final states, because only along a reversible path is the system in equilibrium at every stage.

Phase change example: For melting ice at 273 K, qrev = ΔfusH = 6.00 kJ mol⁻¹, so ΔS = 6000 / 273 = +21.97 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Statistical interpretation (Boltzmann): \[ S = k \ln W \]

where k = R/NA = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (Boltzmann constant), and W = number of microstates.

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. Entropy increases with: increase in T, increase in V (gas expansion), increase in n (more moles), phase transition solid → liquid → gas.
2. For an ideal gas undergoing isothermal expansion: ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = −nR ln(P₂/P₁).
3. Exam prediction: Predict sign of ΔS for a reaction — more gas moles on product side → ΔS positive.

2.3 The Second Law of Thermodynamics ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

"The entropy of the universe always increases for a spontaneous process."

\[ \Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0 \]

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Understanding ΔSsurr

The surroundings are so large that heat exchange does not change their temperature. For the surroundings, any heat transfer is effectively reversible:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{surr}} = -\frac{q_{\text{sys}}}{T} \]

At constant pressure: qsys = ΔH (for the system), so:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{surr}} = -\frac{\Delta H}{T} \]

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. For an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0), ΔSsurr > 0 — the surroundings gain disorder as they absorb heat.
2. For an endothermic reaction (ΔH > 0), ΔSsurr < 0 — the surroundings lose entropy.
3. The Second Law implies that energy spontaneously disperses from localized to spread-out forms.

2.4 The Third Law of Thermodynamics

"The entropy of a perfectly crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero (0 K)."

This allows the calculation of absolute entropies (S°), not just entropy changes. Standard molar entropies are tabulated at 298 K.

Implication: All substances have positive entropy values at any T > 0 K. Perfect order exists only at 0 K.

2.5 Gibbs Free Energy (G) ⚡ CBSE Expected — Very High Yield

Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy into a single criterion for spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure.

Gibbs Free Energy

\[ G = H - TS \]

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S \]

ΔGGibbs free energy change (kJ mol⁻¹)
ΔHEnthalpy change (kJ mol⁻¹)
TAbsolute temperature (K)
ΔSEntropy change (kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)

Spontaneity Criterion: ΔG < 0 → Spontaneous; ΔG = 0 → Equilibrium; ΔG > 0 → Non-spontaneous.

📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

Starting point: ΔStotal = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr > 0 (for spontaneity)

We know: ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys / T (at constant P)

Substitute: ΔStotal = ΔSsys − ΔHsys / T > 0

Multiply by T: TΔSsys − ΔHsys > 0

Rearrange: −(ΔHsys − TΔSsys) > 0

Define ΔG: ΔGsys = ΔHsys − TΔSsys

Therefore: ΔStotal > 0 ⇔ −ΔG/T > 0 ⇔ ΔG < 0

Physical meaning: ΔG is the maximum useful (non-PV) work obtainable from a system: ΔG = wnon-expansion.

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. Reversible vs Irreversible: In a reversible process, the system does maximum useful work equal to −ΔG. In an irreversible process, less useful work is done.
2. The Gibbs energy change gives the driving force of a reaction.
3. 📌 [See Solved Q 6.17 — temperature for spontaneity]

2.6 Gibbs Energy & Chemical Equilibrium ⚡ CBSE Expected — High Yield

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K \]

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT \log K \]

ΔG°Standard Gibbs energy change (kJ mol⁻¹)
RGas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
TTemperature (K)
KEquilibrium constant (dimensionless)
📐 Derivation & HOTS Depth

Derivation: ΔG° = −RT ln K

Van't Hoff isotherm relates ΔG to reaction quotient Q: \[ \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q \]

At equilibrium, ΔG = 0 and Q = K: \[ 0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln K \]

Therefore: \[ \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K \]

🔥 HOTS Strategies:
1. If K > 1, ΔG° < 0 (products favored at equilibrium).
2. If K = 1, ΔG° = 0 (equal product/reactant concentrations).
3. If K < 1, ΔG° > 0 (reactants favored).
4. Temperature dependence: Using ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°, we get: \[ \ln K = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R} \] — the van't Hoff equation for K vs. T.

Multiple Choice Questions (Q 6.1 – 6.6)

Q 6.1 — Which of the following is a thermodynamic state function?

Options: (a) q, (b) w, (c) ΔH, (d) Path

Answer: (c) ΔH 🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Explanation: State functions depend only on initial and final states, not on the path. ΔH (enthalpy change) is a state function. q (heat) and w (work) are path functions.

Q 6.2 — For an adiabatic process, which is true?

Options: (a) ΔT = 0, (b) q = 0, (c) w = 0, (d) ΔU = 0

Answer: (b) q = 0

Explanation: An adiabatic process is defined as one in which no heat is exchanged between system and surroundings. Hence q = 0. This means ΔU = w.

Q 6.3 — The enthalpies of all elements in their standard states are:

Options: (a) zero, (b) negative, (c) positive, (d) different for each

Answer: (a) zero

Explanation: By convention, the standard enthalpy of formation (ΔfH°) of an element in its most stable allotropic form is taken as zero at 1 bar pressure.

Q 6.4 — ΔU° of combustion of CH₄(g) is −X kJ mol⁻¹. ΔH° is:

Answer: ΔH° = ΔU° + ΔngRT 🔥 Frequent Exam Question

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l)

Δng = 1 − (1 + 2) = −2

ΔH° = ΔU° + (−2)RT = −X − 2RT

Since R > 0 and T > 0: ΔH° = −(X + 2RT). So |ΔH°| > |ΔU°|, or ΔH° is more negative than ΔU°.

Correct option: (b) ΔH° is less than ΔU° (more negative).

Q 6.5 — Enthalpy of formation of CH₄(g) from given data

Given: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); ΔcH° = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹

H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l); ΔcH° = −285.8 kJ mol⁻¹

CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l); ΔcH° = −890.3 kJ mol⁻¹

Target: C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g); ΔfH° = ?

Using Hess's Law:

ΔfH°(CH₄) = ΔcH°(C) + 2·ΔcH°(H₂) − ΔcH°(CH₄)

= (−393.5) + 2(−285.8) − (−890.3)

= −393.5 − 571.6 + 890.3 = −74.8 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.6 — A reaction has ΔH = −ve and ΔS = +ve. The reaction is:

Answer: Spontaneous at all temperatures.

Explanation: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. If ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0, then ΔG is always negative regardless of T. This corresponds to the first case in the spontaneity matrix.

Numerical Problems (Q 6.7 – 6.15)

Q 6.7 — A system absorbs 701 J of heat and does 394 J of work. What is ΔU?

Given: q = +701 J (heat absorbed = positive), w = −394 J (work done by system = negative)

First Law: ΔU = q + w = 701 + (−394) = +307 J

The internal energy of the system increases by 307 J.

Q 6.8 — Bomb calorimeter: combustion of 0.75 g benzene gives ΔT = 3.45°C, Ccal = 891 J K⁻¹. Find ΔU per mole.

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Step 1: qV = Ccal × ΔT = 891 × 3.45 = 3074 J = 3.074 kJ

Step 2: ΔU = −qV = −3.074 kJ (heat released by reaction)

Step 3: Moles of C₆H₆ = 0.75 / 78.11 = 0.00960 mol

Step 4: ΔU per mol = −3.074 / 0.00960 = −320.2 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.9 — Heat required to raise 60 g Al from 35°C to 55°C? (cAl = 0.9 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)

q = m × c × ΔT = 60 × 0.9 × (55 − 35) = 60 × 0.9 × 20 = 1080 J

Q 6.10 — Enthalpy change for freezing 1 mol water at 10°C → −10°C

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Data: cwater = 75.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, cice = 36.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔfusH = 6.00 kJ mol⁻¹

The process has three steps:

Step 1: Cool water from 10°C → 0°C: ΔH₁ = n·cwater·ΔT = 1 × 75.3 × (−10) = −753 J = −0.753 kJ

Step 2: Freeze water at 0°C: ΔH₂ = −ΔfusH = −6.00 kJ (reverse of fusion)

Step 3: Cool ice from 0°C → −10°C: ΔH₃ = 1 × 36.8 × (−10) = −368 J = −0.368 kJ

Total: ΔH = −0.753 + (−6.00) + (−0.368) = −7.12 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.11 — Heat released when 35.2 g CO₂ forms from C + O₂? (ΔfH° = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹)

Moles of CO₂: 35.2 / 44.01 = 0.800 mol

Heat released: 0.800 × 393.5 = −314.8 kJ

Q 6.12 — ΔrH for N₂O₄ + 3CO → N₂O + 3CO₂ from ΔfH° data

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Given: ΔfH°(N₂O₄) = +9.16 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔfH°(CO) = −110.5, ΔfH°(N₂O) = +81.0, ΔfH°(CO₂) = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹

ΔrH° = Σν·ΔfH°(products) − Σν·ΔfH°(reactants)

= [ΔfH°(N₂O) + 3·ΔfH°(CO₂)] − [ΔfH°(N₂O₄) + 3·ΔfH°(CO)]

= [81.0 + 3(−393.5)] − [9.16 + 3(−110.5)]

= [81.0 − 1180.5] − [9.16 − 331.5]

= (−1099.5) − (−322.34) = −777.16 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.13 — Standard enthalpy of formation of NH₃(g) from given ΔrH

Given: ½N₂(g) + ³⁄₂H₂(g) → NH₃(g); ΔrH° = −46.1 kJ mol⁻¹

This is already the formation reaction for 1 mol of NH₃.

Therefore: ΔfH°(NH₃) = −46.1 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.14 — Standard enthalpy of formation of CH₃OH(l) using Hess's Law

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Target: C(graphite) + 2H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → CH₃OH(l)

Given:
(1) C + O₂ → CO₂; ΔcH° = −393.5 kJ mol⁻¹
(2) H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O(l); ΔcH° = −285.8 kJ mol⁻¹
(3) CH₃OH + ³⁄₂O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O; ΔcH° = −726.0 kJ mol⁻¹

Manipulate:
(1) + 2×(2) − (3):
C + O₂ + 2H₂ + O₂ − CH₃OH − ³⁄₂O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O − CO₂ − 2H₂O

Simplify: C + 2H₂ + ½O₂ − CH₃OH → 0

So: C + 2H₂ + ½O₂ → CH₃OH

ΔfH°(CH₃OH) = (−393.5) + 2(−285.8) − (−726.0)

= −393.5 − 571.6 + 726.0 = −239.1 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.15 — Enthalpy change and C–Cl bond enthalpy in CCl₄

Given: CCl₄(g) → C(g) + 4Cl(g); ΔaH° = +1304 kJ mol⁻¹

Mean C–Cl bond enthalpy: 1304 / 4 = +326 kJ mol⁻¹

Note: This is the average bond enthalpy. The actual bond dissociation energies of the four C–Cl bonds in CCl₄ differ slightly due to step-wise effects.

Conceptual & Advanced Problems (Q 6.16 – 6.22)

Q 6.16 — For an isolated system, ΔU = 0. What about ΔS?

Answer: ΔS > 0

Explanation: An isolated system has no exchange with surroundings. The Second Law states that for any spontaneous process in an isolated system, ΔS > 0. At equilibrium, ΔS = 0. Since ΔU = 0 does not specify equilibrium, any spontaneous internal change (diffusion, mixing, reaction) will increase entropy.

Q 6.17 — Find T where reaction becomes spontaneous: ΔH = +400 kJ, ΔS = +0.2 kJ K⁻¹

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Spontaneity condition: ΔG < 0 ⇒ ΔH − TΔS < 0

ΔH < TΔS ⇒ T > ΔH/ΔS

T > 400 / 0.2 = 2000 K

The reaction becomes spontaneous above 2000 K (since ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0, case 2).

Q 6.18 — Predict signs of ΔH and ΔS for 2Cl(g) → Cl₂(g)

ΔH: Negative (bond formation releases energy, exothermic)

ΔS: Negative (2 moles of gas atoms → 1 mole of gas molecules; disorder decreases)

Spontaneity: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = (−) − T(−) = (−) + T(+). At low T, ΔG < 0 (spontaneous). At high T, ΔG may become > 0.

Q 6.19 — ΔG° for 2A + B → 2D: ΔU = −10.5 kJ, ΔS = −44.1 J K⁻¹, T = 298 K

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Step 1: Find ΔH from ΔU. Need Δng — not applicable here as states not given. For condensed phases, ΔH ≈ ΔU.

Assuming condensed phases: ΔH ≈ ΔU = −10.5 kJ

Step 2: Convert ΔS to kJ K⁻¹: ΔS = −44.1 J K⁻¹ = −0.0441 kJ K⁻¹

Step 3: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = −10.5 − 298(−0.0441)

= −10.5 + 13.14 = +2.64 kJ mol⁻¹

Since ΔG > 0, the reaction is non-spontaneous at 298 K.

Q 6.20 — ΔG° for reaction with K = 10 at 300 K

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

ΔG° = −2.303 RT log K

= −2.303 × 8.314 × 300 × log(10)

= −2.303 × 8.314 × 300 × 1

= −5744 J mol⁻¹ = −5.74 kJ mol⁻¹

Q 6.21 — Discuss thermodynamic stability of NO(g) from ΔfH° values

Given: ½N₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO(g); ΔfH° = +90 kJ mol⁻¹

NO(g) has positive enthalpy of formation, meaning it requires energy to form NO from its elements. This makes NO thermodynamically less stable than N₂ and O₂.

The decomposition 2NO → N₂ + O₂ would be exothermic (ΔH = −180 kJ). However, it's kinetically slow due to a high activation barrier (which is why NO exists in the atmosphere despite being thermodynamically unstable).

Q 6.22 — Entropy change in surroundings for formation of 1 mol H₂O(l)

🔥 Frequent Exam Question

Given: H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l); ΔfH° = −285.8 kJ mol⁻¹, T = 298 K

ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys / T = −(−285.8 × 10³) / 298

= 285800 / 298 = +959 J K⁻¹

The positive value indicates the surroundings gain entropy as they absorb heat released by the exothermic reaction.

Problem 6.3 — Isothermal reversible expansion work

Problem: 1 mol of ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly from 2.0 L to 10.0 L at 300 K. Calculate w.

Solution:

w = −2.303 nRT log(V₂/V₁) = −2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 300 × log(10/2)

= −2.303 × 8.314 × 300 × 0.699

= −4014 J

The negative sign indicates work done by the system.

Problem 6.4 — Isothermal irreversible expansion

Problem: Same as 6.3, but against constant external pressure of 1 atm.

Solution:

w = −PextΔV = −1 atm × (10.0 − 2.0) L = −8 L·atm

Convert to Joules: 1 L·atm = 101.3 J

w = −8 × 101.3 = −810.4 J

Note: Irreversible work (−810 J) is much less than reversible work (−4014 J) for the same volume change.

Problem 6.6 — Bomb calorimeter calculation for graphite

Problem: 0.5 g graphite burnt in bomb calorimeter, ΔT = 0.85 K, Ccal = 20.7 kJ K⁻¹. Find ΔU for combustion per mole.

Solution:

qV = CcalΔT = 20.7 × 0.85 = 17.595 kJ

ΔU = −qV = −17.595 kJ

Moles of C = 0.5/12 = 0.04167 mol

ΔU per mol = −17.595 / 0.04167 = −422 kJ mol⁻¹
