Chronology of Organic Chemistry

From mysticism to molecular science — the evolution of organic chemistry.

1780 – 1828
Berzelius & Vital Force Theory
Jöns Jacob Berzelius proposed that organic compounds required a “vital force” (vis vitalis) found only in living organisms. This implied organic molecules could not be synthesized in the laboratory from inorganic precursors.
HOTS: Why was Vital Force Theory accepted for 50 years?
  • Pre-1828, all known organic substances (sugar, urea, oils) were indeed isolated from living sources.
  • Inorganic-organic boundary seemed absolute — no one had successfully crossed it.
  • Berzelius's authority as a leading chemist made the theory widely accepted.
  • Lack of experimental tools (thermometers, precise balances) hindered attempts at synthesis.
1828
Friedrich Wöhler — Synthesis of Urea
The turning point. Wöhler heated ammonium cyanate ($\ce{NH4CNO}$, an inorganic salt) and obtained urea ($\ce{(NH2)2CO}$, an organic compound found in urine). This was identical to natural urea.
HOTS: Why is Wöhler's synthesis considered chemistry's most important experiment?
  • It disproved Vital Force Theory in a single, reproducible experiment.
  • Demonstrated that organic compounds obey the same physical/chemical laws as inorganic ones.
  • Opened the floodgates for synthetic organic chemistry — today we synthesize drugs, polymers, dyes.
  • It was an accidental discovery (Wöhler was trying to make ammonium cyanate crystals), highlighting the role of serendipity in science.
  • ⚡ PYQ: Frequently asked: “Explain why Wöhler's synthesis is a landmark event.”
1845
Hermann Kolbe — Synthesis of Acetic Acid
Kolbe synthesized acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) from carbon disulfide ($\ce{CS2}$) via chlorine and catalytic oxidation. This was the first multi-step total synthesis of a complex organic compound.
HOTS: Why was Kolbe's synthesis significant beyond Wöhler's?
  • Wöhler's urea synthesis was a single rearrangement; Kolbe's was a multi-step total synthesis.
  • Demonstrated that even complex organic molecules could be built from elemental carbon sources.
  • Laid the foundation for industrial organic synthesis — acetic acid is now produced at ~15 million tons/year.
1856
Berthelot — Synthesis of Methane
Marcellin Berthelot synthesized methane ($\ce{CH4}$) by passing a mixture of $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{CS2}$ over red-hot copper. He later synthesized acetylene ($\ce{C2H2}$), benzene ($\ce{C6H6}$), and many hydrocarbons, coining the term “synthesis” in chemistry.
HOTS: Berthelot's “synthèse” vs “analyse”
  • Berthelot systematically published Synthèse series (1856–1890s), proving organic compounds follow predictable synthetic pathways.
  • He showed that heat + catalysts could drive inorganic->organic transformations.
  • His work enabled the coal-tar dye industry — the first major chemical industry.
1860s – Present
Modern Structural Theory
Kekulé (1858) proposed tetravalent carbon; Couper and Butlerov independently developed structural theory. In 1865, Kekulé proposed the cyclic structure of benzene. Today, organic chemistry is governed by quantum mechanics, stereoelectronics, and computational prediction.
HOTS: How does structural theory explain isomerism?
  • Different connectivity of the same atoms → different compounds (structural isomers).
  • Same connectivity, different 3D arrangement → stereoisomers.
  • Kekulé's benzene structure initially failed to explain all isomers → led to resonance theory (Pauling, 1930s).

Naming Steps (IUPAC Algorithm)

  1. Find the longest carbon chain containing the principal functional group — this is the parent chain.
  2. Number the chain so that the principal functional group gets the lowest possible locant.
  3. Identify and number substituents (alkyl groups, halogens, etc.) with their locants.
  4. Combine in order: Locants + prefixes (alphabetical) + parent chain name + suffix.
  5. Use multiplicative prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-) for identical substituents. Ignore them when alphabetizing.

Examples with KaTeX

  • $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH2-OH}$ → Butan-1-ol (not butan-4-ol — lower locant wins)
  • $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-COOH}$ → 3-Methylbutanoic acid
  • $\ce{CH3-CH(OH)-CH2-CHO}$ → 3-Hydroxybutanal (aldehyde gets suffix priority over alcohol)
  • $\ce{CH#C-CH2-CH2-CH=CH2}$ → Hex-1-en-5-yne (lower locant for ene-yne tie goes to ene)
  • $\ce{Br-CH2-CH=CH-CH2-Cl}$ → 1-Bromo-4-chlorobut-2-ene
HOTS: Why does the suffix suffix take priority over unsaturation in naming?
  • The principal functional group defines the chemical identity of the compound (acid, alcohol, etc.).
  • Unsaturation ($\ce{C=C}$, $\ce{C#C}$) affects reactivity but does not define the compound's class.
  • NCERT rule: Infixes (-ane, -ene, -yne) come before the suffix in the name.

Electronic Effects in Organic Molecules

Understanding how electrons distribute within molecules explains reactivity, acidity/basicity, and reaction mechanisms.

1. Inductive Effect ($I$)

The polarization of $\sigma$ bonds due to electronegativity differences. It is a permanent, distance-dependent effect that decreases along a carbon chain.

TypeDirectionGroupsExample
$-I$ (electron-withdrawing)Pulls $e^-$ away from the chain$-\ce{NO2}, -\ce{CN}, -\ce{COOH}, -\ce{CHO}, -\ce{OH}, -\ce{NH2}, -\ce{X}$$\ce{Cl-CH2-COOH}$ is stronger acid than $\ce{CH3COOH}$
$+I$ (electron-donating)Pushes $e^-$ toward the chain$-\ce{CH3}, -\ce{C2H5}, -\ce{CH(CH3)2}, -\ce{C(CH3)3}$, $\ce{O-}$$\ce{CH3-CH2-COOH}$ is weaker acid than $\ce{HCOOH}$

Order of $-I$: $-\ce{NO2} > -\ce{CN} > -\ce{COOH} > -\ce{CHO} > -\ce{OH} > -\ce{NH2} > -\ce{F} > -\ce{Cl} > -\ce{Br} > -\ce{I}$

HOTS: Why is $\ce{Cl-CH2-COOH}$ ($pK_a$ 2.86) a stronger acid than $\ce{CH3COOH}$ ($pK_a$ 4.76)?
  • Cl is electronegative ($-I$) → pulls electron density away from the $\ce{O-H}$ bond.
  • This weakens the $\ce{O-H}$ bond and stabilizes the conjugate base ($\ce{Cl-CH2-COO-}$) by delocalizing the negative charge.
  • The effect diminishes with distance: $\ce{Cl-CH2-COOH}$ is stronger than $\ce{CH3-CHCl-COOH}$.
  • ⚡ PYQ: “Arrange acetic, chloroacetic, trichloroacetic acids in increasing acidity” is a classic 2-mark question.
PYQ: Inductive effect & acidity trends

2. Resonance Effect ($R$ or $M$)

Delocalization of $\pi$ electrons or lone pairs through conjugation. Also called mesomeric effect ($M$). It is a permanent effect transmitted through $\pi$ bonds.

TypeDirectionGroupsEffect
$+R$ ($+M$)Donates $e^-$ into the conjugated system$-\ce{OH}, -\ce{NH2}, -\ce{OR}, -\ce{Cl}, -\ce{Br}, -\ce{O-}$Activates benzene ring toward electrophilic substitution
$-R$ ($-M$)Withdraws $e^-$ from the conjugated system$-\ce{NO2}, -\ce{CN}, -\ce{COOH}, -\ce{CHO}, -\ce{COOR}$Deactivates benzene ring; directs meta
HOTS: Why is $-\ce{NH2}$ strongly activating for benzene even though N is electronegative?
  • N has a lone pair that can be donated into the $\pi$ system of the ring ($+R$ effect).
  • This $+R$ effect dominates over the $-I$ effect of N.
  • The resulting resonance structures place negative charge at ortho and para positions → those positions are activated.
  • ⚡ PYQ: “Why is aniline ortho/para-directing but deactivating?” trick question — it's activating!

3. Electromeric Effect ($E$)

A temporary effect seen in the presence of an attacking reagent. Complete transfer of a $\pi$ electron pair to one of the atoms.

  • $+E$ effect: $\pi$ electrons shift toward the attacking reagent (e.g., $\ce{C=C}$ attacked by $\ce{H+}$).
  • $-E$ effect: $\pi$ electrons shift away from the attacking reagent (e.g., $\ce{C=O}$ attacked by a nucleophile).
  • The effect vanishes as soon as the attacking species is removed.

4. Hyperconjugation (Baker-Nathan Effect)

Delocalization via $\sigma \to \pi^*$ overlap — interaction of $\ce{C-H}$ $\sigma$ bonding electrons with an adjacent empty or partially filled $p$ orbital or $\pi^*$ orbital.

  • Number of $\alpha$-H atoms directly correlates with stability of carbocations and alkenes.
  • Stability order: $(\ce{CH3})_3\ce{C+} > (\ce{CH3})_2\ce{CH+} > \ce{CH3CH2+} > \ce{CH3+}$ (tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl).
  • Hyperconjugation also explains why propene ($\ce{CH3-CH=CH2}$) is more stable than ethene.
HOTS: Why does increasing $\alpha$-H count increase carbocation stability?
  • Each $\ce{C-H}$ bond at the $\alpha$-carbon can overlap with the empty $p$ orbital of the carbocation.
  • This overlap delocalizes the positive charge over more atoms, reducing charge density.
  • More $\alpha$-H atoms = more hyperconjugative structures = greater stabilization.
  • ⚡ PYQ: “Explain the order of stability of carbocations” is a staple 3-mark question.

Bond Cleavage & Reaction Intermediates

Homolytic Fission

Each atom gets one electron from the covalent bond. Produces free radicals (species with unpaired electrons). Requires UV light or high temperature. Denoted by half-headed arrows ($\curvearrowright$).

$\ce{Cl-Cl} \xrightarrow{h\nu} \ce{Cl.} + \ce{.Cl}$

Heterolytic Fission

Both electrons go to the more electronegative atom. Produces carbocations ($\ce{R3C+}$) and carbanions ($\ce{R3C-}$). Denoted by full-headed arrows ($\rightarrow$). Favored in polar solvents.

$\ce{(CH3)3C-Br} \xrightarrow{H2O} \ce{(CH3)3C+} + \ce{Br-}$

Stability of Intermediates

IntermediateStability OrderReason
Carbocation$3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \ce{CH3+}$Hyperconjugation + inductive effect of alkyl groups
Carbanion$1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ$Alkyl groups ($+I$) destabilize the negative charge
Free Radical$3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \ce{CH3.}$Hyperconjugation stabilizes the unpaired electron
Alkene stabilitytetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstitutedHyperconjugation of $\alpha$-C-H bonds with $\pi^*$
HOTS: Why does carbocation stability follow a different trend than carbanion stability?
  • Carbocations are electron-deficient → electron-donating alkyl groups ($+I$, hyperconjugation) stabilize them.
  • Carbanions are electron-rich → electron-donating alkyl groups intensify the negative charge, destabilizing them.
  • More substituted carbanions are less stable because charge repulsion dominates.

7Explain why $\ce{CCl4}$ does not give precipitate with $\ce{AgNO3}$ but $\ce{CH3CH2Cl}$ does.PYQ

Answer: In $\ce{CCl4}$, all four Cl atoms are bonded to the same C atom. There is no $\ce{H}$ on the C bearing Cl, so $\ce{SN1}$/$\ce{SN2}$ displacement is extremely difficult. $\ce{CCl4}$ is inert toward nucleophiles.

In $\ce{CH3CH2Cl}$, the $\ce{C-Cl}$ bond can undergo nucleophilic substitution (especially in presence of $\ce{AgNO3}$ in ethanol), liberating $\ce{Cl-}$ which gives $\ce{AgCl}$ (white precipitate).

$$\ce{CH3CH2Cl + AgNO3 -> CH3CH2ONO2 + AgCl \downarrow}$$

10Explain the terms: (a) Electrophile (b) Nucleophile (c) Homolytic fission (d) Heterolytic fission

Answer:

  • Electrophile (electron-loving): A species that accepts an electron pair (e.g., $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Br+}$, $\ce{NO2+}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$). They are electron-deficient.
  • Nucleophile (nucleus-loving): A species that donates an electron pair (e.g., $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$). They are electron-rich.
  • Homolytic fission: Symmetrical bond cleavage — each atom gets one electron. Produces free radicals.
  • Heterolytic fission: Unsymmetrical bond cleavage — the more electronegative atom gets both electrons. Produces ions.
11Draw the resonance structures of $\ce{CH3COO-}$ (acetate ion).HOTS

Answer: Acetate ion ($\ce{CH3COO-}$) has two equivalent resonance structures:

$\ce{CH3-C(=O)-O-}$   ↔   $\ce{CH3-C(-O-)=O}$

Both structures contribute equally. The negative charge is delocalized over both O atoms, making the acetate ion strongly stabilized (resonance energy ~80 kJ/mol). This explains why acetic acid is a weak acid ($pK_a$ 4.76) — the conjugate base is stabilized by resonance.

E1How do you account for the formation of ethane during chlorination of methane?PYQ

Answer: Chlorination of methane proceeds via free radical mechanism:

Initiation: $$\ce{Cl2 ->[h\nu] 2Cl.}$$

Propagation: $$\ce{Cl. + CH4 -> .CH3 + HCl}$$ $$\ce{.CH3 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl.}$$

Termination: $$\ce{.CH3 + .CH3 -> CH3-CH3 (ethane)}$$

When two methyl radicals combine, ethane is formed as a byproduct. The yield of ethane is low because the concentration of $\ce{.CH3}$ radicals is very small.

Why? $\ce{Cl3C-COOH}$ has three strongly $-I$ Cl atoms, hugely stabilizing the conjugate base. $\ce{HCOOH}$ has no +I alkyl group unlike $\ce{CH3COOH}$, making HCOOH stronger.

E4Explain the principle of paper chromatography.

Answer: Paper chromatography is based on the differential partitioning of compounds between a stationary phase (water adsorbed on cellulose fibers of paper) and a mobile phase (solvent).

Components move at different rates ($R_f$ values) based on their solubility in the mobile phase and affinity for the stationary phase. More polar compounds move slower; less polar compounds move faster.

The $R_f$ (Retardation factor) is: $$R_f = \frac{\text{distance traveled by component}}{\text{distance traveled by solvent front}}$$

E60.25 g of an organic compound containing nitrogen was digested by Kjeldahl's method. The ammonia evolved was absorbed in 50 mL of 0.1 M $\ce{H2SO4}$. The excess acid required 30 mL of 0.1 M $\ce{NaOH}$. Find the % of N.PYQHOTS

Answer:

Step 1: Calculate acid consumed:

Initial $\ce{H2SO4} = 50\ \text{mL} \times 0.1\ \text{M} = 5\ \text{mmol}$

$\ce{NaOH}$ used for back-titration = $30\ \text{mL} \times 0.1\ \text{M} = 3\ \text{mmol}$

Since $\ce{2NaOH + H2SO4 -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}$, 3 mmol $\ce{NaOH}$ reacts with 1.5 mmol $\ce{H2SO4}$

$\ce{H2SO4}$ consumed by $\ce{NH3} = 5 - 1.5 = 3.5\ \text{mmol}$

$\ce{2NH3 + H2SO4 -> (NH4)2SO4}$, so $\ce{NH3} = 2 \times 3.5 = 7\ \text{mmol} = 0.007\ \text{mol}$

Mass of N = $0.007 \times 14 = 0.098\ \text{g}$

$$\%\ce{N} = \frac{0.098}{0.25} \times 100 = 39.2\%$$

Alternatively, using formula: $$\%\ce{N} = \frac{1.4 \times M \times (V_2 - V_1)}{W}$$ where $M$ is molarity, $V_2$ = vol of acid (mL), $V_1$ = vol of NaOH (mL) for same $M$, $W$ = sample mass.

E7Explain why $\ce{CCl4}$ is not a conductor of electricity but $\ce{NaCl}$ solution is.

Answer: $\ce{NaCl}$ in water dissociates into $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions (electrolyte), which carry electric current.

$\ce{CCl4}$ is a covalent compound. It does not dissociate into ions in any solvent. Hence it cannot conduct electricity.

E9Why does Lassaigne's test fail for diazonium salts?HOTS

Answer: In Lassaigne's test, the organic compound is fused with Na metal to convert N into $\ce{NaCN}$. However, diazonium salts ($\ce{C6H5N2+X-}$) decompose on heating with Na, and the $\ce{N2}$ gas escapes before forming $\ce{NaCN}$. E11Explain the Dumas method for estimation of nitrogen. Why is it more universal than Kjeldahl?PYQHOTS

Answer: Dumas Method: A known mass of the organic compound is heated with $\ce{CuO}$ in a combustion furnace:

$$\ce{C_xH_yN_z + (2x + y/2)CuO -> xCO2 + (y/2)H2O + (z/2)N2 + (2x + y/2)Cu}$$

The $\ce{N2}$ gas is collected over a solution of KOH in a Schiff's nitrometer. Volume of $\ce{N2}$ at STP is measured.

$$\%\ce{N} = \frac{28 \times V \times 100}{22400 \times W}$$ where $V$ = volume of $\ce{N2}$ (mL at STP), $W$ = sample mass.

Why Dumas is more universal:

  • Kjeldahl works only for compounds where N is in $-3$ oxidation state (amines, amides).
  • Dumas works for all N-containing compounds including nitro, azo, heterocycles, etc.
  • Dumas does not require conversion of N to $\ce{NH3}$, avoiding issues with incomplete digestion.

E13Explain the mechanism of $\ce{SN1}$ and $\ce{SN2}$ reactions with examples.PYQHOTS

Answer:

$\ce{SN1}$ (Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular):

Two steps: (1) Slow: $\ce{R-X -> R+ + X-}$ (carbocation formation, rate-determining). (2) Fast: $\ce{R+ + Nu- -> R-Nu}$.

Example: $\ce{(CH3)3C-Br ->[H2O] (CH3)3C-OH}$ (tertiary halide).

Features: First-order kinetics, racemization, favored by tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents.

$\ce{SN2}$ (Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular):

Single step: $\ce{Nu- + R-X -> [Nu---R---X]^# -> R-Nu + X-}$ (backside attack, Walden inversion).

Example: $\ce{CH3-Br + OH- -> CH3-OH + Br-}$ (methyl halide).

Features: Second-order kinetics, inversion of configuration, favored by primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents.

E14What are the different methods of purification of organic compounds? Explain column chromatography.

Answer: Methods of purification:

  1. Sublimation — for volatile solids (e.g., naphthalene, camphor).
  2. Crystallization — based on solubility difference.
  3. Distillation — based on boiling point difference (simple, fractional, steam, vacuum, azeotropic).
  4. Differential extraction — based on partition between two immiscible solvents.
  5. Chromatography — based on differential adsorption/partition.

Column Chromatography: Stationary phase (alumina/silica gel) packed in a glass column. The mixture is loaded at the top, and the mobile phase (solvent) is passed through. Components travel at different speeds based on their adsorption affinity → collected separately.

E16What is hyperconjugation? Explain with reference to propene.PYQHOTS

Answer: Hyperconjugation is the delocalization of $\sigma$ electrons (from $\ce{C-H}$ bond) into an adjacent $\pi$ orbital or empty p orbital.

In propene ($\ce{CH3-CH=CH2}$), the three $\ce{C-H}$ bonds on the methyl group ($\alpha$ to the double bond) can overlap with the $\pi^*$ orbital of the $\ce{C=C}$, stabilizing the molecule:

$$\ce{H-CH2-\!\!\overset{\pi}{C}\!H\!\!=\!\!CH2}$$

The three $\alpha$-H atoms give 3 hyperconjugative structures, making propene more stable than ethene (which has no $\alpha$-H).

The stability of alkenes follows: tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstituted > ethene, directly correlating with the number of $\alpha$-H atoms.

E21Explain the +I and -I effects. Give suitable examples.PYQ

Answer: Inductive effect is the polarization of $\sigma$ bonds due to electronegativity differences.

$-I$ (electron withdrawing): Groups that pull electron density toward themselves.

Examples: $-\ce{NO2}$, $-\ce{CN}$, $-\ce{COOH}$, $-\ce{CHO}$, $-\ce{OH}$, $-\ce{NH2}$, halogens.

Examples: Alkyl groups ($-\ce{CH3}$, $-\ce{C2H5}$, $-\ce{CH(CH3)2}$, $-\ce{C(CH3)3}$), $\ce{O-}$.

$+I$ order: $-\ce{C(CH3)3} > -\ce{CH(CH3)2} > -\ce{C2H5} > -\ce{CH3} > \ce{H}$

E24Write short notes on: (a) Crystallization (b) Sublimation (c) Steam distillation

Answer:

(a) Crystallization: A purification technique based on the principle that a compound's solubility increases with temperature. The impure solid is dissolved in a minimum amount of hot solvent. On cooling, the pure compound crystallizes out, leaving impurities in solution. Used for purification of solids like benzoic acid, naphthalene.

(b) Sublimation: Some solids directly vaporize on heating without passing through the liquid phase. The vapors are then condensed back to solid on a cold surface. Used for compounds like camphor, naphthalene, $\ce{NH4Cl}$.

(c) Steam distillation: A mixture of steam and the organic compound is distilled. Used for compounds that are volatile with steam but immiscible with water. Examples: aniline, essential oils, eugenol from cloves.
