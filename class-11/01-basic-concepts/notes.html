1. Introduction: What is Chemistry?

Chemistry (from Egyptian kēme — "earth") is the scientific study of matter—its composition, structure, properties, and the changes it undergoes. It is often called the Central Science because it bridges physics (atomic structure, thermodynamics) with biology (biochemistry, molecular genetics) and Earth sciences.

Branches of Chemistry

  • Physical Chemistry: Laws of thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, kinetics, and the mole concept — the foundation of this chapter.
  • Organic Chemistry: Study of carbon-based compounds (hydrocarbons, polymers, drugs).
  • Inorganic Chemistry: Elements, coordination compounds, metallurgy, and periodic trends.
  • Analytical Chemistry: Qualitative and quantitative analysis — titration, spectroscopy, chromatography.
  • Biochemistry: Chemical processes within living organisms (enzymes, DNA, metabolism).

This first chapter establishes the quantitative language of chemistry — the mole, stoichiometry, and concentration — without which no branch of chemistry can operate.

2. Chronology of Chemistry

~3000 BCE – 300 BCE
Alchemy & Ancient Roots
Alchemists discovered practical processes (distillation, sublimation) while chasing transmutation. The four-element theory dominated early thought.
1770s – 1794
Lavoisier: Birth of Modern Chem
Lavoisier's quantitative experiments proved mass is neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions. He published Traite Elementaire de Chimie (1789) — the first modern chemistry textbook.
1808
Dalton's Atomic Theory
Dalton's theory explained the Law of Multiple Proportions and Definite Proportions. He also prepared the first table of atomic weights — the precursor to modern atomic mass.
1811
Avogadro's Hypothesis
Avogadro's insight that gas particles could be diatomic ($\text{H}_2$, $\text{O}_2$) reconciled Dalton's theory with Gay-Lussac's Law of combining volumes. His work was ignored for decades but became foundational.
1869
Mendeleev's Periodic Table
Mendeleev's table revealed periodicity in chemical properties. He famously left blanks for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties — a triumph of chemical classification.
20th Century – Present
Modern Chemistry
Chemistry today spans from quantum dots to drug design, from climate science to materials engineering — all resting on the quantitative foundation of the mole and stoichiometry.

3. Matter and Its Classification

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. It exists in three physical states (solid, liquid, gas) and is classified by composition:

  • Mixtures: Two or more substances physically mixed — can be homogeneous (solutions, alloys) or heterogeneous (suspensions, colloids). Separable by physical means.
  • Pure Substances: Constant composition — either elements (one type of atom) or compounds (fixed ratio of elements combined chemically, separable only by chemical reactions).

Significant Figures ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

Rules for determining significant figures in measured values:

  • All non-zero digits are significant (e.g., $1234 \rightarrow 4$ SF)
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant ($1002 \rightarrow 4$ SF)
  • Leading zeros are NOT significant ($0.0023 \rightarrow 2$ SF)
  • Trailing zeros in a number with a decimal are significant ($1.200 \rightarrow 4$ SF)
  • Trailing zeros in a number without a decimal are ambiguous ($1200 \rightarrow 2, 3,\text{ or }4$ SF depending on precision)
Precision vs. Accuracy: Precision measures reproducibility (closeness of repeated measurements). Accuracy measures closeness to the true value. A dartboard analogy: tight cluster but off-center = precise but not accurate; scattered around bullseye = accurate but not precise.

Scientific Notation: Expressing numbers as $N \times 10^n$ where $1 \leq N < 10$ and $n$ is an integer exponent. E.g., $0.000045 \rightarrow 4.5 \times 10^{-5}$ (2 SF), $602200000000000000000000 \rightarrow 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ (4 SF).

Rules for Arithmetic: Addition/Subtraction — result has the least number of decimal places. Multiplication/Division — result has the least number of significant figures. ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

5. Laws of Chemical Combination

These five empirical laws form the bedrock of stoichiometry. They describe how matter interacts chemically and led directly to Dalton's Atomic Theory.

5.1 Law of Conservation of Mass ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Antoine Lavoisier (1789): Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.

$$ \sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}} $$

Real-world example: In the combustion of $32.0\text{ g}$ of methane ($\text{CH}_4$) with $128.0\text{ g}$ of oxygen, the products — $88.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ and $72.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ — sum to $160.0\text{ g}$, exactly matching the reactants.

5.2 Law of Definite Proportions (Proust's Law) ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Joseph Proust (1799): A chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by mass, regardless of the source or method of preparation.

$$ \text{Mass ratio} = \frac{\text{mass of element A}}{\text{mass of element B}} = \text{constant} $$

Example — Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Whether from rain, river, or laboratory synthesis, the mass ratio is always $1 : 8$ (hydrogen : oxygen). In $18.0\text{ g}$ of water: $2.0\text{ g H}$ and $16.0\text{ g O}$.

5.3 Law of Multiple Proportions ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

John Dalton (1803): When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers.

Classic Example — Carbon & Oxygen:
CO (carbon monoxide): $12\text{ g C}$ combines with $16\text{ g O}$
CO₂ (carbon dioxide): $12\text{ g C}$ combines with $32\text{ g O}$
Mass of oxygen per fixed $12\text{ g C}$: $16 : 32 = 1 : 2$ (simple whole numbers) 📌 See Solved Q 1.7

5.4 Gay Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1808): When gases react, they do so in volumes that bear a simple whole-number ratio to one another and to the volumes of products (if gaseous), measured under same T & P.

Example — Synthesis of water:

$$ 2\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) $$ $$ \text{Volume ratio: } 2 : 1 : 2 $$

5.5 Avogadro's Law ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Amedeo Avogadro (1811): Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules.

$$ V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P) $$

Where $V$ = volume of gas, $n$ = number of moles of gas.

Significance: This law provides the bridge between the macroscopic world (gas volumes) and the microscopic world (number of molecules). At STP ($0^\circ$C, 1 atm), one mole of any gas occupies $22.4\text{ L}$.

6. Dalton's Atomic Theory

Dalton's postulates (1808) unified the laws of chemical combination:

  1. All matter consists of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties.
  3. Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.
  4. Compounds form by the combination of atoms of different elements in simple whole-number ratios.
  5. Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms; atoms are neither created nor destroyed.
Modern Limitations: Dalton's "indivisible" atom was disproved by subatomic particles (electrons, protons, neutrons). Isotopes show that atoms of the same element can have different masses. However, his framework of fixed mass ratios and conservation in reactions remains the foundation of stoichiometry.

7. Atomic, Average, and Molecular Masses

7.1 Atomic Mass Unit (u) ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

One atomic mass unit (u) is defined as exactly $1/12^{\text{th}}$ the mass of one carbon-12 atom. This provides a convenient scale for microscopic masses:

$$ 1 \text{ u} = \frac{1}{12} \times \text{mass of one } ^{12}\text{C atom} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-24} \text{ g} $$

7.2 Atomic Mass

The atomic mass of an element is the mass of one atom expressed in atomic mass units (u). For $^{12}\text{C}$, it is exactly $12\text{ u}$.

7.3 Average Atomic Mass ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

Most elements occur as a mixture of isotopes. The average atomic mass accounts for the natural abundance of each isotope:

$$ \text{Average atomic mass} = \frac{\sum (\text{isotopic mass} \times \text{fractional abundance})}{\sum \text{fractional abundance}} $$
Variables:
Isotopic mass — mass of a specific isotope (in u)
Fractional abundance — fraction of atoms of that isotope in nature
Units: atomic mass units (u) — dimensionless ratio, though often expressed in g mol$^{-1}$ for molar mass

Derivation — Weighted Average Logic

If an element has two isotopes with masses $m_1, m_2$ and abundances $p_1\%, p_2\%$:

$$ \text{Average mass} = \frac{m_1 \times p_1 + m_2 \times p_2}{p_1 + p_2} = \frac{m_1 p_1 + m_2 p_2}{100} $$

HOTS Strategies:

  1. Reverse Abundance: If average atomic mass is given, set up the equation with one unknown abundance (the other = $100 - x$). Solve for $x$.
  2. Three-Isotope Problems: Use two given abundances and the average mass to find the third abundance via a system of linear equations.
  3. Mass Spectrometry Data: In problems where peak intensities are given (rather than percentages), treat intensities as proportional to abundance.

Example — Chlorine: $^{35}\text{Cl}$ (mass = $34.9689\text{ u}$, 75.77%) and $^{37}\text{Cl}$ (mass = $36.9659\text{ u}$, 24.23%)

$$ \text{Avg} = \frac{(34.9689 \times 75.77) + (36.9659 \times 24.23)}{100} = 35.45\text{ u} $$

7.4 Molecular Mass ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

The molecular mass is the sum of atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule, expressed in u.

Example — Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):

$$ \text{Molecular mass of H}_2\text{O} = (2 \times 1.008) + (1 \times 16.00) = 18.016\text{ u} $$

7.5 Formula Mass

For ionic compounds (e.g., $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{CaCO}_3$) that exist as formula units rather than discrete molecules, we use formula mass — the sum of atomic masses in the empirical formula.

Example — Sodium chloride: $\text{NaCl} \rightarrow 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44\text{ u}$

Key Insight: The numerical value of atomic/molecular mass in u is exactly the same as the molar mass in g mol$^{-1}$. For example, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ has molecular mass $18.016\text{ u}$ and molar mass $18.016\text{ g mol}^{-1}$. This equivalence is the bridge from a single molecule to one mole. ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

8. The Mole Concept ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

The mole (symbol: mol) is the SI base unit for amount of substance. It is defined as the amount containing exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities — this number is Avogadro's constant ($N_A$).

$$ 1 \text{ mol} = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ particles} $$ $$ N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ mol}^{-1} $$

Why $6.022 \times 10^{23}$? This number is chosen so that the mass of one mole of a substance in grams equals its atomic/molecular mass in u. A single $^{12}\text{C}$ atom weighs $12\text{ u}$; one mole of $^{12}\text{C}$ atoms weighs exactly $12\text{ g}$.

Molar Mass ($M$): The mass of one mole of a substance. Units: $\text{g mol}^{-1}$.
$$ M = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{amount (mol)}} $$

Converting Between Mass, Moles, and Particles

$$ n = \frac{m}{M} \quad \text{or} \quad n = \frac{N}{N_A} $$
Variables:
$n$ = amount of substance (mol)
$m$ = mass (g)
$M$ = molar mass (g mol$^{-1}$)
$N$ = number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions, formula units)
$N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$ (Avogadro's constant)

Dimensional Analysis Framework

The mole is a conversion factor between the macroscopic scale (grams) and the microscopic scale (atoms/molecules):

$$ \text{Grams} \xrightarrow{\div M} \text{Moles} \xrightarrow{\times N_A} \text{Particles} $$ $$ \text{Particles} \xrightarrow{\div N_A} \text{Moles} \xrightarrow{\times M} \text{Grams} $$

HOTS Strategies:

  1. Multi-step conversion: A problem gives grams of compound and asks for number of atoms of a specific element. Convert: $\text{g} \rightarrow \text{mol compound} \rightarrow \text{mol element} \times \text{subscript} \rightarrow \text{atoms} \times N_A$.
  2. Hydrates: For compounds like $\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$, include the water mass in molar mass. The "moles of compound" gives 5$\times$ moles of water.
  3. Relative mass comparisons: Given equal masses of different elements, the one with the smallest molar mass has the most moles (and most atoms).

Worked Example: How many atoms are in $5.40\text{ g}$ of aluminium? ($M_{\text{Al}} = 27.0\text{ g mol}^{-1}$)

$$ n = \frac{5.40}{27.0} = 0.200 \text{ mol}$$ $$ N = 0.200 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.20 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms}$$

9. Percentage Composition ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

The mass percentage of each element in a compound provides a crucial link between experimental data and chemical formulas.

$$ \text{Mass \% of element} = \frac{\text{mass of element in 1 mol of compound}}{\text{molar mass of compound}} \times 100 $$

Example — Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$, $M = 46.07\text{ g mol}^{-1}$):

$$ \%\text{C} = \frac{2 \times 12.01}{46.07} \times 100 = 52.14\% $$ $$ \%\text{H} = \frac{6 \times 1.008}{46.07} \times 100 = 13.13\% $$ $$ \%\text{O} = \frac{1 \times 16.00}{46.07} \times 100 = 34.73\% $$
Using % as a conversion bridge: If a compound is $40.0\%$ carbon by mass, then $100\text{ g}$ contains $40.0\text{ g C}$. This is the starting point for empirical formula determination.

10. Empirical and Molecular Formulas ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound. The molecular formula is the actual number of atoms in one molecule.

$$ \text{Molecular formula} = (\text{Empirical formula})_n $$ $$ n = \frac{\text{Molecular mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}} $$

Algorithm — Empirical Formula from Mass % Data:

  1. Assume $100\text{ g}$ sample → mass of each element = its %
  2. Convert mass to moles: $n = m/M$
  3. Divide each mole value by the smallest mole value
  4. If necessary, multiply by a small integer to get whole numbers

HOTS Strategies:

  1. Decimal thresholds: If ratios are within 0.1 of an integer, round directly (e.g., 1.98 → 2). If close to 0.5 (e.g., 1.5), multiply all by 2. Similarly for 0.33 → multiply by 3, 0.25 → multiply by 4.
  2. Difference between empirical and molecular: Only molecular formula requires molecular mass data (often from mass spectrometry or osmotic pressure). Empirical formula alone cannot distinguish glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) from formaldehyde ($\text{CH}_2\text{O}$).
  3. Combustion analysis: Given masses of $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ produced, back-calculate grams of C and H, then O by difference.

Worked Example — A compound is $40.00\%$ C, $6.71\%$ H, $53.29\%$ O. The molecular mass is $180.16\text{ g mol}^{-1}$. Find empirical and molecular formulas.

Step 1: In $100\text{ g}$: $40.00\text{ g C}$, $6.71\text{ g H}$, $53.29\text{ g O}$.

Step 2: Moles: C = $40.00/12.01 = 3.331$; H = $6.71/1.008 = 6.656$; O = $53.29/16.00 = 3.331$

Step 3: Divide by smallest (3.331): C : H : O = $1 : 1.998 : 1 \approx 1 : 2 : 1$ → Empirical formula = $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$

Step 4: Empirical formula mass = $12.01 + 2.016 + 16.00 = 30.03\text{ g mol}^{-1}$

Step 5: $n = 180.16 / 30.03 = 6$ → Molecular formula = $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ (glucose)

11. Stoichiometry and Stoichiometric Calculations ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

Stoichiometry (from Greek stoicheion — element, metron — measure) is the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a chemical reaction. The balanced chemical equation provides molar ratios that serve as conversion factors.

$$ \text{For } aA + bB \rightarrow cC + dD: \quad \frac{n_A}{a} = \frac{n_B}{b} = \frac{n_C}{c} = \frac{n_D}{d} $$
Variables:
$a, b, c, d$ — stoichiometric coefficients (dimensionless)
$n_A, n_B, n_C, n_D$ — amount of each substance (mol)

Mass-Mass Calculations ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Real-world context — Industrial synthesis of water: How much oxygen is needed to completely react with $10.0\text{ g}$ of hydrogen in the Haber-Bosch water-gas shift context?

$$ 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} $$

Step 1: Moles of $\text{H}_2 = 10.0\text{ g} / 2.016\text{ g mol}^{-1} = 4.96\text{ mol}$

Step 2: From balanced eq: $2\text{ mol H}_2 : 1\text{ mol O}_2$, so $n_{\text{O}_2} = 4.96 / 2 = 2.48\text{ mol}$

Step 3: Mass of $\text{O}_2 = 2.48 \times 32.00 = 79.4\text{ g}$

Mass-Volume and Volume-Volume Calculations

At STP ($0^\circ$C, 1 atm): $1\text{ mol}$ of any gas occupies $22.4\text{ L}$. This is the molar volume.

$$ V_{\text{gas}} = n \times 22.4\text{ L mol}^{-1} \quad (\text{at STP}) $$

Example — Methane combustion: What volume of $\text{CO}_2$ (at STP) is produced from burning $16.0\text{ g}$ of methane?

$$ \text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} $$

$n_{\text{CH}_4} = 16.0 / 16.04 = 0.998\text{ mol} \rightarrow n_{\text{CO}_2} = 0.998\text{ mol}$

$$ V_{\text{CO}_2} = 0.998 \times 22.4 = 22.4\text{ L} $$

12. Limiting Reagent (Limiting Reactant) ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely consumed in a reaction, determining the maximum amount of product formed. The other reactants are in excess.

Algorithm to Identify Limiting Reagent:

  1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
  2. Convert all given masses to moles: $n = m/M$.
  3. Divide each mole value by its stoichiometric coefficient.
  4. The reactant with the smallest resulting value is the limiting reagent.
  5. Use the limiting reagent's moles to calculate theoretical yield of product.
  6. Subtract consumed amount from excess reactant's initial amount to find excess remaining.

HOTS Strategies:

  1. Multiple products: When a reaction produces multiple products, the limiting reagent determines the yield of ALL products simultaneously.
  2. Sequential reactions: In multi-step processes, the overall limiting reagent is determined by the first reaction. Any excess from step 1 becomes the starting material for step 2.
  3. Percentage yield: $\% \text{ yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$. The theoretical yield is always based on the limiting reagent. ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

Worked Example — Haber process: If $28.0\text{ g N}_2$ reacts with $10.0\text{ g H}_2$:

$$ \text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NH}_3 $$

$n_{\text{N}_2} = 28.0/28.0 = 1.00\text{ mol}$; $n_{\text{H}_2} = 10.0/2.016 = 4.96\text{ mol}$

Divide by coefficients: N$_2$: $1.00/1 = 1.00$; H$_2$: $4.96/3 = 1.65$

Since $1.00 < 1.65$, N$_2$ is the limiting reagent.

Theoretical yield: $1.00\text{ mol N}_2 \rightarrow 2.00\text{ mol NH}_3 = 34.1\text{ g}$

H$_2$ consumed: $3 \times 1.00 = 3.00\text{ mol}$ (out of $4.96\text{ mol}$) → $1.96\text{ mol H}_2$ excess = $3.95\text{ g}$

13. Concentration Terms in Solutions ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

A solution comprises a solute (minor component) dissolved in a solvent (major component). Concentration expresses the amount of solute per quantity of solution or solvent.

13.1 Mass Percentage ($\%$ w/w)

$$ \%\text{ w/w} = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solution}}} \times 100 = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solute}} + m_{\text{solvent}}} \times 100 $$
Variables:
$m_{\text{solute}}$ — mass of solute (g)
$m_{\text{solution}}$ — total mass of solution (g)
Units: dimensionless (%)

13.2 Volume Percentage ($\%$ v/v)

$$ \%\text{ v/v} = \frac{V_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}} \times 100 $$
Variables:
$V_{\text{solute}}$ — volume of solute (mL or L)
$V_{\text{solution}}$ — total volume of solution (same units)
Commonly used for liquid-liquid solutions (ethanol in water)

13.3 Mass by Volume Percentage ($\%$ w/v) ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

$$ \%\text{ w/v} = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}} \times 100 $$
Variables:
$m_{\text{solute}}$ — mass of solute (g)
$V_{\text{solution}}$ — volume of solution (mL)
Units: g/100 mL

13.4 Parts Per Million (ppm)

$$ \text{ppm} = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solution}}} \times 10^6 $$

Used for very dilute solutions: trace elements in water, pollutants in air.

13.5 Mole Fraction ($x$) ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

$$ x_i = \frac{n_i}{\sum n_{\text{all components}}} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum x_i = 1 $$
Variables:
$n_i$ — moles of component $i$ (mol)
$\sum n$ — total moles of all components (mol)
$x_{\text{solute}} + x_{\text{solvent}} = 1$ for a binary solution

Mole Fraction — Why It Matters:

Mole fraction is temperature-independent because it depends on mass (via moles), not volume. It is used in Raoult's law for vapor pressure, and in calculating vapor composition during distillation.

HOTS Strategy:

Connecting mole fraction to molarity: When density ($\rho$) is given, convert mole fraction to molarity via:

$$ M = \frac{x_{\text{solute}} \times \rho \times 1000}{x_{\text{solute}} M_{\text{solute}} + x_{\text{solvent}} M_{\text{solvent}}} $$

This is a frequent board exam problem type — density acts as a conversion bridge.

13.6 Molarity ($M$) ⚡ CBSE Expected - High Yield

$$ M = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution (L)}}} = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{M_{\text{solute}} \times V_{\text{(L)}}} $$
Variables:
$n_{\text{solute}}$ — amount of solute (mol)
$V_{\text{solution}}$ — volume of solution (L)
Units: mol L$^{-1}$ (or M)

Temperature Dependence of Molarity:

Molarity depends on volume, which expands with temperature. As $T \uparrow$, $V \uparrow$, so $M \downarrow$ — even though the amount of solute is unchanged. This is why molarity is not preferred for precise thermodynamic work.

Dilution Formula:

$$ M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 $$

Where (1) = concentrated stock solution and (2) = diluted solution. The product $M \times V$ = moles of solute, which is conserved during dilution.

HOTS Strategy:

Mixing two solutions of the same solute:

$$ M_{\text{mix}} = \frac{M_1 V_1 + M_2 V_2}{V_1 + V_2} $$

This is a weighted average by volume.

13.7 Molality ($m$) ⚡ CBSE High-Yield

$$ m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solvent (kg)}}} $$
Variables:
$n_{\text{solute}}$ — amount of solute (mol)
$m_{\text{solvent}}$ — mass of solvent (kg)
Units: mol kg$^{-1}$ (or $m$)

Why Molality Is Temperature-Independent:

Mass does not change with temperature. Unlike volume, $m_{\text{solvent}}$ remains constant regardless of thermal expansion. For this reason, molality (not molarity) is used in colligative property calculations (freezing point depression, boiling point elevation).

HOTS Strategy — Density Bridge:

If molarity ($M$) and density ($\rho$, in g mL$^{-1}$) are given, convert to molality:

$$ m = \frac{M}{\rho - \frac{M \times M_{\text{solute}}}{1000}} \times 1000 $$

Where the denominator is effectively the mass of solvent (in g) per liter of solution. This is a common exam problem.

Q 1.1: Calculate the molecular mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$, and $\text{CH}_4$.

Solution:

Atomic masses: H = $1.008\text{ u}$, O = $16.00\text{ u}$, C = $12.01\text{ u}$

$\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $2(1.008) + 16.00 = 2.016 + 16.00 = \mathbf{18.016\text{ u}}$

$\text{CO}_2$: $12.01 + 2(16.00) = 12.01 + 32.00 = \mathbf{44.01\text{ u}}$

$\text{CH}_4$: $12.01 + 4(1.008) = 12.01 + 4.032 = \mathbf{16.042\text{ u}}$

Q 1.2: Calculate the mass percent of each element in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$.

Solution:

Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $M = 18.016\text{ g mol}^{-1}$):

$$ \%\text{H} = \frac{2 \times 1.008}{18.016} \times 100 = \frac{2.016}{18.016} \times 100 = 11.19\% $$ $$ \%\text{O} = \frac{16.00}{18.016} \times 100 = 88.81\% $$

Carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$, $M = 44.01\text{ g mol}^{-1}$):

$$ \%\text{C} = \frac{12.01}{44.01} \times 100 = 27.29\% $$ $$ \%\text{O} = \frac{2 \times 16.00}{44.01} \times 100 = \frac{32.00}{44.01} \times 100 = 72.71\% $$

Q 1.7: State and illustrate the Law of Multiple Proportions with examples.

Solution:

Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton, 1803): When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers.

Example — Sulfur and Oxygen:

SO$_2$: $32\text{ g S}$ combines with $32\text{ g O}$

SO$_3$: $32\text{ g S}$ combines with $48\text{ g O}$

For fixed $32\text{ g S}$, O masses are $32 : 48 = 2 : 3$ — a simple whole-number ratio.

Q 1.8: Define Avogadro's Law and give its significance.

Avogadro's Law: Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules.

Significance:

  • Provides the relationship between gas volume and number of moles: $V \propto n$
  • Leads to the concept of molar volume: $22.4\text{ L}$ at STP
  • Distinguishes between atoms and molecules (e.g., $\text{O}_2$ is diatomic)
Q 1.9: What is the difference between empirical and molecular formula? Give an example.

Empirical formula: Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (e.g., $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ for glucose).

Molecular formula: Actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule (e.g., $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ for glucose).

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula: $n = M_{\text{molecular}} / M_{\text{empirical}}$.

Q 1.10: A compound contains $4.07\%$ H, $24.27\%$ C, and $71.65\%$ Cl. Its molar mass is $98.96\text{ g mol}^{-1}$. Find empirical and molecular formulas.

Solution:

In $100\text{ g}$: H = $4.07\text{ g}$, C = $24.27\text{ g}$, Cl = $71.65\text{ g}$

Moles: H = $4.07/1.008 = 4.04$; C = $24.27/12.01 = 2.02$; Cl = $71.65/35.45 = 2.02$

Divide by $2.02$: C : H : Cl = $1 : 2 : 1$ → Empirical formula = $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}$

Empirical mass = $12.01 + 2.016 + 35.45 = 49.48\text{ g mol}^{-1}$

$n = 98.96/49.48 = 2$ → Molecular formula = $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{Cl}_2$

Q 1.20: $5.0\text{ g}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ reacts with $50\text{ mL}$ of $1.0\text{ M}$ HCl. Find limiting reagent and $\text{CO}_2$ produced.

Solution:

$\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$

$n_{\text{CaCO}_3} = 5.0/100.09 = 0.0500\text{ mol}$

$n_{\text{HCl}} = 1.0 \times 0.050 = 0.050\text{ mol}$

Divide by coefficients: CaCO$_3$: $0.0500/1 = 0.0500$; HCl: $0.050/2 = 0.025$

HCl has the smaller value → HCl is the limiting reagent.

Moles of $\text{CO}_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times n_{\text{HCl}} = 0.025\text{ mol}$

Mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 0.025 \times 44.01 = 1.10\text{ g}$

Q 1.22: How many significant figures in (a) $0.0025$, (b) $208$, (c) $5005$, (d) $126000$, (e) $500.0$?

Solution:

(a) $0.0025$ → leading zeros not significant → 2 SF

(b) $208$ → all non-zero + zero between → 3 SF

(c) $5005$ → zeros between non-zero → 4 SF

(d) $126000$ → ambiguous: 3, 4, 5, or 6 SF depending on precision of measurement

(e) $500.0$ → trailing zero with decimal → 4 SF
