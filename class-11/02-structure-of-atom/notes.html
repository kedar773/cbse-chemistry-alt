1.1 Subatomic Particles

Atoms are made of three fundamental particles. Think of an atom like a tiny stadium — a heavy nucleus at the centre with electrons whizzing around it in empty space.

Real-life example: If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be like a pea at the centre — almost all the space is empty!

Mass of electron: 9.109 × 10−31 kg  |  Charge: 1.602 × 10−19 C  |  Specific charge (e/m): 1.76 × 1011 C/kg

1.2 Thomson's Plum Pudding Model CBSE Basic

J.J. Thomson (1898) discovered the electron using cathode ray tubes. He proposed that an atom is a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it — like raisins in a pudding or seeds in a watermelon. This model failed to explain α-particle scattering.

1.3 Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment ⚡ CBSE Expected

Ernest Rutherford (1911) bombarded gold foil with α-particles:

  • Most passed straight through → atom is mostly empty space
  • Some deflected by small angles → positive charge is concentrated
  • Very few (1 in 20,000) bounced back → a tiny, dense nucleus exists

Limitation: Could not explain why electrons don't spiral into the nucleus.

1.4 Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom ⚡ High Yield

Niels Bohr (1913) fixed Rutherford's problem by adding quantum rules.

Three Postulates:

  1. Electrons revolve in permitted circular orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy.
  2. Angular momentum is quantized: mevr = nℏ (n = 1, 2, 3...)
  3. Energy jumps: E2 − E1 = hν
Bohr's Radius: rn = n2 × 52.9 pm   📌 [See Solved Q 2.17]
Energy: En = −13.6/n2 eV = −2.18×10−18/n2 J
Velocity: vn = 2.188×106 × (Z/n) m/s

1.6 Limitations of Bohr's Model CBSE Expected

  • Works only for hydrogen-like species (He+, Li2+)
  • Fails for multi-electron atoms
  • Cannot explain fine structure (splitting in magnetic fields)
  • Violates Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

2.1 Dual Nature of Matter ⚡ High Yield

Louis de Broglie (1924) proposed that matter has dual nature — every moving particle has a wavelength.

De Broglie Wavelength: λ = h/mv = h/p   📌 [See Solved Q 2.24]
For accelerated electron: λ = h/√(2meV) = 1.226/√V nm

Real-life: A cricket ball at 30 m/s has λ ≈ 10−34 m (undetectable). An electron at 106 m/s has λ ≈ 0.7 nm (measurable).

2.2 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle ⚡ High Yield

We cannot simultaneously know both the exact position and momentum of a particle.

Δx · Δp ≥ h/(4π)   or   ΔE · Δt ≥ h/(4π)   📌 [See Solved Q 2.27]

Analogy: Photographing a speeding car — sharp photo (good position, no speed) vs blurred (speed visible, poor position).

2.4 Orbital Shapes and Nodes ⚡ High Yield

  • s-orbital (l=0): Spherical. 0 angular nodes.
  • p-orbital (l=1): Dumbbell (2 lobes). 1 angular node.
  • d-orbital (l=2): Cloverleaf (4 lobes). 2 angular nodes.
Angular = l  |  Radial = n−l−1  |  Total = n−1
⚡ Board Exam Favourite! 📌 [See Solved Q 2.38]

Example: 3p (n=3,l=1): Angular=1, Radial=1, Total=2.

2.5 Electronic Configuration & Aufbau Principle

Order: 1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p→5s→4d→5p→6s→4f→5d→6p→7s→5f→6d→7p

Three Rules:

  1. Aufbau: Fill lowest energy orbitals first.
  2. Pauli's Exclusion: Max 2 e− per orbital, opposite spins.
  3. Hund's Rule: Fill all singly with parallel spins before pairing.
⚠️ Anomalous Configurations:
Cr (Z=24): [Ar] 3d5 4s1 (half-filled d stability) ⚡ CBSE Expected
Cu (Z=29): [Ar] 3d10 4s1 (fully-filled d stability) ⚡ CBSE Expected

2.6 Photoelectric Effect ⚡ High Yield

Einstein's Equation: hν = hν0 + ½mv2   or   KEmax = hν − φ   📌 [See Solved Q 2.9]

Applications: Solar panels, automatic doors, burglar alarms, digital cameras.

Q 2.47: Nodal plane in pz orbital.

The xy-plane is the nodal plane (zero probability for e in pz).

Q 2.48: Which orbital is non-directional?

s-orbital is spherically symmetrical, hence non-directional.

Q 2.49: Why are p-orbitals directional?

Dumbbell shape along specific axes. Probability concentrated along axis, zero at nodal plane.

Q 2.50: Is 2p4 with △▽ △ △ valid?

Yes. Each orbital gets one △ before pairing follows Hund's rule.

Q 2.51: Unpaired e in S (Z=16)?

Q 2.52: Explain Pauli, Hund, Aufbau. ⚡ Important

Pauli: No two e share all four Q.N. Max 2/orbital, opposite spins. Hund: Fill singly, parallel spins first. Aufbau: Lowest energy first.

Q 2.54: Significance of (n+l) rule?

Lower (n+l) = lower energy. If same (n+l), lower n fills first. Explains 4s before 3d.

Q 2.64: Difference between orbit and orbital. ⚡ Important

Orbit: Fixed 2D path (Bohr), violates uncertainty. Orbital: 3D probability region (QM), consistent with uncertainty.
