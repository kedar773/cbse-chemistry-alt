1. Genesis of Periodic Classification

Döbereiner’s Triads (1829)

Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner grouped elements into triads where the atomic weight of the middle element was approximately the mean of the other two. Examples: Li (6.9), Na (23.0), K (39.1) — ½(6.9+39.1) = 23.0 ✓. Also: Ca–Sr–Ba, Cl–Br–I. Limitation: Only ~20 elements known; only a few triads existed.

Newlands’ Law of Octaves (1865)

John Newlands arranged elements in order of increasing atomic weight and observed that every eighth element had similar properties (like musical octaves). Example: Li (1), Na (8), K (15). Limitation: Failed beyond calcium (Ca, 40); could not accommodate transition elements; assumed only 56 elements existed.

Mendeleev’s Periodic Table (1869)

Dmitri Mendeleev arranged 63 known elements by atomic weight and chemical properties. His table had 12 rows and 8 columns. Key achievements:

  • Left gaps for undiscovered elements (predicted eka-boron, eka-aluminium, eka-silicon)
  • Predicted properties of Ga (eka-Al), Sc (eka-B), Ge (eka-Si) with remarkable accuracy
  • Could accommodate noble gases when discovered (Group 18)
  • Placed some elements out of strict atomic weight order to keep similar chemistry together (e.g., Te–I, Ar–K)

Limitations: No satisfactory explanation for position of H or transition metals; no explanation for periodicity; atomic weight not always the fundamental property.

Moseley’s Modern Periodic Law (1913)

Henry Moseley studied X-ray spectra of elements and discovered that the frequency (ν) of characteristic X-rays is related to atomic number (Z): √ν ∝ (Z − b). This established that atomic number, not atomic weight, is the fundamental property determining element position.

Modern Periodic Law: “The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.”

2. Modern Periodic Table: Blocks & Periods

The table is organized by electron configuration into 4 blocks, 18 groups, 7 periods. The period number = principal quantum number (n) of the valence shell. Group number is related to the number of valence electrons.

s-Block Elements (Groups 1–2)

ns1–2 configuration. Includes alkali metals (Gr 1: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) and alkaline earth metals (Gr 2: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra).

  • Low ionization enthalpy — easily lose electrons to form M+ (Gr 1) or M2+ (Gr 2) ions
  • Soft metals with low melting points (Gr 1); harder in Gr 2
  • Strong metallic character; highly reactive (stored under oil)
  • Compound character: predominantly ionic; M+ and M2+ are colorless (no d-electrons)
  • Flame coloration due to low IE → excited e− returns to ground state emitting visible light
  • Diagonal relationship: Li ∼ Mg (similar ionic size, polarizing power, covalent character in chlorides/nitrates)
  • See Solved Q 3.8

p-Block Elements (Groups 13–18)

ns2np1–6 configuration. Contains metals, metalloids, and non-metals.

  • Metalloid staircase: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te — elements bordering the diagonal have intermediate properties
  • Oxidation states: variable (group number − 10 for lower, group number for higher). Inert pair effect: Down a group, the ns2 pair becomes increasingly reluctant to participate in bonding (e.g., Tl+ more stable than Tl3+; Pb2+ > Pb4+). This is due to poor shielding of d/f electrons, increasing Zeff on s-electrons.
  • Noble gases (Gr 18): full octet (except He), very high IE, low reactivity
  • Anomalous behavior of second-period elements (Li–Ne): small size, high Zeff, absence of d-orbitals → maximum covalency of 4

d-Block Elements (Groups 3–12)

(n−1)d1–10ns1–2. Transition metals between s- and p-blocks.

  • Three transition series: 1st (Sc–Zn), 2nd (Y–Cd), 3rd (La–Hg, incl. lanthanides)
  • Variable oxidation states due to small energy difference between (n−1)d and ns orbitals
  • Formation of colored ions (d→d transitions), paramagnetism (unpaired d-electrons)
  • Catalytic activity, complex formation, high melting/boiling points
  • 3d54s1 (Cr) and 3d104s1 (Cu) — exceptions to aufbau order for extra stability

f-Block Elements (Lanthanides & Actinides)

(n−2)f1–14(n−1)d0–1ns2. Placed separately below the main table.

  • 4f series (La–Lu, Z=57–71): Lanthanide contraction — steady decrease in atomic/ionic radii across the series (from La3+ 106 pm to Lu3+ 85 pm) due to poor shielding of 4f electrons → increasing Zeff
  • 5f series (Ac–Lr, Z=89–103): radioactive; actinide contraction similar but less pronounced
  • Consequences of lanthanide contraction: similar ionic radii of post-lanthanide elements (Zr–Hf, Nb–Ta, Mo–W) → chemical similarity; difficult separation of lanthanides

3. Quantum Mechanical Context of Periodicity

Aufbau principle: Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p. The (n+ℓ) rule governs order: lower (n+ℓ) is filled first; for same (n+ℓ), lower n is filled first.

Pauli exclusion principle: No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers. Each orbital holds maximum 2 electrons with opposite spins.

Hund’s rule of maximum multiplicity: Electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly first (with parallel spins) to maximize total spin, minimizing energy via exchange energy.

The periodic trends in all chemical properties (radii, IE, EGH, EN) are direct consequences of how Z, n, σ, and Zeff evolve as we move across a period (ΔZ dominates) or down a group (Δn dominates). This is the central unifying theme of the chapter.

Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff) — The Master Variable

Almost every periodic trend originates from a single quantity: effective nuclear charge (Zeff), the net positive charge experienced by a given electron after accounting for shielding by inner electrons.

Zeff = Z − σ

where Z = atomic number (nuclear charge) and σ = shielding (screening) constant, estimated via Slater’s rules.

Key insight: As Z increases across a period, electrons enter the same principal shell (n constant). Each added electron experiences incomplete shielding from the others in the same shell (σ increases slightly). The net effect: Zeff increases across a period → electrons pulled closer → smaller atom size, harder to remove, greater attraction.

Down a group: n increases (new shell). Although Z also increases dramatically, the shielding by all inner shells (σ) also jumps, and the distance from nucleus dominates. Zeff stays relatively constant or decreases slightly → larger atom, easier to remove outermost electron.

📌 Slater’s Rules (simplified): For an ns/np electron, each other electron in the same group contributes σ = 0.35 (except 1s: 0.30); each electron in the (n−1) shell contributes σ = 0.85; each in (n−2) or lower contributes σ = 1.00. For nd/nf: each other electron in same group contributes σ = 0.35; all lower shells contribute σ = 1.00. Poorer shielding of d and f electrons is why transition and inner-transition elements show subtle trends.

1. Atomic and Ionic Radii

Definition: Atomic radius — half the distance between two adjacent nuclei in the elemental state. Types: covalent radius (homonuclear diatomic), metallic radius (metal crystal), van der Waals radius (noble gases, non-bonded).

Mechanics of the Trend

Across a period: Z increases → +1 proton; new electron enters the same shell → σ increases only ~0.35 (Slater). ΔZeff ≈ +0.65 per step. Stronger pull → radius decreases.

Down a group: n increases by 1 → new, larger principal shell added. Even though Z grows significantly, the outermost electron is now much farther from the nucleus. ΔZeff ≈ constant. Radius increases.

Exceptions

  • Noble gases: van der Waals radius (~50% larger than preceding halogen covalent radius). No covalent bonding in monatomic gases.
  • Ga vs Al: Atomic radius of Ga (~135 pm) is smaller than Al (~143 pm). Reason: d-block contraction — 3d10 electrons in Ga are poor shields (σ per d electron ≈ 1.00 for ns electrons vs ~0.85 for (n−1)p), so Zeff on Ga’s 4p electron is higher than expected.
  • Lanthanide Contraction: Gradual decrease in radii across 4f series due to poor shielding of 4f electrons. Consequences: Zr (160) ≈ Hf (159), Nb (146) ≈ Ta (146), Mo (139) ≈ W (139) — nearly identical radii → similar chemistry, difficult separation.

Ionic Radii — Size Relationships

Cations: M → Mn+ + ne−. Loss of entire valence shell → drastic size decrease (e.g., Na: 186 pm → Na+: 102 pm). Even when losing from same shell (transition metals), increased Zeff per remaining electron shrinks the ion.

Anions: X + e− → X−. Added electron increases inter-electronic repulsion → electron cloud expands → radius increases (e.g., Cl: 99 pm → Cl−: 181 pm).

Isoelectronic Series: Species with same number of electrons but different Z. Radius decreases as Z increases: O2− (140) > F− (133) > Na+ (102) > Mg2+ (72) > Al3+ (53.5). All 10 electrons (neon core); increasing nuclear charge pulls the same electron cloud tighter.

2. Ionization Enthalpy (ΔiHθ)

Definition: The minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom: M(g) → M+(g) + e−; ΔiH > 0 (endothermic).

Mechanics of the Trend

Across a period: Zeff increases → electrons held more tightly → more energy needed to remove outermost electron. ΔiH increases.

Down a group: n increases → outermost electron is farther and better shielded → easier to remove. ΔiH decreases.

Key Anomalies with Quantum Explanation

(i) Be (1s²2s²) vs B (1s²2s²2p¹) — Be > B

Be has a filled 2s subshell (2s²). Removing an electron from a filled s-subshell requires breaking the stable spherical pair. B has one electron in 2p, which is higher in energy (by ~2 eV) and better shielded from the nucleus. Thus B’s Δi1H (801 kJ/mol) < Be (900 kJ/mol).

(ii) N (2s²2p³) vs O (2s²2p⁴) — N > O

N has half-filled 2p³ configuration: three electrons in three p-orbitals with parallel spins. This configuration enjoys extra exchange energy (stabilization from quantum mechanical exchange integrals between electrons of same spin in degenerate orbitals). Removing an electron from N destroys this stable half-filled arrangement. O has 2p⁴: one doubly-occupied p-orbital. The paired electron experiences inter-electronic repulsion, making it easier to remove. Hence O’s Δi1H (1314 kJ/mol) < N (1402 kJ/mol).

(iii) Mg (3s²) vs Al (3s²3p¹) — Mg > Al

Same logic as Be vs B. Mg's filled 3s² vs Al's single 3p¹ electron.

(iv) P (3s²3p³) vs S (3s²3p⁴) — P > S

Same as N vs O. P's half-filled 3p³ (exchange energy stabilization).

Successive Ionization Enthalpies

Δi1H < Δi2H < Δi3H ... because each successive electron is removed from a more positive ion (higher Zeff per remaining electron). A sharp jump in successive IE values indicates that the electron came from an inner shell (closer to the noble gas core). This explains group valency: Na (IE1 low, IE2 huge → Na+ is stable; never Na2+).

3. Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegHθ)

Definition: The energy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom: X(g) + e− → X−(g); ΔegH may be −ve or +ve.

Mechanics of the Trend

Across a period: Zeff increases → incoming electron experiences stronger attraction → more energy released (more negative ΔegH). Exception: noble gases (already stable octet; electron enters next shell) and Group 15 (half-filled p³, stable).

Down a group: New shell added, atom larger, incoming electron is farther from nucleus, less attraction. However, anomaly at the top of each group:

Key Anomalies

F vs Cl: Cl (−349 kJ/mol) has MORE negative ΔegH than F (−328 kJ/mol)

Despite F being more electronegative, F has the least negative ΔegH among halogens. Reason: F has an extremely compact 2p subshell (2s²2p⁵). Adding a 7th electron creates severe inter-electronic repulsion in the small 2p space. The energy released by attraction is partially offset by this repulsion, making ΔegH less negative. Cl has a larger 3p subshell; the added electron experiences less repulsion.

O vs S: O (−141 kJ/mol) less negative than S (−200 kJ/mol)

Same reasoning as F vs Cl: O’s 2p subshell too compact.

N (≈ 0 kJ/mol)

Half-filled 2p³ is highly stable. Adding an electron would force pairing and destroy the exchange-energy-stabilized configuration. Essentially no energy benefit.

Noble Gases (positive ΔegH)

Stable ns²np⁶ octet. Adding an electron requires placing it in the next higher shell (n+1)s — energetically unfavorable.

See Solved Q 3.16, 3.18

4. Electronegativity (χ)

Definition: The tendency of an atom in a covalently bonded molecule to attract the shared pair of electrons toward itself. (Pauling scale, 0–4).

Mechanics of the Trend

Across a period: Zeff increases → atom pulls electron pair harder → χ increases.

Down a group: n increases → bond length increases → atom has weaker pull → χ decreases.

Highest: F (4.0). Lowest: Cs (0.79), Fr (0.7).

Outliers

  • Ga and Al have nearly identical χ (1.81 Al, 1.82 Ga) due to d-block contraction offsetting the expected decrease down Group 13.
  • Tl has slightly higher χ than In for similar reasons (f-block contraction).

Applications: Bond polarity (Δχ > 1.7 → predominantly ionic; Δχ < 0.5 → non-polar covalent; intermediate → polar covalent). Electronegativity differences explain boiling points, dipole moments, and chemical reactivity.

Diagonal Relationship — The s-p Block Bridge

Elements of second period (Li, Be, B) show similarities with elements diagonally below + right (Mg, Al, Si). Cause: similar charge density (charge/radius ratio), hence almost equal polarizing power, comparable covalent character, and analogous properties.

  • Li ∼ Mg: Both form monoxides (Li₂O, MgO); carbonates decompose on heating; nitrides with Li₃N, Mg₃N₂; chlorides are deliquescent, soluble in organic solvents.
  • Be ∼ Al: Both form amphoteric oxides; BeCl₂ and AlCl₃ are covalent (Lewis acids); carbides yield methane.
  • B ∼ Si: Both are semiconductors; form volatile covalent halides (BF₃, SiF₄); acids are weak (H₃BO₃, H₂SiO₃); borides and silicides are similar.

5. Periodic Trends in Oxides

Oxide nature varies systematically from basic → amphoteric → acidic across a period (left to right).

Period 3 Example:

Na₂O (basic) → MgO (basic) → Al₂O₃ (amphoteric) → SiO₂ (weakly acidic) → P₄O₁₀ (acidic) → SO₃ (acidic) → Cl₂O₇ (strongly acidic)

Why? As metallic character decreases across a period (ΔZ, Zeff increases → IE increases → less willing to lose electrons and form ionic bonds → covalency in bonding increases). Electronegative elements form covalent bonds with oxygen; the O—X bond is polarized with δ⁺ on X, δ⁻ on O. In water, these highly polar molecules readily release H⁺ (proton) → acidic. Electropositive metals (left side) form ionic oxides with O²⁻, which accepts H⁺ from water → basic (OH⁻).

Down a group: Acidic character decreases, basic character increases. Example: CO₂ (acidic) → SiO₂ (weak acid) → GeO₂ (amphoteric) → SnO₂ (amphoteric → basic) → PbO₂ (basic).

Amphoteric Oxides

Oxides that react with both acids and bases: BeO, Al₂O₃, ZnO, Ga₂O₃, GeO₂, SnO₂, PbO, Sb₂O₃, etc.

Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (basic behavior: oxide reacts with acid)
Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O (acidic behavior: oxide reacts with base → aluminate)

Peroxides and Superoxides

  • Normal oxides: O²⁻ (e.g., Na₂O, MgO)
  • Peroxides: O₂²⁻, contain O—O single bond (e.g., Na₂O₂, BaO₂). Formed only by larger cations.
  • Superoxides: O₂⁻, paramagnetic (e.g., KO₂, RbO₂, CsO₂). Only formed by large Group 1 metals (K, Rb, Cs) where lattice energy stabilizes the large anion.

6. Chemical Reactivity & Metallic Character

Metallic character measures how readily an atom loses electrons (i.e., low IE, low EN). Increases down a group, decreases across a period.

Non-metallic character: High IE, high EN, high ΔegH (more negative). Increases across a period, decreases down a group.

Trend in Reactivity of Metals

Metals at the bottom-left (Cs, Fr) are the most reactive (largest atomic radius, lowest IE, readily lose valence electron). Metals in the middle of a period (Al, Zn, Fe) are moderately reactive. In Group 1, reactivity increases down: Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs.

Trend in Reactivity of Non-Metals

Non-metals at the top-right (F, O) are the most reactive (small size, high EN, high Zeff, strong tendency to gain electrons). Reactivity down Group 17 decreases: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

Screening effect vs Nuclear pull: These are the two competing forces determining all periodic properties. Inner electrons “screen” the nuclear charge (σ), reducing Zeff. The balance between increasing Z (more pull) vs increasing σ (more screening) + increasing n (distance) determines whether a property increases or decreases.

📌 This conceptual balance is the single most tested reasoning question in CBSE. Explain any trend by: (a) what happens to Z, (b) what happens to σ/n, (c) net Zeff change, (d) how this affects the property.

Q 3.1 What is the basic theme of classification of elements?

The basic theme is to arrange the known elements in a systematic order that groups together elements with similar properties and reveals periodic trends in properties. This helps in the systematic study of the 118+ known elements — instead of memorizing individual properties, we learn patterns across groups and periods. Classification also highlights gaps that led to the discovery of new elements (e.g., Mendeleev's eka-Al → Ga).

Q 3.2 What is the basis of Mendeleev’s classification?

Mendeleev arranged elements in order of increasing atomic weight, grouping elements with similar chemical formulas of their hydrides and oxides (e.g., Cl₂O₇, Br₂O₇, I₂O₇ — same formula). He left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties. Where atomic weight order contradicted chemical similarity (Te vs I, Ar vs K), he placed them by properties, implying atomic weight was not the fundamental property.

Q 3.3 What is the basic difference between Mendeleev’s and Modern Periodic Law?

Mendeleev used atomic weight as the organizing principle; Modern Periodic Law (Moseley, 1913) uses atomic number. Moseley's X-ray studies showed that the frequency of emitted X-rays depends on the nuclear charge (Z), not atomic mass. Atomic number is the fundamental property determining the electron configuration and chemical properties.

Q 3.4 How does atomic radius vary in a period and in a group? Explain.

Across a period: Atomic radius decreases. Reason: Nuclear charge (Z) increases while electrons enter the same shell (n constant). Zeff increases (σ only increases by ~0.35 per electron in the same shell), pulling the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.

Down a group: Atomic radius increases. Reason: A new principal shell (higher n) is added, which is farther from the nucleus. Although Z increases significantly, the distance increase dominates, and Zeff remains relatively constant.

Q 3.5 Explain the periodic trend in ionization enthalpy across a period and down a group.

Across a period: Ionization enthalpy (IE) increases due to increasing Zeff — electrons are held more tightly, requiring more energy for removal.

Down a group: IE decreases because the outermost electron is at a greater distance (higher n) and experiences more shielding — less energy needed to remove it.

Notable exceptions: Be > B, N > O (as explained in Deep Concept Core) — these arise from subshell stability (filled/half-filled configurations).

Q 3.6 How does electron gain enthalpy vary across a period and down a group?

Across a period: ΔegH generally becomes more negative (more energy released) as Zeff increases, making the atom more attractive to an incoming electron. Exceptions: Group 15 elements (half-filled ns²np³ — stable, electron addition unfavorable) and noble gases (already octet).

Down a group: ΔegH generally becomes less negative because the extra electron is added farther from the nucleus. Exception: F (less negative than Cl) due to severe inter-electronic repulsion in the compact 2p subshell.

Q 3.7 Define electronegativity. How does it vary in the periodic table?

Definition: Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a covalent bond to attract the shared electron pair toward itself (Pauling scale).

Variation: Increases across a period (higher Zeff → stronger pull on bond electrons); decreases down a group (greater distance, more shielding → weaker pull). The most electronegative element is fluorine (χ = 4.0).

Q 3.8 How would you justify the presence of 18 elements in the 5th period?

The 5th period corresponds to n = 5. The electron filling sequence is: 5s (2 e⁻), 4d (10 e⁻), 5p (6 e⁻). Total: 2 + 10 + 6 = 18 elements. The 4d subshell (n=4, ℓ=2) fills after 5s because 4d has higher energy than 5s but lower than 5p, following the (n+ℓ) rule: 5s (5+0=5), 4d (4+2=6), 5p (5+1=6). Between 4d and 5p, the one with lower n (4d) fills first.

Q 3.9 What are the blocks of the periodic table? Give their characteristic features.

The four blocks are based on the orbital being filled in the valence shell:

s-block: ns¹⁻². Groups 1–2. Highly reactive metals; low IE; form +1/+2 cations.

p-block: ns²np¹⁻⁶. Groups 13–18. Includes metals, non-metals, metalloids; variable oxidation states; inert pair effect down groups.

d-block: (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns¹⁻². Groups 3–12. Transition metals; variable oxidation states; colored compounds; paramagnetism; catalytic activity.

f-block: (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴(n−1)d⁰⁻¹ns². Lanthanides and actinides; placed below main table; lanthanide contraction.

Q 3.10 What is the diagonal relationship? Explain with examples.

Diagonal relationship refers to the similarity in properties between an element in the 2nd period and the element diagonally below and to the right in the 3rd period. This arises from similar charge density (ionic charge/radius ratio) due to opposing trends in radius and charge.

Li(1) ∼ Mg(2): Both form monoxides (Li₂O, MgO), carbonates decompose on heating, form nitrides with N₂ (Li₃N, Mg₃N₂).

Be(2) ∼ Al(3): Both are amphoteric, form covalent chlorides (BeCl₂, AlCl₃ — Lewis acids), their carbides yield CH₄.

B(3) ∼ Si(4): Both are semiconductors, form volatile halides (BF₃, SiF₄), weak oxyacids (H₃BO₃, H₂SiO₃).

Q 3.11 Explain the significance of atomic radii in understanding periodic trends.

Atomic radius directly influences: (i) ionization enthalpy (smaller atom → higher IE as e⁻ is closer to nucleus, harder to remove), (ii) electron gain enthalpy (smaller atom → more repulsion for incoming e⁻ → less negative ΔegH), (iii) electronegativity (smaller atom → bond pair closer to nucleus → higher EN), (iv) chemical reactivity (larger atom → easier to lose e⁻ → more metallic). The radius is the most visually intuitive manifestation of Zeff changes — as Zeff increases, radius shrinks; as n increases, radius expands.

Q 3.12 Arrange the following in order of increasing atomic radius: Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar.

All are Period 3 elements. Atomic radius decreases across a period. Therefore: Ar < Cl < S < P < Si < Al < Mg < Na (increasing order). Argon's van der Waals radius is actually larger than Cl's covalent radius, but in terms of covalent/atomic radius trend, Ar (if measured as van der Waals) would appear larger. NCERT typically considers the decreasing trend: Na > Mg > Al > Si > P > S > Cl.

Q 3.13 What is effective nuclear charge? How is it related to the size of an atom?

Effective nuclear charge (Zeff) is the net positive charge experienced by an electron after accounting for the screening/shielding by inner electrons: Zeff = Z − σ. As Zeff increases, the outer electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus, making the atom smaller. Conversely, as Zeff decreases, the atomic radius increases. This is the single most important concept connecting nuclear charge to chemical behavior.

Q 3.14 Define screening effect. How does it affect periodic properties?

The screening (shielding) effect is the reduction in effective nuclear charge experienced by an outermost electron due to repulsion by inner electrons. Inner shell electrons “screen” the nuclear charge, reducing Zeff. A larger screening effect makes outer electrons easier to remove (lower IE), easier to expand the atom (larger radius), and less electronegative. Screening increases down a group (more inner shells) but stays relatively constant across a period (same number of inner shells).

Q 3.15 Why does the ionization enthalpy of elements increase across a period?

Across a period, the nuclear charge (Z) increases by +1 for each step, while the new electron enters the same principal shell (same n). The shielding effect increases only slightly (σ increases by ~0.35 per Slater's rules). Therefore Zeff increases significantly across the period. A higher Zeff means electrons are held more tightly by the nucleus, requiring more energy to remove them — hence ionization enthalpy increases. However, deviations occur at Be → B (filled 2s² vs 2p¹) and N → O (half-filled 2p³ stability).

Q 3.16 Explain why fluorine has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine.

Despite being more electronegative, fluorine (ΔegH = −328 kJ/mol) has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine (−349 kJ/mol). This is because fluorine has an extremely small atomic size with a compact 2p subshell (2s²2p⁵). Adding a seventh electron into this already electron-dense 2p subshell creates severe inter-electronic repulsion. This repulsion partially offsets the energy released by the nucleus-electron attraction, making the overall ΔegH less negative. Chlorine has a larger 3p subshell where the added electron experiences significantly less repulsion. This is a classic case where size overrides electronegativity in determining EGH.

Q 3.17 Arrange the elements N, P, O, S in increasing order of (i) ionization enthalpy, (ii) electron gain enthalpy.

(i) Ionization enthalpy (increasing): S < P < O < N. Reasoning: Across periods, IE increases. Down a group, IE decreases. So P < N (down Group 15) and S < O (down Group 16). Between P and O: P (~1012) < O (~1314) kJ/mol because P is in Period 3 (larger, more shielded). N has the highest due to its small size and half-filled 2p³ stability.

(ii) Electron gain enthalpy (increasing negativity): N < O < P < S. Reasoning: N has approximately zero EGH (half-filled 2p³ stability). O has less negative EGH than S due to the size-repulsion effect (same as F vs Cl, but less pronounced). P is less negative than S because S is to the right in the same period (higher Zeff). More negative means more energy released, so order from least to most negative: N (0) < O (−141) < P (−180) < S (−200) kJ/mol.

Q 3.18 Using the periodic table, predict the element with the (a) highest ionization enthalpy, (b) most negative electron gain enthalpy, (c) highest electronegativity, (d) smallest atomic radius.

(a) Helium (He) — highest IE (2372 kJ/mol) due to its filled 1s² shell, very small size, and high Zeff. (He is also the smallest element). Among reactive elements, it's Cl or F; but He's IE is highest.

(b) Chlorine (Cl) — most negative ΔegH (−349 kJ/mol). Not F (due to repulsion in small 2p) and not O or S. Cl has the ideal balance of high Zeff and sufficient space in the 3p subshell to accommodate the extra electron.

(c) Fluorine (F) — highest electronegativity (4.0 on Pauling scale).

(d) Helium (He) — smallest atomic radius (31 pm, van der Waals). Among reactive elements, H (37 pm) or F (71 pm covalent).

Q 3.19 What is the basic difference in the configurations of elements of groups 1 and 2?

Group 1 (alkali metals): ns¹ — one valence s-electron. Group 2 (alkaline earth metals): ns² — two valence s-electrons. Group 1 elements readily lose one electron to form M⁺ ions (stable noble gas configuration). Group 2 elements lose two electrons to form M²⁺ ions. The second ionization enthalpy of Group 1 is very high (breaking noble gas configuration). Group 2 have higher first IE than corresponding Group 1 (higher Zeff due to one more proton, same shell).

Q 3.20 What is the general configuration of p-block elements?

The general valence shell configuration of p-block elements is ns²np¹⁻⁶, where n = 2 to 7 (period number). The p-subshell can hold 1 to 6 electrons, giving Groups 13 (np¹) through 18 (np⁶). Elements in the same group have the same number of p-electrons in their valence shell, leading to similar chemical behavior.

Q 3.21 Give the group number and block of elements with configuration: (i) [Ar]4s² (ii) [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p³ (iii) [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ (iv) [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p⁶

(i) [Ar]4s² → ns² → s-block, Group 2 → Calcium (Ca, Z=20)

(ii) [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p³ → p-block (valence p), Group 15 (s² + p³ = 5 valence e⁻) → Arsenic (As, Z=33)

(iii) [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ → d-block (d subshell filling), Group 6 (5+1 = 6 valence e⁻) → Chromium (Cr, Z=24). Note: expected 3d⁴4s² but half-filled d⁵ + s¹ is more stable.

(iv) [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹⁰6s²6p⁶ → p-block, Group 18 (full s²p⁶ octet) → Radon (Rn, Z=86).

Q 3.22 Classify the following oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric, or neutral: Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃, Cl₂O₇, CO, NO.

Basic: Na₂O (strongly basic — Na is highly electropositive, O²⁻ accepts H⁺), MgO (basic — Mg is metallic).

Amphoteric: Al₂O₃ (reacts with both acids and bases).

Acidic: SiO₂ (weakly acidic, reacts with bases to form silicates), P₄O₁₀ (acidic — P₂O₅ + H₂O → H₃PO₄), SO₃ (acidic — SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄), Cl₂O₇ (strongly acidic — Cl₂O₇ + H₂O → 2HClO₄).

Neutral: CO (no reaction with acids or bases under normal conditions), NO (neutral oxide, does not form salt with acids or bases directly).

This variation from basic to acidic across Period 3 illustrates the gradual change from electropositive metallic character to electronegative non-metallic character.

Q 3.23 Arrange the following in order of increasing metallic character: Si, Be, Mg, Na, P, Al, Cl, S, Ar.

Metallic character increases down a group (Be → Mg) and decreases across a period (Na → Mg → Al → Si → P → S → Cl → Ar). Using the periodic table positions:

Ar (noble gas, zero metallic) < Cl < S < P < Si < Al < Be < Mg < Na — increasing metallic character. Alternatively, Be is less metallic than Mg because it's in Period 2 (smaller, higher IE).

Correct order: Ar < Cl < S < P < Si < Be < Al < Mg < Na (increasing metallic character).

Q 3.24 Explain the following: (a) IE of N is greater than O (b) IE of Be is greater than B (c) IE of Mg is greater than Al (d) IE of P is greater than S.

(a) N > O: N has 2p³ (half-filled), three electrons in three separate p-orbitals with parallel spins. This configuration has exchange energy stabilization — quantum mechanical stabilization from electrons of the same spin exchanging positions. Removing an electron destroys this stable half-filled arrangement, requiring extra energy. O has 2p⁴ — one doubly occupied orbital. The paired electron experiences repulsion, making removal easier.

(b) Be > B: Be has 2s² (filled s-subshell). A filled s-subshell is spherically symmetric and very stable. B has 2s²2p¹ — the single 2p electron is at higher energy, farther from nucleus on average, and easier to remove.

(c) Mg > Al: Same logic as (b): Mg has 3s² (filled), Al has 3s²3p¹.

(d) P > S: Same logic as (a): P has 3p³ (half-filled, exchange energy stabilization), S has 3p⁴.

All four cases demonstrate the periodic principle: subshell stability (filled s² and half-filled p³, d⁵, f⁷) causes deviations from the general increasing IE trend across a period.

Q 3.25 What is the significance of the terms ‘isolated gaseous atom’ in the definition of ionization enthalpy?

"Isolated" means the atom is not bonded to any other atom (no bonding interactions, no intermolecular forces). "Gaseous" means the atom is in the vapor phase (no lattice energy or solvation energy effects). Both conditions ensure that the measured ionization enthalpy is an intrinsic property of the atom itself, unaffected by chemical bonding or physical state. This allows meaningful comparison between different elements.

Q 3.26 The first IE of Mg is 737 kJ/mol while that of Al is 577 kJ/mol — justify.

Mg has the configuration [Ne]3s² — a filled 3s subshell. Removing one electron requires breaking the stable s² pair (greater energy: 737 kJ/mol). Al has [Ne]3s²3p¹ — the 3p electron is at higher energy, farther from the nucleus (lower Zeff for p vs s), and is easier to remove (577 kJ/mol). This is the same filled-subshell anomaly observed with Be vs B.

Q 3.27 Arrange the following ions in order of increasing ionic radius: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, O²⁻, F⁻.

These are isoelectronic species — all have 10 electrons (neon configuration: 1s²2s²2p⁶). For isoelectronic ions, as nuclear charge (Z) increases, the electrons are pulled closer, reducing radius.

Z(O²⁻) = 8, Z(F⁻) = 9, Z(Na⁺) = 11, Z(Mg²⁺) = 12, Z(Al³⁺) = 13.

Increasing Z → decreasing radius. Therefore:

Al³⁺ (53.5 pm) < Mg²⁺ (72 pm) < Na⁺ (102 pm) < F⁻ (133 pm) < O²⁻ (140 pm) (increasing radius).

The oxide ion O²⁻ is the largest despite having the smallest Z — excess electrons cause maximum expansion.

Q 3.28 Which of the following has the largest size? (a) K⁺, (b) Ca²⁺, (c) S²⁻, (d) Ar.

K⁺ (Z=19), Ca²⁺ (Z=20), S²⁻ (Z=16), Ar (Z=18) — all are isoelectronic (18 electrons, argon configuration). Size decreases with increasing Z. Order: S²⁻ (Z=16, largest) > Ar (Z=18) > K⁺ (Z=19) > Ca²⁺ (Z=20, smallest). The anion S²⁻ has the lowest nuclear charge for the same electron cloud → least pull → largest size.

Answer: S²⁻

Q 3.29 Which of the following is larger — Cl⁻ or Cl? Explain.

Cl⁻ is larger than Cl. When a neutral Cl atom (1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵) gains an electron to become Cl⁻ (3p⁶, noble gas configuration), the added electron increases inter-electronic repulsion within the valence shell, causing the electron cloud to expand outward. Additionally, the 17 protons must now attract 18 electrons — the attraction per electron decreases slightly. Net result: ionic radius of Cl⁻ (181 pm) is significantly larger than covalent radius of Cl (99 pm).

Q 3.30 What are isoelectronic species? Give examples.

Isoelectronic species are atoms/ions that have the same number of electrons but different nuclear charges. They exhibit a clear trend: as Z increases, size decreases.

Examples (10-electron series): O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ — all 10 electrons (neon core).

Examples (18-electron series): S²⁻, Cl⁻, K⁺, Ca²⁺ — all 18 electrons (argon core).

Examples (2-electron series): He, Li⁺, Be²⁺ — all 2 electrons (helium core).

Q 3.31 Define periodic properties. Explain why they are called ‘periodic’.

Periodic properties are physical and chemical characteristics of elements that show a repeating pattern when elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number. Examples: atomic radius, ionization enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, electronegativity, metallic character. They are called ‘periodic’ because the same trends repeat in each period — the properties are a function of the electron configuration, which itself repeats (new shells, same sub-shell filling pattern) with each period. This periodicity is a direct consequence of the quantum mechanical structure of atoms: electronic configuration determines chemical behavior, and configurations follow a cyclical pattern governed by the aufbau principle.

Q 3.32 Account for the following: (a) IE of Na is less than that of Mg. (b) IE of Mg is greater than that of Al. (c) IE of Ar is greater than that of K.

(a) Na < Mg: Both in Period 3. Mg has one more proton (Z=12 vs Na=11) in the same shell, giving higher Zeff, so more energy is needed to remove an electron from Mg.

(b) Mg > Al: Mg has filled 3s² (stable), Al has 3s²3p¹ — the 3p electron is at higher energy and easier to remove. Despite Al having higher Z (+1), the 3p electron is better shielded.

(c) Ar > K: Ar has a stable octet configuration (3s²3p⁶) with very high IE (1520 kJ/mol). K is in Period 4, beginning the new shell with configuration [Ar]4s¹. The 4s electron is far from the nucleus, well shielded, and very easy to remove (419 kJ/mol).
