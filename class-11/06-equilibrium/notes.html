1. The Concept of Dynamic Equilibrium NCERT Core

Equilibrium represents a state in a reversible reaction where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction ($r_f = r_b$).

Dynamic Nature of Equilibrium
$$\text{At Equilibrium: } r_{\text{forward}} = r_{\text{backward}} \quad (\Delta G = 0)$$

Macroscopic properties (concentrations, pressure, temperature, density, color) remain constant over time, yet both opposing processes continue at equal rates.

Characteristics of Chemical Equilibrium

  • Attainable from either direction: Equilibrium can be achieved starting from reactants or from products in a closed system.
  • Dynamic in nature: Reaction does not stop; dynamic exchange of molecules persists indefinitely.
  • Closed system requirement: Essential so that volatile matter or energy is not permanently lost to surroundings.
  • Thermodynamic condition: $\Delta G = 0$ at equilibrium, where Gibbs free energy reaches an absolute minimum.
  • Catalyst effect: A catalyst speeds up forward and backward rates equally by lowering the activation energy barrier; it does not alter the position or numerical value of $K_c$.

2. Law of Mass Action & Equilibrium Constants ($K_c$ and $K_p$) High Yield

Proposed by Guldberg and Waage (1864): At a given temperature, the rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of active masses (molar concentrations) of the reacting species raised to powers equal to their stoichiometric coefficients.

For a general homogeneous gaseous equilibrium:

$$a\text{A}(g) + b\text{B}(g) \rightleftharpoons c\text{C}(g) + d\text{D}(g)$$
Equilibrium Expressions
$$K_c = \frac{[\text{C}]^c [\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a [\text{B}]^b} \quad\text{(in terms of molar concentrations)}$$ $$K_p = \frac{(P_{\text{C}})^c (P_{\text{D}})^d}{(P_{\text{A}})^a (P_{\text{B}})^b} \quad\text{(in terms of partial pressures in bar or atm)}$$
Derivation: Relation Between Kp and Kc ▼

Step-by-Step Proof of $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}$

Step 1: Ideal Gas Equation:
For an ideal gas, $PV = nRT \implies P = \left(\frac{n}{V}\right)RT = cRT$.
Thus, for any gas $i$: $P_i = [i]RT$.
Step 2: Substitute into $K_p$:
$$K_p = \frac{([\text{C}]RT)^c ([\text{D}]RT)^d}{([\text{A}]RT)^a ([\text{B}]RT)^b} = \frac{[\text{C}]^c [\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a [\text{B}]^b} \cdot \frac{(RT)^{c+d}}{(RT)^{a+b}}$$
Step 3: Define $\Delta n_g$:
$\Delta n_g = (c+d) - (a+b) = \sum n_{g(\text{products})} - \sum n_{g(\text{reactants})}$.
$$\mathbf{K_p = K_c (RT)^{\Delta n_g}}$$

Note: Use $R = 0.0831 \text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$ if pressure is in bar, or $R = 0.0821 \text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$ if pressure is in atm.

Three Critical Cases of $\Delta n_g$

Condition Relationship Example Reaction Pressure Effect on Yield
$\Delta n_g = 0$ $K_p = K_c$ (Dimensionless) $\text{H}_2(g) + \text{I}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{HI}(g)$ Pressure changes have no effect on equilibrium composition.
$\Delta n_g > 0$ $K_p > K_c$ (at $T > 12.2\text{ K}$) $\text{PCl}_5(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{PCl}_3(g) + \text{Cl}_2(g)$ Higher pressure shifts reaction backward (towards fewer gas moles).
$\Delta n_g < 0$ $K_p < K_c$ (at $T > 12.2\text{ K}$) $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$ Higher pressure shifts reaction forward (favors products).

3. Reaction Quotient ($Q$) & Predicting Reaction Direction

The Reaction Quotient ($Q_c$ or $Q_p$) has the same algebraic form as the equilibrium constant, but is evaluated using non-equilibrium (instantaneous) concentrations or partial pressures.

Condition Thermodynamic Meaning Reaction Direction
$Q_c < K_c$ Reactants are in excess; system must form more products. Shifts Forward ($\rightarrow$)
$Q_c = K_c$ Dynamic equilibrium achieved; rate of forward = rate of backward. At Equilibrium ($\rightleftharpoons$)
$Q_c > K_c$ Products are in excess; products consume to form reactants. Shifts Backward ($\leftarrow$)

Thermodynamics of Equilibrium ($\Delta G^\circ$ and $K$)

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q$$ $$\text{At equilibrium, } \Delta G = 0 \text{ and } Q = K \implies \mathbf{\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K = -2.303 RT \log_{10} K}$$

1. Le Chatelier's Principle CBSE Mandatory 3/5 Marks

"If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in concentration, pressure, volume, or temperature, the equilibrium shifts in such a direction as to undo or minimize the effect of the applied change."

1. Effect of Concentration

  • Adding a reactant or removing a product: Shifts equilibrium in the forward direction.
  • Adding a product or removing a reactant: Shifts equilibrium in the backward direction.
  • Note: Equilibrium constant $K_c$ remains completely unchanged!

2. Effect of Pressure / Volume

  • An increase in total external pressure (or decrease in volume) shifts equilibrium towards the side with fewer moles of gas.
  • A decrease in pressure shifts equilibrium towards the side with more moles of gas.
  • If $\Delta n_g = 0$, pressure change has no effect on the equilibrium state.

3. Effect of Temperature (Alters $K_{eq}$!)

Reaction Nature $\Delta H$ Effect of Increasing Temperature Effect on Equilibrium Constant ($K$)
Exothermic $\Delta H < 0$ (Heat released) Shifts Backward ($\leftarrow$) $K$ decreases as $T$ increases
Endothermic $\Delta H > 0$ (Heat absorbed) Shifts Forward ($\rightarrow$) $K$ increases as $T$ increases
Van 't Hoff Equation
$$\log_{10}\left(\frac{K_2}{K_1}\right) = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{2.303 R} \left[\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right]$$

4. Effect of Inert Gas Addition

  • At Constant Volume: Inert gas increases total pressure, but partial pressures and concentrations of reacting gases remain unchanged. $\implies$ No shift in equilibrium!
  • At Constant Pressure: Adding inert gas increases system volume, decreasing partial pressures of all gases. Equilibrium shifts towards the side with larger number of gaseous moles ($\Delta n_g$).

2. High-Yield Industrial Applications

A. Haber's Process for Ammonia Synthesis

$$\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g) \quad \Delta H = -92.4 \text{ kJ/mol} \quad (\Delta n_g = -2)$$
  • High Pressure (~200 atm): Favors forward reaction because $\Delta n_g = 2 - 4 = -2 < 0$.
  • Optimum Temperature (~700 K): Low temperature favors exothermic forward yield, but temperature must be moderate for acceptable reaction rate.
  • Catalyst: Finely divided Iron ($\text{Fe}$) with Molybdenum ($\text{Mo}$) or $\text{K}_2\text{O}/\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ promoter.
  • Continuous removal of $\text{NH}_3$: Liquefying and removing $\text{NH}_3$ continuously drives forward conversion.

B. Contact Process for $\text{SO}_3$ Synthesis

$$2\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3(g) \quad \Delta H = -196 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

Favored by high pressure (1–2 bar sufficient), optimum temperature (~720 K), and $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ catalyst.

1. Acid-Base Concepts & Autoionization of Water High Yield

Theory Acid Definition Base Definition Limitation / Example
Arrhenius Substance giving $\text{H}^+$ (or $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) in water Substance giving $\text{OH}^-$ in water Restricted to aqueous solutions only; cannot explain basicity of $\text{NH}_3$.

Lewis Electron pair acceptor (electrophile) Electron pair donor (nucleophile) Broadest concept: $\text{BF}_3, \text{AlCl}_3$ are Lewis acids; $\text{NH}_3, \text{H}_2\text{O}$ are Lewis bases.

Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

A pair of substances that differ by only one proton ($\text{H}^+$).

  • $\text{Conjugate Acid} = \text{Base} + \text{H}^+$ (e.g. Conjugate acid of $\text{NH}_3$ is $\text{NH}_4^+$)
  • $\text{Conjugate Base} = \text{Acid} - \text{H}^+$ (e.g. Conjugate base of $\text{HSO}_4^-$ is $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$)
  • Golden Rule: Strong acid has a weak conjugate base; weak acid has a strong conjugate base.

Ionic Product of Water ($K_w$)

Water undergoes auto-protolysis: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$

Auto-Ionization Constant
$$K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ at } 25^\circ\text{C } (298\text{ K})$$ $$\text{Since auto-ionization is endothermic, } K_w \text{ increases with temperature! (At } 90^\circ\text{C, } K_w \approx 10^{-12} \implies \text{neutral pH} = 6\text{)}$$

2. The pH Scale & Weak Electrolyte Dissociation

Logarithmic Definitions
$$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+] \qquad \text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$$ $$\mathbf{\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = \text{pK}_w = 14.0 \quad (\text{at } 25^\circ\text{C})}$$

Ostwald's Dilution Law (For Weak Monoprotic Acids)

For a weak acid $\text{HA} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{A}^-$ with initial concentration $C$ and degree of dissociation $\alpha$:

Derivation: Ostwald's Dilution Law ▼

Step-by-Step Proof

Initial: $[\text{HA}] = C, [\text{H}^+] = 0, [\text{A}^-] = 0$
Equilibrium: $[\text{HA}] = C(1-\alpha), [\text{H}^+] = C\alpha, [\text{A}^-] = C\alpha$
$$K_a = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]} = \frac{(C\alpha)(C\alpha)}{C(1-\alpha)} = \frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$$
For weak electrolytes, $\alpha \ll 1 \implies 1-\alpha \approx 1$:
$$K_a \approx C\alpha^2 \implies \mathbf{\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{C}}}$$ $$[\text{H}^+] = C\alpha = C\sqrt{\frac{K_a}{C}} = \mathbf{\sqrt{K_a \cdot C}}$$ $$\mathbf{\text{pH} = \frac{1}{2}[\text{p}K_a - \log_{10} C]}$$

1. Common Ion Effect & Buffer Solutions High Yield

Common Ion Effect: The suppression of the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte by the addition of a strong electrolyte containing a common ion. (e.g. Adding $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$ suppresses ionization of $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$).

Buffer Solutions

A solution that resists change in its pH upon the addition of small amounts of strong acid, strong base, or upon dilution.

3. Solubility Product Constant ($K_{sp}$) & Precipitation Criteria

For a sparingly soluble salt $\text{A}_x\text{B}_y(s) \rightleftharpoons x\text{A}^{y+}(aq) + y\text{B}^{x-}(aq)$ with molar solubility $S$ (mol/L):

General Solubility Product Expression
$$K_{sp} = [\text{A}^{y+}]^x [\text{B}^{x-}]^y = (xS)^x (yS)^y = \mathbf{x^x y^y S^{x+y}}$$
Salt Stoichiometry Example Dissociation $K_{sp}$ in terms of $S$ Solubility ($S$)
$1:1$ Type (AB) $\text{AgCl}, \text{BaSO}_4$ $\text{AB} \rightleftharpoons \text{A}^+ + \text{B}^-$ $K_{sp} = S^2$ $S = \sqrt{K_{sp}}$
$1:2$ Type ($\text{AB}_2$) $\text{PbCl}_2, \text{CaF}_2$ $\text{AB}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{A}^{2+} + 2\text{B}^-$ $K_{sp} = (S)(2S)^2 = 4S^3$ $S = \left(\frac{K_{sp}}{4}\right)^{1/3}$
$1:3$ Type ($\text{AB}_3$) $\text{Al(OH)}_3, \text{Fe(OH)}_3$ $\text{AB}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{A}^{3+} + 3\text{B}^-$ $K_{sp} = (S)(3S)^3 = 27S^4$ $S = \left(\frac{K_{sp}}{27}\right)^{1/4}$
$2:3$ Type ($\text{A}_2\text{B}_3$) $\text{As}_2\text{S}_3, \text{Bi}_2\text{S}_3$ $\text{A}_2\text{B}_3 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{A}^{3+} + 3\text{B}^{2-}$ $K_{sp} = (2S)^2(3S)^3 = 108S^5$ $S = \left(\frac{K_{sp}}{108}\right)^{1/5}$

Condition for Precipitation: Ionic Product ($Q_{sp}$) vs $K_{sp}$

  • $Q_{sp} < K_{sp}$ (Unsaturated solution): No precipitate forms; more solute can dissolve.
  • $Q_{sp} = K_{sp}$ (Saturated solution): Dynamic equilibrium between precipitate and dissolved ions.
  • $Q_{sp} > K_{sp}$ (Super-saturated solution): Precipitation occurs! Solid precipitates out until $Q_{sp} = K_{sp}$.

NCERT Example 6.1 Calculation of Equilibrium Constant (Kc) from Equilibrium Concentrations

Problem: The following concentrations were obtained for the formation of $\text{NH}_3$ from $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{H}_2$ at equilibrium at $500\text{ K}$: $[\text{N}_2] = 1.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$, $[\text{H}_2] = 3.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$, and $[\text{NH}_3] = 1.2 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$. Calculate the equilibrium constant $K_c$.

Balanced Equation: $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3(g)$
Equilibrium Law Expression: $$K_c = \frac{[\text{NH}_3]^2}{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3}$$
Substitution: $$K_c = \frac{(1.2 \times 10^{-2})^2}{(1.5 \times 10^{-2})(3.0 \times 10^{-2})^3} = \frac{1.44 \times 10^{-4}}{(1.5 \times 10^{-2})(2.7 \times 10^{-5})} = \frac{1.44 \times 10^{-4}}{4.05 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 355.55\text{ M}^{-2}$$
Final Answer: $K_c = 3.56 \times 10^2 \text{ L}^2\cdot\text{mol}^{-2}$ (or $356\text{ M}^{-2}$)
NCERT Example 6.2 Conversion Between Kc and Kp ($K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}$)

Problem: At $450\text{ K}$, $K_p = 2.0 \times 10^{10}\text{ bar}^{-1}$ for the reaction: $2\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3(g)$. What is $K_c$ at this temperature?

Calculate $\Delta n_g$: $$\Delta n_g = n_{\text{products}(g)} - n_{\text{reactants}(g)} = 2 - (2 + 1) = 2 - 3 = -1$$
Relate $K_p$ and $K_c$: $$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g} \implies K_c = K_p(RT)^{-\Delta n_g} = K_p(RT)^1$$ Using $R = 0.0831 \text{ L}\cdot\text{bar}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ and $T = 450\text{ K}$: $$K_c = (2.0 \times 10^{10} \text{ bar}^{-1}) \times (0.0831 \times 450 \text{ bar}\cdot\text{M}^{-1}) = 2.0 \times 10^{10} \times 37.395 \approx 7.48 \times 10^{11} \text{ M}^{-1}$$
Final Answer: $K_c = 7.48 \times 10^{11} \text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$
NCERT In-Text 6.3 Predicting Reaction Direction Using Reaction Quotient ($Q_c$)

Problem: The value of $K_c$ for the reaction $2\text{A} \rightleftharpoons \text{B} + \text{C}$ is $2 \times 10^{-3}$ at a given temperature. At a given time, the composition of the reaction mixture is $[\text{A}] = [\text{B}] = [\text{C}] = 3 \times 10^{-4}\text{ M}$. In which direction will the reaction proceed?

Write the Reaction Quotient ($Q_c$) Expression: $$Q_c = \frac{[\text{B}][\text{C}]}{[\text{A}]^2}$$
Calculate $Q_c$: $$Q_c = \frac{(3 \times 10^{-4}) \times (3 \times 10^{-4})}{(3 \times 10^{-4})^2} = \frac{9 \times 10^{-8}}{9 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.0$$
Comparison with $K_c$:
Here, $Q_c = 1.0$ and $K_c = 2 \times 10^{-3} = 0.002$.
Since $\mathbf{Q_c > K_c}$, products are in excess relative to equilibrium proportions.
Final Answer: The reaction will proceed in the backward (reverse) direction to reach equilibrium.
NCERT Exercise 6.8 ICE Table & Degree of Dissociation ($lpha$) of $\text{PCl}_5$

Problem: $13.8\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4$ was placed in a $1.0\text{ L}$ reaction vessel at $400\text{ K}$ and allowed to attain equilibrium: $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2(g)$. The total pressure at equilibrium was found to be $9.15\text{ bar}$. Calculate $K_c$, $K_p$, and partial pressures at equilibrium.

Initial Moles & Initial Pressure:
Molar mass of $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4 = 2(14) + 4(16) = 92 \text{ g/mol}$.
Initial moles $n_0 = \frac{13.8}{92} = 0.15 \text{ mol}$ in $V = 1.0\text{ L}$.
Initial pressure $P_0 = \frac{nRT}{V} = \frac{0.15 \times 0.0831 \times 400}{1.0} = 4.986 \approx 4.98\text{ bar}$.
ICE Pressure Analysis:
$\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2(g)$
Initial: $P_{\text{N}_2\text{O}_4} = P_0$, $P_{\text{NO}_2} = 0$
Equilibrium: $P_{\text{N}_2\text{O}_4} = P_0 - p$, $P_{\text{NO}_2} = 2p$
Total Pressure: $P_{\text{total}} = (P_0 - p) + 2p = P_0 + p = 9.15\text{ bar}$
$$p = 9.15 - P_0 = 9.15 - 4.98 = 4.17\text{ bar}$$
Equilibrium Partial Pressures & $K_p$:
$$P_{\text{N}_2\text{O}_4} = 4.98 - 4.17 = 0.81\text{ bar}$$ $$P_{\text{NO}_2} = 2(4.17) = 8.34\text{ bar}$$ $$K_p = \frac{(P_{\text{NO}_2})^2}{P_{\text{N}_2\text{O}_4}} = \frac{(8.34)^2}{0.81} = \frac{69.56}{0.81} = 85.87\text{ bar}$$
Final Answer: $P_{\text{N}_2\text{O}_4} = 0.81\text{ bar}$, $P_{\text{NO}_2} = 8.34\text{ bar}$, $K_p = 85.9\text{ bar}$, $K_c = \frac{K_p}{RT} = \frac{85.87}{0.0831 \times 400} = 2.58\text{ mol/L}$.
NCERT Exercise 6.12 Standard Gibbs Free Energy Change ($\Delta G^\circ$) and $K$

Problem: Calculate $\Delta G^\circ$ for conversion of oxygen to ozone, $3\text{O}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{O}_3(g)$ at $298\text{ K}$, if $K_p$ for this conversion is $2.47 \times 10^{-29}$.

Formula: $$\Delta G^\circ = -2.303 RT \log_{10} K_p$$
Calculation:
$R = 8.314 \text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, $T = 298\text{ K}$, $K_p = 2.47 \times 10^{-29}$
$$\log_{10}(2.47 \times 10^{-29}) = \log_{10}(2.47) - 29 = 0.3927 - 29 = -28.6073$$ $$\Delta G^\circ = -2.303 \times 8.314 \times 298 \times (-28.6073) = +163,228 \text{ J/mol}$$
Final Answer: $\Delta G^\circ = +163.2 \text{ kJ/mol}$ (A large positive $\Delta G^\circ$ confirms that the equilibrium lies almost completely on the reactant side).

Part B: Ionic Equilibrium Solutions

NCERT Classic Trap pH of Ultra-Dilute Strong Acid ($10^{-8}\text{ M HCl}$)

Problem: Calculate the pH of a $1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M } \text{HCl}$ aqueous solution at $25^\circ\text{C}$.

Common Error to Avoid: Directly calculating $\text{pH} = -\log(10^{-8}) = 8$ is completely incorrect! An acid solution can NEVER have a basic pH ($> 7$).
Because the acid is extremely dilute ($[\text{H}^+] \le 10^{-6}\text{ M}$), autoionization of water ($10^{-7}\text{ M}$) MUST be taken into account.
Equilibrium Setup:
Let $[\text{H}^+]$ from $\text{H}_2\text{O} = x$.
Total $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{H}^+]_{\text{HCl}} + [\text{H}^+]_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = (10^{-8} + x)\text{ M}$.
Total $[\text{OH}^-] = x\text{ M}$.
$$K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = (10^{-8} + x)(x) = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$$ $$x^2 + 10^{-8}x - 10^{-14} = 0$$
Solving Quadratic Equation:
$$x = \frac{-10^{-8} + \sqrt{(10^{-8})^2 - 4(1)(-10^{-14})}}{2} = \frac{-10^{-8} + \sqrt{10^{-16} + 4 \times 10^{-14}}}{2} \approx \frac{-0.01 \times 10^{-6} + 2.00025 \times 10^{-7}}{2} = 9.51 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M}$$ $$[\text{H}^+]_{\text{total}} = 10^{-8} + 9.51 \times 10^{-8} = 1.051 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.051 \times 10^{-7}) = 7 - \log_{10}(1.051) = 7 - 0.0216 \approx 6.98$$
Final Answer: $\text{pH} = 6.98$ (Slightly acidic, as physically expected for dilute acid).
NCERT Example 6.11 Ostwald's Dilution Law & Ionization of Weak Acid ($ ext{HF}$)

Problem: The ionization constant of $\text{HF}$ is $3.2 \times 10^{-4}$ at $298\text{ K}$. Calculate the degree of dissociation ($\alpha$) of $\text{HF}$ in its $0.02\text{ M}$ solution. Also calculate the concentration of all species ($[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$, $[\text{F}^-]$, and $[\text{HF}]$) at equilibrium, and its pH.

Equilibrium Expression: $\text{HF} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{F}^-$
$$K_a = \frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$$ Check approximate $\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{3.2 \times 10^{-4}}{0.02}} = \sqrt{0.016} = 0.126 = 12.6\%$.
Since $\alpha > 5\%$, the approximation $1-\alpha \approx 1$ is invalid; we must solve the exact quadratic equation:
$$0.02\alpha^2 + 3.2 \times 10^{-4}\alpha - 3.2 \times 10^{-4} = 0$$ $$\alpha^2 + 0.016\alpha - 0.016 = 0 \implies \alpha = \frac{-0.016 + \sqrt{(0.016)^2 + 4(0.016)}}{2} = \frac{-0.016 + 0.2535}{2} = 0.1187$$
Equilibrium Concentrations & pH:
$$[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = [\text{F}^-] = C\alpha = 0.02 \times 0.1187 = 2.37 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$ $$[\text{HF}] = C(1-\alpha) = 0.02(1 - 0.1187) = 1.76 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pH} = -\log(2.37 \times 10^{-3}) = 3 - \log(2.37) = 3 - 0.375 = 2.625 \approx 2.63$$
Final Answer: $\alpha = 0.119$ ($11.9\%$), $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+] = 2.37 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$, $[\text{HF}] = 1.76 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$, $\text{pH} = 2.63$.
NCERT Example 6.14 Common Ion Effect on Weak Base ($ ext{NH}_4 ext{OH} + ext{NH}_4 ext{Cl}$)

Problem: Calculate the $[\text{OH}^-]$ and pH of a solution containing $0.10\text{ M } \text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ and $0.10\text{ M } \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$. Given $K_b(\text{NH}_4\text{OH}) = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. Compare the $[\text{OH}^-]$ with that in $0.10\text{ M } \text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ alone.

Case 1: $0.10\text{ M } \text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ alone:
$$[\text{OH}^-] = \sqrt{K_b \cdot C} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.10} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pOH} = 2.87 \implies \text{pH} = 14 - 2.87 = 11.13$$
Case 2: In presence of common ion $\text{NH}_4^+$ from $0.10\text{ M } \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$:
$\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ dissociates completely: $[\text{NH}_4^+] \approx 0.10\text{ M}$.
$$K_b = \frac{[\text{NH}_4^+][\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{NH}_4\text{OH}]} \implies [\text{OH}^-] = K_b \times \frac{[\text{NH}_4\text{OH}]}{[\text{NH}_4^+]}$$ $$[\text{OH}^-] = 1.8 \times 10^{-5} \times \left(\frac{0.10}{0.10}\right) = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pOH} = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 5 - 0.255 = 4.745 \implies \mathbf{\text{pH} = 14 - 4.75 = 9.25}$$
Final Answer: Addition of $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ suppresses $[\text{OH}^-]$ by nearly 100-fold (from $1.34 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$ to $1.8 \times 10^{-5}\text{ M}$) due to the common ion effect, lowering pH from $11.13$ to $9.25$.
NCERT Exercise 6.22 Buffer Solution pH via Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

Problem: Calculate the pH of a buffer solution prepared by mixing $50\text{ mL}$ of $0.2\text{ M } \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ with $50\text{ mL}$ of $0.1\text{ M } \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$. Given $\text{p}K_a(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = 4.74$.

Molar Concentrations in Final Mixed Volume ($V = 100\text{ mL}$):
$$[\text{Acid}] = \frac{M_1 V_1}{V_{\text{total}}} = \frac{0.2 \times 50}{100} = 0.10\text{ M}$$ $$[\text{Salt}] = \frac{M_2 V_2}{V_{\text{total}}} = \frac{0.1 \times 50}{100} = 0.05\text{ M}$$
Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation: $$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log_{10}\left(\frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Acid}]}\right)$$ $$\text{pH} = 4.74 + \log_{10}\left(\frac{0.05}{0.10}\right) = 4.74 + \log_{10}(0.5) = 4.74 + (-0.3010) = 4.439$$
Final Answer: $\text{pH} = 4.44$
NCERT Example 6.18 Salt Hydrolysis: pH and Degree of Hydrolysis ($h$) of $ ext{CH}_3 ext{COONa}$

Problem: Calculate the degree of hydrolysis ($h$) and the pH of a $0.05\text{ M}$ sodium acetate ($\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$) solution at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Given $K_a(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.

Salt Nature: Salt of weak acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) and strong base ($\text{NaOH}$) $\implies$ Anionic Hydrolysis.
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^-$$ $$K_h = \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.56 \times 10^{-10}$$
Degree of Hydrolysis ($h$): $$h = \sqrt{\frac{K_h}{C}} = \sqrt{\frac{5.56 \times 10^{-10}}{0.05}} = \sqrt{1.112 \times 10^{-8}} = 1.054 \times 10^{-4} \approx 0.0105\%$$
pH of Weak Acid + Strong Base Salt: $$\text{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}\text{p}K_a + \frac{1}{2}\log_{10} C$$ $$\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.74$$ $$\log_{10} C = \log_{10}(0.05) = \log_{10}(5 \times 10^{-2}) = 0.699 - 2 = -1.301$$ $$\text{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}(4.74) + \frac{1}{2}(-1.301) = 7 + 2.37 - 0.6505 = 8.7195 \approx 8.72$$
Final Answer: $h = 1.05 \times 10^{-4}$ ($0.0105\%$), $\text{pH} = 8.72$ (Basic, $\text{pH} > 7$).
NCERT In-Text 6.14 Solubility Product ($K_{sp}$) in Pure Water vs. Common Ion Solution

Problem: The solubility product of $\text{AgCl}$ is $1.8 \times 10^{-10}$ at $298\text{ K}$.
(a) Calculate the solubility of $\text{AgCl}$ in pure water.
(b) Calculate its solubility in $0.10\text{ M } \text{NaCl}$ aqueous solution.

Part (a): In Pure Water (AB type salt):
$$\text{AgCl}(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$$ $$K_{sp} = S^2 \implies S = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.34 \times 10^{-5} \text{ mol/L}$$
Part (b): In $0.10\text{ M } \text{NaCl}$:
$\text{NaCl}$ fully dissociates: $[\text{Cl}^-] = 0.10\text{ M} + S' \approx 0.10\text{ M}$ (since $S' \ll 0.10$).
$$K_{sp} = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] = (S')(0.10) = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$$ $$S' = \frac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.10} = 1.8 \times 10^{-9} \text{ mol/L}$$
Final Answer:
(a) Solubility in pure water: $1.34 \times 10^{-5}\text{ mol/L}$
(b) Solubility in $0.10\text{ M } \text{NaCl}$: $1.8 \times 10^{-9}\text{ mol/L}$ (decreased by ~7,400 times due to common ion effect!).
NCERT Exercise 6.28 Precipitation Condition: Comparing $Q_{sp}$ with $K_{sp}$

Problem: Will precipitation occur if equal volumes of $0.002\text{ M } \text{AgNO}_3$ and $0.002\text{ M } \text{NaCl}$ are mixed? Given $K_{sp}(\text{AgCl}) = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$.

Effect of Mixing Equal Volumes:
When equal volumes ($V_1 = V_2$) are mixed, the total volume doubles ($V_{\text{total}} = 2V$), so all concentrations are halved:
$$[\text{Ag}^+] = \frac{0.002}{2} = 0.001\text{ M} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$ $$[\text{Cl}^-] = \frac{0.002}{2} = 0.001\text{ M} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$
Calculate Ionic Product ($Q_{sp}$): $$Q_{sp} = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] = (1.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$$
Comparison with $K_{sp}$:
$$Q_{sp} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \quad \text{vs} \quad K_{sp} = 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$$ Since $\mathbf{Q_{sp} > K_{sp}}$, the solution is super-saturated with respect to $\text{AgCl}$.
Final Answer: Yes, precipitation of $\text{AgCl}$ will occur immediately upon mixing.
