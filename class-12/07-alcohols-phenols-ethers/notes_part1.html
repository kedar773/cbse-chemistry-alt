1. Classification of Alcohols, Phenols & Ethers

Compounds containing C–O–H or C–O–C linkages are central to this chapter. They are classified based on (a) the number of –OH groups and (b) the hybridization of the carbon bearing the –OH.

1.1 Mono, Di, Tri & Poly-hydric

TypeExamplesNotes
MonohydricCH3OH, C2H5OH, C6H5OHOne –OH group
Dihydric (Glycols)Ethylene glycol HOCH2–CH2OH, Propylene glycolVicinal diols are common
Trihydric (Glycerols)Glycerol — CH2OH–CHOH–CH2OHThree –OH groups
PolyhydricSorbitol, Mannitol (sugar alcohols)Multiple –OH groups

1.2 Alcohols – based on carbon bearing –OH

ClassStructure of C bearing –OHExampleReactivity Note
Primary (1°) –CH2OH (C bonded to one other C) CH3CH2OH, CH3CH2CH2OH Oxidised to aldehyde → carboxylic acid
Secondary (2°) >CH–OH (C bonded to two other Cs) (CH3)2CHOH (isopropanol), Cyclohexanol Oxidised to ketone
Tertiary (3°) >C<–OH (C bonded to three other Cs) (CH3)3COH (tert-butyl alcohol) Resistant to oxidation; dehydrates readily

Example 11.1 📌 See Example 11.1 Classify the following as primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols: (i) CH3–CH2–CH(OH)–CH3, (ii) CH3–C(OH)(CH3)–CH2–CH3.

Solution
(i) Carbon bearing –OH is bonded to two carbons (CH3– and –CH2–CH3) → secondary (2°) alcohol.
(ii) Carbon bearing –OH is bonded to three carbons (CH3–, CH3–, –CH2CH3) → tertiary (3°) alcohol.

1.3 Allylic, Benzylic & Vinylic Alcohols

  • Allylic alcohol – –OH on the carbon next to a C=C, e.g. CH2=CH–CH2OH (prop-2-en-1-ol).
  • Benzylic alcohol – –OH on the carbon attached to a benzene ring, e.g. C6H5–CH2OH (benzyl alcohol / phenylmethanol).
  • Vinylic alcohol – –OH directly on a C=C carbon, e.g. CH2=CH–OH. These are unstable and tautomerise to carbonyls (enol ↔ keto).

1.4 Phenols

When –OH is attached directly to an aromatic (sp²) ring, the compound is a phenol. The C–OH carbon is sp² and the lone pair on O is delocalised into the ring → phenoxide is much more stable than alkoxide.

1.5 Ethers

Ethers have the general formula (R–O–R′) where R and R′ are alkyl or aryl. If both are alkyl → simple/alkyl ethers; if one is aryl → mixed (alkyl-aryl) ethers, e.g. C6H5–O–CH3 (anisole).

2. Nomenclature

2.1 Common (trivial) names of Alcohols

The alkyl group + the word "alcohol". Examples: methyl alcohol (CH3OH), ethyl alcohol (C2H5OH), isopropyl alcohol ((CH3)2CHOH), tert-butyl alcohol ((CH3)3COH), allyl alcohol (CH2=CHCH2OH), benzyl alcohol (C6H5CH2OH), cyclohexyl alcohol (C6H11OH).

2.2 IUPAC rules for Alcohols

  1. Select the longest C-chain containing the C bearing –OH.
  2. Number so that –OH gets the lowest possible locant.
  3. Replace the terminal -e of the parent alkane with -ol; precede by the locant, e.g. CH3CH2OH → ethan-1-ol; CH3CH(OH)CH3 → propan-2-ol.
  4. For diols → -diol; triols → -triol. Example: HOCH2CH2OH → ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol).
  5. For unsaturated alcohols, the suffix becomes -en-ol and –OH gets priority over the C=C in numbering. Example: CH2=CHCH2OH → prop-2-en-1-ol (allyl alcohol).

Solution

2.3 IUPAC names of Phenols

The simplest phenol is named phenol itself (C6H5OH). Substituted phenols are numbered starting from –OH (= position 1) and substituents cited in alphabetical order. Examples:

  • o-Cresol = 2-Methylphenol
  • m-Cresol = 3-Methylphenol
  • p-Cresol = 4-Methylphenol
  • 1,2,3-Benzenetriol = Pyrogallol

2.4 Common & IUPAC names of Ethers

Common system: name both alkyl groups alphabetically and add the word "ether". Example: CH3OC2H5 → ethyl methyl ether; C2H5OC2H5 → diethyl ether (often simply "ether").

IUPAC system: alkoxy-alkane. The smaller alkyl group becomes the alkoxy substituent on the larger parent alkane. Examples:

  • CH3OCH3 → methoxymethane (dimethyl ether)
  • CH3OC2H5 → methoxyethane (ethyl methyl ether)
  • C6H5OCH3 → methoxybenzene (anisole, methyl phenyl ether)
  • C6H5OC2H5 → ethoxybenzene (phenetole)

3. Structures of Functional Groups

3.1 Alcohols (C–O–H, sp³ C)

In alcohols, the C bonded to –OH is sp³ hybridised with a bond angle of ~109°. The O is also sp³ with two lone pairs. The C–O–H bond angle in methanol is 108.9° (close to tetrahedral). The O–H bond is polar (electronegativity O = 3.5, H = 2.1), making alcohols capable of strong intermolecular H-bonding.

3.2 Phenols (C–O–H, sp² C)

In phenol, the C attached to –OH is sp² hybridised. One of the lone pairs on O is delocalised into the aromatic π system, giving the C–O bond partial double bond character. The C–O–H bond angle is slightly larger than 109° (≈ 109° but with shorter C–O ≈ 136 pm vs. 141 pm in ethanol). This delocalisation is the root cause of phenol's enhanced acidity and ortho/para directing behaviour in EAS.

3.3 Ethers (C–O–C, sp³ O)

In ethers, the oxygen is sp³ hybridised with two lone pairs, giving a bent (angular) shape. C–O–C bond angle in dimethyl ether is 111.7° (close to tetrahedral). Because the oxygen carries no H, ethers cannot H-bond with themselves → very low boiling points compared to isomeric alcohols (e.g. dimethyl ether b.p. –24 °C vs. ethanol b.p. 78 °C). They can, however, accept H-bonds from O–H or N–H donors (good solvents for many organic reactions).

4. Alcohols – Preparation

Alcohols can be prepared from alkenes, carbonyl compounds (aldehydes/ketones/acids/esters), Grignard reagents, and by reduction of various functional groups. Key methods are summarised below.

4.1 From Alkenes (Hydration)

(a) Acid-catalysed Hydration (Markovnikov)

CH3–CH=CH2 + H2O (H+) → CH3–CH(OH)–CH3

Proceeds via carbocation intermediate (Markovnikov addition). Regiochemistry follows stability of the carbocation.

(b) Hydroboration–Oxidation (anti-Markovnikov)

CH3–CH=CH2 (i) BH3/THF   (ii) H2O2, OH– → CH3–CH2–CH2OH

Syn-addition, anti-Markovnikov — useful when the less-substituted alcohol is needed.

(c) Oxymercuration–Demercuration

R–CH=CH2 (i) Hg(OAc)2, H2O   (ii) NaBH4 → R–CH(OH)–CH3

Markovnikov alcohol without rearrangement (the mercurinium ion is a non-classical cation).

4.2 From Carbonyl Compounds by Reduction

SubstrateReagentProduct
Aldehyde (RCHO)NaBH4 / LiAlH4 or H2/Ni1° alcohol (RCH2OH)
Ketone (R2C=O)NaBH4 / LiAlH4 or H2/Ni2° alcohol (R2CHOH)
Carboxylic acid (RCOOH)LiAlH4 only (NaBH4 does not reduce)1° alcohol (RCH2OH)
Ester (RCOOR′)LiAlH41° alcohol (RCH2OH) + R′OH
Catalytic hydrogenation recap: Aldehydes and ketones reduce readily with H2/Ni, Pd or Pt; carboxylic acids and esters require the stronger LiAlH4. The Grignard method (next) is often preferred when more complex, branched alcohols are required.

4.4 Other Industrial Methods

  • From monosaccharides by fermentation: C6H12O6 zymase → 2 C2H5OH + 2 CO2.
  • Methanol by catalytic reduction of CO: CO + 2H2 CuO/ZnO, 573 K, 300 atm → CH3OH.
  • Ethanol from ethene: CH2=CH2 + H2O H+ → C2H5OH.
  • From haloalkanes by SN1/SN2 with aq. KOH or moist Ag2O (less common in NCERT).

5. Alcohols – Physical Properties

5.1 Boiling Point

Boiling points of alcohols are much higher than corresponding alkanes, haloalkanes or ethers because of strong intermolecular H-bonding. Among isomeric alcohols, b.p. order is 1° > 2° > 3° (more branched → less surface area → weaker London forces). Example: butan-1-ol (117.7 °C) > 2-methylpropan-2-ol (82 °C).

5.2 Solubility

Lower alcohols (up to ~C3) are completely miscible with water because they form H-bonds with H2O. As the alkyl chain grows, the hydrophobic part dominates → solubility drops sharply. Polyols (glycol, glycerol) are highly water-soluble because of multiple –OH groups.

5.3 Density & State

Lower alcohols are colourless liquids with a characteristic smell; methanol and ethanol are completely miscible with water. Alcohols are less dense than water; only polyhydric alcohols (glycerol, glycol) are denser.

6. Alcohols – Chemical Reactions

6.1 Acidic character – Reaction with active metals

2 R–OH + 2 Na → 2 R–O–Na+ + H2↑

Acid strength order: CH3OH > 1° > 2° > 3°. Increasing alkyl substitution pushes electron density onto O, destabilising the alkoxide. Phenol is a much stronger acid than alcohols (covered in Section 7).

6.2 Esterification (with carboxylic acids)

R–OH + R′–COOH conc. H2SO4 ⇄ R′–COOR + H2O

The reaction is reversible and acid-catalysed. Conc. H2SO4 protonates the carbonyl O, making the carbonyl C more electrophilic. 📌 See Exercise Q 11.5

6.3 Reaction with Hydrogen Halides (HX)

R–OH + H–X → R–X + H2O   (X = Cl, Br, I)

Reactivity order of HX: HI > HBr > HCl. Reactivity order of alcohol: 3° > 2° > 1° < CH3OH (for SN1); the reverse for SN2 (1° > 2°).

6.4 Reaction with Phosphorus Halides

ReagentProductUse

6.5 Dehydration to Alkenes

R–CH2–CH(OH)–R′ conc. H2SO4 or H3PO4, 413 K → R–CH=CH–R′ + H2O

Order of ease: 3° > 2° > 1° (E1 mechanism via carbocation). Saytzeff's rule: the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product. At lower temperature (413 K) intermolecular dehydration gives an ether; at higher temperature (~443 K) intramolecular dehydration gives the alkene.

Acid-catalysed dehydration (E1) of 2°/3° alcohols Step 1: Protonation of –OH by H2SO4 → R–OH2+ Step 2: Loss of H2O → carbocation R+ Step 3: Loss of β-H by base (HSO4–) → alkene (Concerted E2 also possible with 1° alcohols.)

6.6 Oxidation

2° (R2CHOH)Same oxidantsKetone

Chromic acid test (Jones): a 2° alcohol or 1° alcohol turns orange K2Cr2O7/H2SO4 green (Cr3+) within seconds; 3° alcohols do not react.

RCH2OH K2Cr2O7/H2SO4 → RCHO [O] → RCOOH

6.7 The Lucas Test

Conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2 = Lucas reagent. Used to distinguish 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols by the rate of appearance of turbidity (formation of insoluble alkyl chloride):

  • 3° alcohol: immediate cloudiness (SN1, fastest).
  • 2° alcohol: cloudiness in ~5 min (SN1).
  • 1° alcohol: no turbidity at room temperature; reacts only on heating (SN2).

Example 11.3 📌 See Example 11.3 Write the names of reagents and equations for the preparation of phenol from cumene.

Solution
The Cumene process (Hock process):
Cumene (isopropylbenzene) + O2 air, cumene hydroperoxidase → cumene hydroperoxide.

This is the industrial method of choice because both products are valuable.

In-Text Q 11.4 📌 See In-Text Q 11.4 Give the IUPAC names of: (i) CH3CH2–O–CH2CH2CH3 (ii) (CH3)2CH–O–CH2CH2CH3

Solution
(i) Ethoxypropane (larger alkyl = propane; smaller as substituent = ethoxy).
(ii) 2-Methylpropan-1-ol? No — it's an ether: 2-methyl-1-propoxypropane → 2-Methyl-1-propoxypropane (i.e., the parent chain is propane bearing the –O–CH2CH2CH3 substituent at C-1 and a CH3 at C-2). Simplest accepted: 1-propoxy-2-methylpropane / propoxy-isobutane (common).

Exercise Q 11.4 📌 See Exercise Q 11.4 How will you prepare: (a) 1-Phenylethanol from a suitable alkene (b) 2-Methyl-1-propanol from a suitable aldehyde.

Solution
(a) 1-Phenylethanol = C6H5–CH(OH)–CH3. Use Markovnikov hydration of styrene: C6H5–CH=CH2 + H2O H+ → C6H5–CH(OH)–CH3.
(b) 2-Methyl-1-propanol = (CH3)2CH–CH2OH. Reduce isobutyraldehyde (2-methylpropanal): (CH3)2CH–CHO NaBH4 or H2/Ni → (CH3)2CH–CH2OH.
