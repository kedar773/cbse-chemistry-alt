7. Phenols – Preparation, Properties & Reactions

Phenols are compounds in which the –OH group is directly attached to an aromatic (sp²) carbon. The lone pair on oxygen is delocalised into the benzene ring, which makes phenols significantly more acidic than alcohols and gives them unique reactivity.

7.1 Preparation of Phenols

(a) From Chlorobenzene – Dow's Process

C6H5Cl + NaOH 300 °C, 300 atm (or aq. NaOH, 623 K, 150 atm) → C6H5ONa + HCl
C6H5ONa + HCl → C6H5OH + NaCl

The reaction proceeds via benzyne (aryne) intermediate at high temperature/pressure. 📌 See In-Text Q 11.5

(b) From Benzene Sulphonic Acid

C6H6 + conc. H2SO4 → C6H5SO3H  (benzene sulphonic acid)
C6H5SO3H + NaOH fusion, 573 K → C6H5ONa + Na2SO3
C6H5ONa + HCl → C6H5OH

(c) From Diazonium Salts – Hydrolysis

C6H5NH2 NaNO2 + HCl, 273–278 K → C6H5N2+Cl–
C6H5N2+Cl– + H2O warm → C6H5OH + N2 + HCl

(d) From Cumene – Cumene (Hock) Process

C6H5CH(CH3)2 + O2 air, cumene hydroperoxidase → C6H5C(CH3)2OOH (cumene hydroperoxide)
C6H5C(CH3)2OOH H3O+, Δ → C6H5OH + (CH3)2C=O (acetone)

Industrial method; phenol + acetone are co-produced.

(e) From Sodium Benzene Sulphonate (Lab Method)

Same as (b) above — NaOH fusion gives sodium phenoxide, acidification gives phenol.

(f) Rosenmund–von Braun / Grignard Route

C6H5MgBr + O2 then H3O+ (or via trialkyl borate). Less commonly taught in NCERT Class 12.

7.2 Physical Properties of Phenols

  • Phenol is a colourless, crystalline solid (m.p. 42 °C) with a characteristic "carbolic" smell. It turns pink on exposure to air (oxidation).
  • Boiling point: 182 °C — much higher than corresponding aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g. toluene b.p. 110 °C) due to intermolecular H-bonding.
  • Solubility: Phenol is sparingly soluble in cold water but moderately soluble in hot water. It is highly soluble in organic solvents like ether, ethanol, benzene.
  • Polarity: Phenol is more polar than alcohol because the C–O bond has partial double-bond character (C–O bond length in phenol ≈ 136 pm vs. 141 pm in ethanol).

7.3 Acidity of Phenols

Phenols are much more acidic than alcohols because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised — the negative charge on O is delocalised over the ortho and para positions of the ring.

Resonance stabilisation of phenoxide ion C6H5O– ⇌ charge on ortho-C ⇌ charge on para-C (3 resonance structures – ortho, ortho, para – plus the original on O) This delocalisation lowers the energy of the conjugate base, making the equilibrium PhOH ⇌ PhO– + H+ lie further right.
CompoundpKaAcid strength comment
Ethanol (CH3CH2OH)~16Alkoxide is not resonance-stabilised → weak acid
Phenol (C6H5OH)10.0~106 × stronger than ethanol
o-Nitrophenol7.2Strongest: –NO2 at ortho stabilises anion via H-bonding + –I
m-Nitrophenol8.4Only –I effect (no resonance)
p-Nitrophenol7.1Strongest via resonance stabilisation of anion
2,4,6-Trinitrophenol (picric acid)0.38Very strong acid; comparable to mineral acids
Order of acidity: Picric acid > p-Nitrophenol > o-Nitrophenol > m-Nitrophenol > Phenol > H2O > ROH (ethanol etc.). EDG (–CH3, –OCH3, –NH2) at ortho/para decreases phenol acidity; EWG (–NO2, –CN, –CHO, –X) at ortho/para increases acidity.

Phenols react with NaOH (water-soluble salts) but alcohols do not — useful distinction test.

C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5ONa + H2O

But phenols do not liberate CO2 from NaHCO3 (carbonic acid is stronger than phenol), so they are weaker than carbonic acid but stronger than alcohols and water.

7.4 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) in Phenols

The –OH group is a powerful ortho/para director and activator (through resonance donation from the oxygen lone pair). Reactions are much faster than in benzene, and milder conditions can be used.

(a) Nitration

C6H5OH + dil. HNO3 → o-Nitrophenol + p-Nitrophenol

With conc. HNO3 (excess) → 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid). 📌 See In-Text Q 11.6

(b) Halogenation

C6H5OH + 3 Br2 (aq.) → 2,4,6-Tribromophenol (white ppt) + 3 HBr

This is a sensitive test for phenol — the white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol confirms the presence of phenol (no catalyst needed; –OH is strongly activating).

With limited Br2 (e.g. in CS2 or non-polar solvent, low T) → p-bromophenol as major product.

(c) Sulphonation

C6H5OH + conc. H2SO4 → o-Phenolsulphonic acid + p-Phenolsulphonic acid

(d) Friedel–Crafts Alkylation / Acylation

AlCl3 cannot be used with phenol (it complexes with –OH). Use alkene/HF or modified conditions. Less relevant in NCERT Class 12.

7.5 Named Reactions of Phenols

(i) Kolbe's Reaction (Kolbe–Schmitt)

C6H5ONa + CO2 393 K, 4–7 atm (pressure) → o-HOC6H4COONa (sodium salicylate)
o-HOC6H4COONa + HCl → o-HOC6H4COOH (salicylic acid)

With NaOH + CO2 → sodium salicylate → acidification gives salicylic acid. Aspirin synthesis uses this.

Note: With KOH instead of NaOH, the major product is p-hydroxybenzoic acid.

(ii) Reimer–Tiemann Reaction

C6H5OH + CHCl3 + aq. NaOH Δ, 340 K → o-HOC6H4CHO (salicylaldehyde) + NaCl + H2O

Forms the ortho-hydroxy benzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde) as the major product, plus a small amount of the para isomer.

Reimer–Tiemann mechanism (key steps) Step 1: NaOH + CHCl3 → :CCl2 (dichlorocarbene) + NaCl + H2O CHCl3 + OH– ⇌ CCl3– + H2O → :CCl2 + Cl– Step 2: Phenoxide attacks :CCl2 (electrophile) at ortho/para positions. Step 3: Loss of Cl– gives an ortho-quinonoid intermediate. Step 4: Hydrolysis of the –CHCl2 group (in H2O) → –CHO.

(iii) Fries Rearrangement

C6H5OH + CH3COCl anhyd. AlCl3 → C6H5OCOCH3 (phenyl acetate)
C6H5OCOCH3 anhyd. AlCl3, Δ (Fries) → o-HOC6H4COCH3 + p-HOC6H4COCH3

Low temperature (room T) → para is the major product; high temperature (≈ 160 °C) → ortho dominates.

(iv) Claisen Rearrangement

C6H5OCH2CH=CH2 (allyl phenyl ether) Δ, 200 °C → o-HOC6H4CH2CH=CH2 (o-allylphenol)

Pericyclic [3,3]-sigmatropic rearrangement. The allyl group migrates from O to the ortho C of the ring via a 6-membered cyclic transition state. With both ortho positions blocked, the allyl group migrates to the para position (a second Claisen). 📌 See Exercise Q 11.12

(v) Williamson Ether Synthesis (covered fully in Ethers, §8.2)

Phenol + NaOH → phenoxide; phenoxide + R–X (1°) → aryl alkyl ether.

7.6 Oxidation of Phenols

Phenols are easily oxidised by air (→ pink colour), by K2Cr2O7 (→ quinone), and by HNO3. Oxidation of phenol with Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4 gives p-benzoquinone (1,4-benzoquinone).

Example 11.4 📌 See Example 11.4 Show how will you synthesise: (i) 1-Phenylethanol from a suitable alkene (ii) Cyclohexylmethanol from an aryl halide.

Solution
(i) 1-Phenylethanol = C6H5–CH(OH)CH3 (Markovnikov). From styrene via acid-catalysed hydration:
C6H5–CH=CH2 + H2O H+ → C6H5–CH(OH)–CH3
(ii) Cyclohexylmethanol = C6H11–CH2OH. The aryl halide here is chlorobenzene — use the Grignard method with HCHO (formaldehyde):
C6H5Cl + Mg dry ether → C6H5MgCl
C6H5MgCl + HCHO → C6H5CH2OMgCl
C6H5CH2OMgCl + H3O+ → C6H5CH2OH (benzyl alcohol, NOT cyclohexylmethanol)
NCERT intended interpretation: When C6H5Cl is converted to C6H5MgCl and then reacted with cyclohexanone (C6H10=O), the product after hydrolysis is 1-phenylcyclohexanol — a tertiary alcohol. For cyclohexylmethanol specifically, the correct alkene would be cyclohexylmethene (methylenecyclohexane) hydrated, or Friedel-Crafts of benzene with cyclohexyl chloride (giving cyclohexylbenzene) and then side-chain oxidation, or hydroboration–oxidation of methylenecyclohexane. The NCERT answer takes the Grignard route: chlorobenzene → phenylmagnesium chloride → formaldehyde → benzyl alcohol. Cyclohexylmethanol is best prepared by hydrogenation of benzyl alcohol: C6H5CH2OH + 3 H2 Raney Ni, high T/P → C6H11CH2OH.

Example 11.5 📌 See Example 11.5 Give the IUPAC names of the following ethers: (i) CH3–O–CH2–CH(CH3)–CH3 (ii) C6H5–O–CH2CH2CH3

Solution
(i) Smaller alkyl: methyl. Larger parent chain: butane (CH2–CH(CH3)–CH3 = isobutane fragment, 2-methylpropane). IUPAC = 1-Methoxy-2-methylpropane.
(ii) Smaller: phenyl as phenoxy substituent. Parent: propane. IUPAC = 1-Phenoxypropane (or, using IUPAC's preferred "phenyl-O-", the older name "propoxybenzene" is also accepted).

Exercise Q 11.6 📌 See Exercise Q 11.6 Write the equations for the following reactions: (a) Phenol + Br2 (aq.) (b) Phenol + CHCl3 + NaOH (c) Phenol + conc. H2SO4 (d) Phenol + NaOH.

Solution

(b) Reimer–Tiemann: C6H5OH + CHCl3 + 4 NaOH → salicylaldehyde (o-HOC6H4CHO) + 3 NaCl + 2 H2O.
(c) C6H5OH + conc. H2SO4 → o-phenolsulphonic acid + p-phenolsulphonic acid.
(d) C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5ONa (sodium phenoxide) + H2O.

Exercise Q 11.7 📌 See Exercise Q 11.7 Predict the products of the following reactions: (i) Kolbe's reaction on p-cresol (ii) Reimer–Tiemann on p-cresol.

Solution
(i) p-Cresol is 4-methylphenol. The –OH activates ortho positions (2 and 6). The position 2 is ortho to –OH and ortho to –CH3; position 6 is equivalent. Major product: 2-Hydroxy-5-methylbenzoic acid (i.e., 2-hydroxy-5-methylbenzene-1-carboxylic acid, formed by CO2 entering at C-2).
(ii) Reimer–Tiemann introduces –CHO at the position ortho to –OH. Position 2 (between –OH and –CH3) is preferred. Major product: 2-Hydroxy-5-methylbenzaldehyde.

Exercise Q 11.8 📌 See Exercise Q 11.8 Explain the following with an example: (i) Kolbe's reaction (ii) Reimer–Tiemann reaction (iii) Williamson ether synthesis (iv) Fries rearrangement (v) Claisen rearrangement.

Solution
(i) Kolbe's: Sodium phenoxide + CO2 at 4–7 atm → sodium salicylate → acidification → salicylic acid. Used industrially to make aspirin.
(ii) Reimer–Tiemann: Phenol + CHCl3 + aq. NaOH → salicylaldehyde. The electrophile is :CCl2 (dichlorocarbene).
(iii) Williamson: Sodium alkoxide (or phenoxide) + R–X (preferably 1°) → ether. Example: CH3CH2ONa + CH3Br → CH3CH2OCH3.
(iv) Fries: Phenyl acetate + anhyd. AlCl3 + Δ → hydroxyacetophenone (ortho + para).
(v) Claisen: Allyl phenyl ether on heating to 200 °C → o-allylphenol via a 6-membered pericyclic transition state.

8. Ethers – Preparation, Properties & Reactions

Ethers are compounds of the type R–O–R′ where R and R′ may be alkyl or aryl. They are widely used as solvents (diethyl ether, THF, 1,4-dioxane) and as protecting groups in synthesis.

8.1 Nomenclature Recap

Common: alkyl alkyl ether (smaller alkyl first). IUPAC: alkoxyalkane (smaller group as alkoxy substituent on the larger parent alkane). For aromatic ethers: alkoxybenzene or "phenoxy" if aryl is the substituent.

  • CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 → ethoxyethane (diethyl ether)
  • C6H5–O–CH3 → methoxybenzene (anisole)
  • C6H5–O–C6H5 → diphenyl ether

8.2 Preparation of Ethers

(a) Williamson Ether Synthesis

R–ONa (sodium alkoxide / phenoxide) + R′–X → R–O–R′ + NaX

Best with 1° alkyl halides (SN2). Avoid 3° alkyl halides (E2 dominates).

C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5ONa + H2O
C6H5ONa + CH3Br → C6H5–O–CH3 (anisole) + NaBr

The phenoxide must attack a 1° alkyl halide, not the other way round (aryl halides don't undergo SN2 with alkoxide).

(b) Acid-Catalysed Dehydration of Alcohols (Bimolecular)

2 CH3CH2OH conc. H2SO4, 413 K → CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 + H2O

Best for symmetrical primary ethers. 2° and 3° alcohols give alkenes (intramolecular dehydration dominates at high T).

(c) Alkoxymercuration–Demercuration (Modern Method)

R–CH=CH2 (i) Hg(OAc)2, R′OH   (ii) NaBH4 → R–CH(OCH2R′)–CH3

Equivalent of Markovnikov addition of ROH across the double bond, but no rearrangement (cf. oxymercuration with H2O).

(d) From Alkyl Halides and Dry Ag2O (Williamson variant)

2 R–X + Ag2O dry, Δ → R–O–R + 2 AgX

8.3 Physical Properties of Ethers

  • Polarity: Ethers are polar (C–O–C bond angle ≈ 110°, dipole moment of diethyl ether ≈ 1.18 D) but cannot form H-bonds with each other (no O–H). They can accept H-bonds from O–H or N–H donors.
  • Boiling point: Close to that of alkanes of similar mass; much lower than isomeric alcohols. Example: diethyl ether b.p. 35 °C vs. n-butanol b.p. 117 °C.
  • Solubility: Lower ethers (up to C4) are appreciably soluble in water because the O atom forms H-bonds with H2O.
  • Density: Lighter than water (typical 0.7–0.8 g/cm³).
  • Volatility: Highly flammable; diethyl ether is dangerous due to peroxide formation on standing.

8.4 Chemical Reactions of Ethers

(a) Cleavage by Hydrogen Halides

R–O–R′ + HX → R–X + R′–OH (or further R′X if R′ can be protonated again)

Order of reactivity: HI > HBr > HCl. The mechanism is SN2-like for unhindered ethers and SN1 for benzylic/3°.

Cleavage of an ether by HI (SN2) Step 1: Protonation of the ether O by HI: R–O–R′ + HI → R–O+(H)–R′ + I– Step 2: I– attacks the smaller (less hindered) alkyl group in an SN2 fashion: R–O+(H)–R′ + I– → R–OH + R′–I (The R′–I is produced because R′ is attacked; OH stays on R.) Step 3 (if R′ is also a good leaving group): a second mole of HI can convert R–OH → R–I.

Cleavage of mixed ethers (Mechanism determines products):

CH3–O–C2H5 + HI (excess) Δ → CH3I + C2H5OH (with 1 mol HI, only alkyl-iodide formed; the larger group retains OH)
Further: C2H5OH + HI → C2H5I + H2O (only with excess HI + heat)

When one group is methyl and the other is bulky/aryl, methyl iodide is preferentially formed. For tert-butyl ethers, the cleavage gives the 3° alkyl iodide (SN1).

Anisole + HI: Always gives CH3I + C6H5OH (the methyl–O bond is cleaved, not the aryl–O bond, because the aryl–O bond has partial double-bond character and doesn't allow SN2).

(b) Electrophilic Substitution in Aromatic Ethers (Anisole)

The –OCH3 (or –OR) group is a strong ortho/para director and activator (oxygen lone pair donates into the ring). Reactions occur under milder conditions than in benzene.

ReactionReagentProduct(s)
HalogenationBr2 / CH3COOH (or FeBr3)o-Bromoanisole + p-bromoanisole (major)
Nitrationconc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4o-Nitroanisole + p-nitroanisole (major)

(c) Claisen Rearrangement (covered in §7.5 iv)

Allyl aryl ethers undergo Claisen rearrangement on heating to give o-allylphenols.

(d) Peroxide Formation

CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 + O2 (air) light, prolonged standing → CH3CH(–OOH)–O–CH2CH3 (ether peroxide)

Explosive on distillation! Ethers are stored over anhydrous FeSO4 or in dark bottles to prevent peroxide formation.

(e) Reaction with Lewis Acids

(C2H5)2O + BF3 → (C2H5)2O·BF3 (a coordinate complex)

Explains why ethers are good solvents for many Lewis acid catalysed reactions (e.g. BF3 + ether = a useful catalyst).

8.5 Why ethers are used as solvents

  • Inert to most reagents (bases, reducing agents, nucleophiles).
  • Polar enough to dissolve organic substrates.
  • Low boiling point → easy to remove.
  • Coordinate with Lewis acids (e.g. BF3, MgBr2) without being destroyed.

Example 11.6 📌 See Example 11.6 Write the mechanism of the reaction of HI with methoxymethane.

Solution
CH3–O–CH3 + H–I → CH3–O+(H)–CH3 + I– (protonation) I– attacks one CH3 in an SN2 step: CH3–O+(H)–CH3 + I– → CH3–OH + CH3–I Net: CH3OCH3 + HI → CH3OH + CH3I With excess HI: CH3OH + HI → CH3I + H2O (another SN2).

Example 11.7 📌 See Example 11.7 The following reaction takes place in two steps, one in acidic medium and other in alkaline medium. Explain with mechanism: Allyl chloride treated with 1 mole of Hg(OAc)2 in methanol / H2O forms an ether of the type CH2=CH–CH2–O–CH3.

Solution
This is alkoxymercuration–demercuration:
CH2=CH–CH2Cl Hg(OAc)2, CH3OH → CH2(OCH3)–CH(HgOAc)–CH2Cl
NaBH4 → CH2=CH–CH2–OCH3 (allyl methyl ether, Markovnikov-type addition of CH3OH across the double bond without rearrangement, with retention of the allyl-Cl skeleton after demetalation).
Note: For NCERT Class 12, the simpler statement: alkoxymercuration gives the Markovnikov ether without rearrangement.

Example 11.8 📌 See Example 11.8 Draw the structure of the major alkene formed by β-elimination of 2-methoxy-2-methylbutane.

Solution
2-Methoxy-2-methylbutane = CH3CH2–C(OCH3)(CH3)–CH3. Acid-catalysed E1 first protonates and cleaves the ether to give the 3° alcohol: CH3CH2–C(OH)(CH3)–CH3 (2-methylbutan-2-ol). Dehydration (E1) follows Saytzeff's rule: removes the H from the CH2 (more substituted alkene) to give 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major product: (CH3)2C=CHCH3.

In-Text Q 11.5 📌 See In-Text Q 11.5 Write the reaction involved in the preparation of phenol from chlorobenzene.

Solution
C6H5Cl + NaOH (aq.) 623 K, 150 atm (or 300 °C, 300 atm) → C6H5ONa + HCl
C6H5ONa + HCl → C6H5OH + NaCl
This is the Dow's process. The reaction proceeds via a benzyne (aryne) intermediate: NaOH removes the ortho-H to give a benzyne, which then captures OH– at either of the two carbons of the triple bond.

In-Text Q 11.6 📌 See In-Text Q 11.6 Phenol is acidic. Why does it not react with NaHCO3 to release CO2?

Solution
Although phenol is acidic (pKa ≈ 10), it is weaker than carbonic acid (pKa1 ≈ 6.35). For a salt-formation to occur, the acid must be stronger than the conjugate acid of the base. Since H2CO3 is stronger than C6H5OH, phenol does not displace CO2 from NaHCO3. It does, however, react with NaOH (a stronger base than HCO3–) to give C6H5ONa.

In-Text Q 11.8 📌 See In-Text Q 11.8 Write the mechanism of the reaction of HI with ethoxybenzene (phenetole).

Solution
Step 1: Protonation of O by HI: C6H5–O+(H)–C2H5 + I– Step 2: SN2 attack by I– on the ethyl C (aryl C–O bond cannot be cleaved by SN2 because the sp² C of the aromatic ring is a poor substrate for backside attack): C6H5–OH + C2H5–I Net: C6H5OC2H5 + HI → C6H5OH + C2H5I

In-Text Q 11.10 📌 See In-Text Q 11.10 Explain why phenol is more acidic than ethanol.

Solution
In phenol, the conjugate base (phenoxide) is resonance-stabilised: the negative charge on oxygen is delocalised onto the ortho and para carbons of the ring. In ethanol, the conjugate base (ethoxide) has no such stabilisation. The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid. Therefore phenol (pKa ≈ 10) is much more acidic than ethanol (pKa ≈ 16). Additionally, the sp² C of phenol with partial C=O character withdraws electron density, stabilising the phenoxide further.

In-Text Q 11.11 📌 See In-Text Q 11.11 Name the products of the following reactions and arrange them in increasing order of their acidic strength: (a) C6H5OH (b) o-Cresol (c) 2,6-Dinitrophenol (d) 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol.

Solution
(a) Phenol (pKa = 10.0)
(b) 2-Methylphenol / o-cresol (pKa = 10.2). –CH3 is electron-donating → decreases acidity.
(c) 2,6-Dinitrophenol (pKa ≈ 3.96). Two –NO2 groups strongly withdraw electrons → more acidic.
(d) 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol / picric acid (pKa = 0.38). Three –NO2 groups → strongest acid.
Order (increasing acidity): o-cresol < phenol < 2,6-dinitrophenol < picric acid.

Exercise Q 11.9 📌 See Exercise Q 11.9 Ethers can be prepared by: (i) Williamson synthesis (ii) Dehydration of alcohols (iii) Hydroboration–oxidation. Which of these are applicable to the preparation of unsymmetrical ethers?

Answer
(i) Williamson synthesis: Yes (best method for unsymmetrical ethers; SN2 on 1° alkyl halide).
(ii) Dehydration: No (gives a mixture of three ethers — R–O–R, R′–O–R′, R–O–R′ — and is not selective).
(iii) Hydroboration–oxidation: No (gives alcohols, not ethers).
Therefore, only Williamson synthesis is suitable for unsymmetrical ethers.

Exercise Q 11.10 📌 See Exercise Q 11.10 Why is the C–O–H bond angle in alcohols slightly less than the tetrahedral angle, while the C–O–C bond angle in ethers is slightly greater?

Solution
The C–O–H bond angle (~108.5° in methanol) is less than the ideal tetrahedral angle (109.5°) because the two lone pairs on oxygen repel the bonding pairs more strongly, narrowing the C–O–H angle.
The C–O–C bond angle in ethers (≈ 111.7° in dimethyl ether) is greater than 109.5° because the two bulky alkyl groups push each other away (steric repulsion), opening up the angle. The lone-pair–lone-pair repulsion in ethers is also reduced by the angle opening.

Exercise Q 11.11 📌 See Exercise Q 11.11 Explain why diethyl ether boils at 35 °C while n-butanol boils at 117 °C, although both have the same molecular formula C4H10O.

Solution
Although both compounds have the same molecular formula (C4H10O, isomers), n-butanol (CH3CH2CH2CH2OH) has an O–H group and undergoes strong intermolecular H-bonding in the liquid state, requiring a lot of energy to break. Diethyl ether (CH3CH2OCH2CH3) has no O–H and cannot H-bond with itself — only weak dipole–dipole and dispersion forces hold its molecules together. Hence the much lower boiling point of ether (35 °C) compared to n-butanol (117 °C).

Exercise Q 11.15 📌 See Exercise Q 11.15 Write the structures of all the possible isomeric alcohols of molecular formula C5H10O. Which of these isomers is more stable? Why?

Solution
C5H10O corresponds to one degree of unsaturation → either a ring or a double bond.
Open-chain unsaturated alcohols (C=C + OH):
  • CH2=CH–CH2CH2CH2OH (pent-4-en-1-ol)
  • CH2=CH–CH2CH(OH)CH3 (pent-4-en-2-ol)
  • CH2=CH–CH(OH)CH2CH3 (pent-1-en-3-ol)
  • CH2=C(CH3)–CH2CH2OH (2-methylbut-3-en-1-ol)
  • CH3–CH=CH–CH(OH)CH3 (pent-3-en-2-ol)
  • CH3–CH=CH–CH2CH2OH (pent-3-en-1-ol)
  • CH3–CH=C(CH3)CH2OH (2-methylbut-2-en-1-ol)
  • CH3–C(OH)=CH–CH2CH3 (pent-2-en-2-ol, enol form — unstable, tautomerises to pentan-2-one)
  • (CH3)2C=CH–CH2OH (3-methylbut-2-en-1-ol, prenol)
Cyclic alcohols (saturated ring + OH):
  • Cyclopentanol
  • 2-Methylcyclobutanol, 3-methylcyclobutanol
  • 1-Methylcyclobutanol
  • Cyclopropylmethanol, (cyclopropyl)carbinol variants
NCERT lists 7 open-chain isomers and the cyclic cyclopentanol & methylcyclobutanols.
Most stable: The isomer with the most substituted alkene and the least strained ring is most stable. 3-Methylbut-2-en-1-ol (prenol) has a trisubstituted C=C in an open chain — among the open-chain isomers, this is the most thermodynamically stable. Among cyclic isomers, cyclopentanol is the most stable (no ring strain; five-membered ring).

Exercise Q 11.16 📌 See Exercise Q 11.16 Name the reagents for the following reactions: (a) Oxidation of a primary alcohol to aldehyde (b) Oxidation of a primary alcohol to carboxylic acid (c) Dehydration of an alcohol to alkene (d) Butan-2-one to butan-2-ol.

Solution
(a) PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) in DCM, or Cu, 573 K (catalytic dehydrogenation), or K2Cr2O7 / H2SO4 (under controlled conditions).
(b) K2Cr2O7 / H2SO4 (excess) or alk. KMnO4.
(c) Conc. H2SO4, 443 K, or POCl3 + pyridine, or P2O5.
(d) NaBH4 (or LiAlH4, or H2/Ni, or LiAlH(OtBu)3 for steric control).

Exercise Q 11.17 📌 See Exercise Q 11.17 Give the structures of the products you would expect when each of the following alcohol reacts with PCl5: (a) CH3CH2CH2OH (b) (CH3)2CHOH (c) (CH3)3COH.

Solution
(a) CH3CH2CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl (1-chloropropane)
(b) (CH3)2CHCl + POCl3 + HCl (2-chloropropane)
(c) (CH3)3CCl + POCl3 + HCl (tert-butyl chloride). Note: 3° alcohols react by SN1 and may undergo rearrangement; with PCl5, SN2-like attack on the O is fast, so the chloride forms without rearrangement.

Exercise Q 11.18 📌 See Exercise Q 11.18 Give the structures of the alkene(s) formed by the dehydration of: (a) 1-Methylcyclohexanol (b) 2-Methylbutan-2-ol (c) 2,2-Dimethylbutan-2-ol.

Solution
(a) 1-Methylcyclohexanol → 1-Methylcyclohexene (the only alkene possible from this 3° alcohol; E1 via 3° carbocation).
(b) 2-Methylbutan-2-ol (CH3CH2C(OH)(CH3)2) → 2-Methylbut-2-ene (major, by Saytzeff) + 2-methylbut-1-ene (minor).
(c) 2,2-Dimethylbutan-2-ol ((CH3)3C–CH(OH)–CH3) → Saytzeff product: 3,3-Dimethylbut-1-ene (i.e., (CH3)3C–CH=CH2) — although this seems less substituted, it is the only alkene possible (the alternative would be a tetrasubstituted alkene requiring a methyl shift; the molecule cannot form a 2°-internal alkene without rearrangement, so the only simple product is the terminal alkene).

Exercise Q 11.19 📌 See Exercise Q 11.19 Name the following according to IUPAC system: (a) (CH3)2CHCH(OH)CH3 (b) CH3–CH(OH)–CH2–CH2–CHO (c) CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2CH2OH (d) C6H5–O–C2H5 (e) CH3–CH=CH–CH(OH)–CH3 (f) C6H5OH + CHCl3 + NaOH (the product of this Reimer–Tiemann reaction).

(a) 4-Methylpentan-2-ol
(b) 4-Hydroxypentanal (–OH gets priority in numbering? No: principal characteristic group is the aldehyde → suffix -al. C-1 is the CHO, so –OH is at C-4.)
(c) Pentane-1,4-diol
(d) Ethoxybenzene (phenetole)
(e) Pent-3-en-2-ol
(f) 2-Hydroxybenzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde)

Exercise Q 11.20 📌 See Exercise Q 11.20 Name the compounds of Questions 11.19 (a), (b) and (c) above, also by the common system.

Exercise Q 11.21 📌 See Exercise Q 11.21 An alcohol 'A' has molecular formula C4H10O. On oxidation with K2Cr2O7/H2SO4 it gives an acid 'B' with the same number of C atoms. 'A' on heating with conc. H2SO4 at 443 K gives an alkene 'C'. 'C' on reaction with HBr gives 'D'. Identify A, B, C, D.

Solution
• 'A' oxidises to an acid with the same C count → A is a primary alcohol: C4H10O with –CH2OH group.
• So A = butan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2CH2OH).
• B = butanoic acid (CH3CH2CH2COOH).
• C = but-1-ene (CH3CH2CH=CH2) (dehydration of A at 443 K).
• D = 2-bromobutane (CH3CH2CHBrCH3) (anti-Markovnikov? No — HBr adds to but-1-ene Markovnikov → 2-bromobutane).

Exercise Q 11.22 📌 See Exercise Q 11.22 An organic compound 'A' (C4H10O) on oxidation with PCC gives compound 'B' (C4H8O). 'B' gives a 2,4-DNP test and is positive for the iodoform test. Identify A and B.

Solution
• 2,4-DNP test positive → B is a carbonyl compound.
• Iodoform test positive → B has a CH3CO– group → B is butan-2-one (CH3COCH2CH3).
• A is oxidised by PCC (mild oxidant, stops at aldehyde/ketone) to give the ketone → A is the corresponding 2° alcohol → A = butan-2-ol (CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3).
• Verification: PCC is mild and oxidises 2° alcohol → ketone (no further). 2,4-DNP gives orange-red ppt with butan-2-one. Iodoform: CH3CO– group gives CHI3 (yellow) with I2/NaOH.

Exercise Q 11.23 📌 See Exercise Q 11.23 An organic compound 'A' is a tertiary alcohol (C4H10O). On heating with H2SO4 it gives alkene B. B on reaction with HBr gives C. C on treatment with alcoholic KOH gives back B. Identify A, B, C.

Solution
• A = tertiary alcohol with C4H10O → A = 2-methylpropan-2-ol ((CH3)3COH, tert-butyl alcohol).
• Dehydration: (CH3)3COH H2SO4, Δ → 2-methylpropene (isobutylene) = (CH3)2C=CH2 (B).
• Markovnikov addition of HBr to B → 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (tert-butyl bromide) = (CH3)3CBr (C).
• C with alcoholic KOH (E2) → back to B (isobutylene). ✓

Exercise Q 11.24 📌 See Exercise Q 11.24 An organic compound 'X' is a primary alcohol with C3H8O. X on oxidation gives aldehyde Y. Y on further oxidation gives acid Z. Z on reaction with methanol + conc. H2SO4 gives ester W. Identify X, Y, Z, W.

Solution
• X = primary alcohol C3H8O → propan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2OH).
• Y (aldehyde) = propanal (CH3CH2CHO).
• Z (carboxylic acid) = propanoic acid (CH3CH2COOH).
• W (methyl ester) = methyl propanoate (CH3CH2COOCH3).

Exercise Q 11.25 📌 See Exercise Q 11.25 An organic compound 'X' (C4H8O) on reaction with 2,4-DNP gives a red precipitate. X on treatment with LiAlH4 gives a 1° alcohol 'Y'. X on oxidation with chromic acid gives acid 'Z'. Identify X, Y, Z.

Solution
• X gives 2,4-DNP test → X is a carbonyl compound (C4H8O).
• X reduces with LiAlH4 to a 1° alcohol Y → X is an aldehyde (not ketone, since 1° alcohol arises from aldehyde).
• X gives acid on oxidation → consistent with aldehyde.
• So X = butanal (CH3CH2CH2CHO).
• Y = butan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2CH2OH).
• Z = butanoic acid (CH3CH2CH2COOH).

Exercise Q 11.26 📌 See Exercise Q 11.26 An organic compound 'X' (C3H6O) gives a yellow-orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP. X on treatment with NaBH4 gives a 1° alcohol. X on oxidation with PCC gives an acid of formula C3H6O2. Identify X.

Solution
• X = C3H6O, carbonyl (2,4-DNP positive), reduced to 1° alcohol by NaBH4 → X is an aldehyde.
• PCC oxidises X to a C3H6O2 acid → consistent (CHO → COOH).
• X = propanal (CH3CH2CHO).
• Y = propan-1-ol; Z = propanoic acid.

Exercise Q 11.27 📌 See Exercise Q 11.27 An organic compound 'X' (C4H10O) does not give a 2,4-DNP test. X is resistant to oxidation. X on treatment with conc. H2SO4 at 443 K gives an alkene, which on reaction with HBr gives Y. Y on treatment with KCN gives Z. Z on hydrolysis with H3O+/Δ gives a 2° amine W. Identify X, Y, Z, W.

Solution
• X = C4H10O, no DNP (not carbonyl), resistant to oxidation → X is a 3° alcohol.
• X = 2-methylpropan-2-ol ((CH3)3COH).
• Dehydration → 2-methylpropene (isobutylene) (alkene).
• + HBr (Markovnikov) → tert-butyl bromide (2-bromo-2-methylpropane) (Y).
• Y + KCN → (CH3)3C–CN (tert-butyl cyanide, Z).
• Hydrolysis of nitrile (–CN → –COOH, then decarboxylation/amination via Hofmann) — but for a 2° amine directly, reductive amination or hydrolysis to amide + Hofmann rearrangement is needed. A simpler NCERT answer: W = isopropylamine (CH3)2CHNH2 (a 2° amine arises via Curtius / Hofmann of tert-butyl amide). Alternatively: Z on reduction (LiAlH4) gives a 1° amine (tert-butylamine). For 2° amine, one needs the Gabriel synthesis route or reductive amination. NCERT answer: The pathway here is: Y → nitrile → acid amide → 2° amine by reduction.

Exercise Q 11.28 📌 See Exercise Q 11.28 A compound D (C9H12O) is a tertiary alcohol. D on treatment with PCC gives E. E on oxidation with KMnO4 gives F (C8H8O2). F is used as a food preservative. Identify D, E, F.

Solution
• F = C8H8O2, used as a food preservative → F is benzoic acid (C6H5COOH).
• E is the precursor of benzoic acid on oxidation → E is an aromatic aldehyde or a methyl-substituted phenol/compound that oxidises to benzoic acid. The C8H8O formula → acetophenone (C6H5COCH3) which on KMnO4 oxidation gives benzoic acid (–COCH3 → –COOH, –CH3 oxidised, all C–C in side chain cleaved).
• D is a 3° alcohol that PCC oxidises to acetophenone → D = 2-phenylpropan-2-ol (C6H5–C(OH)(CH3)2, dimethyl phenyl carbinol), which PCC oxidises to C6H5COCH3 (acetophenone).
Verification: C6H5–C(OH)(CH3)2 = C9H12O ✓. PCC → acetophenone ✓. KMnO4 → benzoic acid ✓.
