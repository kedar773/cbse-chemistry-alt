1. Introduction to Amines

Amines are the derivatives of ammonia (NH3) obtained by replacing one, two, or all three hydrogen atoms with alkyl (R) or aryl (Ar) groups.

The nitrogen atom in amines is sp3 hybridised with a pyramidal geometry and carries a lone pair of electrons — this lone pair is the key feature that makes amines basic and nucleophilic.

The C–N–C bond angle is close to 107° (slightly less than the tetrahedral 109.5° due to lone pair-bond pair repulsion).

  • Aromatic amines are often named as anilines (e.g., toluidine, anisidine).

4. Structures — Primary, Secondary, Tertiary (Aliphatic & Aromatic)

All amines feature a pyramidal sp3 nitrogen with a lone pair directed at the apex. The aryl amines are not strictly pyramidal — the N is slightly planar due to conjugation with the ring.

Key Feature The lone pair on N is what makes amines act as:
  • Lewis bases — they donate the lone pair to H+ or metal ions.
  • Nucleophiles — they attack electrophiles (e.g., alkyl halides, carbonyl C).

5. Preparation Methods of Amines

Method 1 — Reduction of Nitro Compounds

Used to prepare 1° amines from nitroalkanes or nitroarenes.

R–NO2 + 3 H2 ──Ni/Pt/Pd──▶ R–NH2 + 2 H2O
C6H5NO2 + 3 H2 ──Sn/HCl, then NaOH──▶ C6H5NH2 (Aniline)

Industrial route: Catalytic reduction with H2/Ni, or Fe/HCl (preferred in CBSE textbooks).

Method 2 — Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides (Hofmann Method)

Alkyl halide treated with alcoholic NH3 — gives a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary salt.

R–X + NH3 → R–NH2 → R2NH → R3N → R4N+X−

Disadvantage: mixture of products, hard to separate. Quaternary ammonium salt is the final product when excess alkyl halide is used.

Method 3 — Reduction of Nitriles & Amides
R–C≡N + 2 H2 ──Na/C2H5OH or LiAlH4──▶ R–CH2–NH2

Amide reduction (LiAlH4) yields a 1° amine with the same number of C atoms.

Method 4 — Reduction of Oximes & Nitroso Compounds

Used to convert a carbonyl (C=O) into a 1° amine (one C less in the alkyl side).

Method 5 — Reductive Amination (Leuckart Reaction)

Aldehydes/ketones + NH3 in presence of a reducing agent (NaBH3CN or H2/Ni) give 1°, 2°, or 3° amines directly.

Method 6 — Hofmann Bromamide Degradation HIGH-YIELD ⭐

Converts an amide (R–CONH2) into a 1° amine (R–NH2) with one fewer carbon atom. Reagent: Br2 / NaOH.

R–CO–NH2 + Br2 + 4 NaOH → R–NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2 NaBr + 2 H2O

Key features: No over-alkylation; converts Ar–CONH2 → Ar–NH2 cleanly.

See Solved Q 13.7
Method 7 — Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis HIGH-YIELD ⭐

Best method to prepare a pure 1° aliphatic amine (no mixture, no over-alkylation).

Limitation: Aromatic amines (Ar–NH2) cannot be prepared because aryl halides do not undergo SN2.

See Solved Q 13.4
Method 8 — From Carboxylic Acids via Schmidt Reaction

R–COOH + HN3/H2SO4 → R–NH2 (one C less, like Hofmann). Less commonly asked in CBSE.

6. Physical Properties

  • Boiling points: 1° > 2° > 3° for similar MW. Higher 1°/2° amines engage in intermolecular H-bonding (N–H···N). Tertiary amines cannot H-bond as donors → lowest BP.
  • Solubility: Lower aliphatic amines (up to C5) are miscible with water due to H-bonding with H2O. Aromatic amines (e.g., aniline) are sparingly soluble.
  • Smell: Lower aliphatic amines smell like rotting fish. Aniline & its derivatives are toxic and absorbed through skin.
  • Basicity: Discussed in detail in the next section.

7. Basicity Trends & pKb Analysis

The basicity of an amine depends on the availability of the lone pair on N for donation to H+. More available lone pair = stronger base. Higher pKb means weaker base; lower pKb means stronger base.

7.1 Comparison in Aqueous Phase (Gas vs Aqueous)

In the gas phase (no solvation effects), basicity follows the inductive order:

(CH3)3N > (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > NH3

In aqueous phase (solvation + inductive + steric effects), the order is:

(CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3

Why this anomalous order? (CBSE favourite reasoning question)

Reasoning
  1. +I (inductive) effect of CH3 groups increases electron density on N → basicity ↑. (Predicts 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3.)
  2. Solvation stabilisation of the conjugate acid (R–NH3+) by H2O is maximum for 1° (3 N–H bonds), then 2° (2), then 3° (1) — this counteracts +I.
  3. Steric hindrance around the bulky 3° amine hinders both H-bonding with solvent and protonation.

Net result in aqueous solution: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3. The 3° amine is destabilised by poor solvation of its cation and by steric crowding.

7.3 Comparison: Methyl vs Ethyl Series

Ethylamines are more basic than methylamines in aqueous solution because the larger +I effect of –C2H5 (vs –CH3) pushes more electron density onto N. However, the 2° > 1° > 3° trend is preserved in both series.

(C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > (CH3)2NH > (CH3)3N > CH3NH2 > NH3

8. Why Aliphatic Amines > NH3 > Aromatic Amines

Basicity Order (Aliphatic vs Aromatic, Aqueous)

(CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3 > C6H5NHCH3 > C6H5NH2 > (C6H5)2NH

pKb values: CH3NH2 = 3.38 < NH3 = 4.74 < C6H5NH2 = 9.38 (aniline is very weak).

Reasoning — Why aromatic amines are WEAK bases
  • Resonance: In aniline, the N lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring → less available for protonation.
  • No +I effect: Aryl groups are not alkyl; no electron-donating inductive effect.
  • Destabilisation of conjugate acid: Protonation of aniline destroys the resonance stabilisation of the free amine, so the equilibrium lies far to the left.

Aliphatic amines get a +I push from alkyl groups → lone pair more available → stronger base.

9. Effects of Substituents on Basicity

(a) In the Gas Phase — Inductive effect dominates

3° > 2° > 1° > NH3 — purely +I effect; no solvation.

(b) In Aqueous Phase — Combination of +I, solvation, and steric effects

2° > 1° > 3° > NH3 (anomalous for methyl/ethyl series).

(c) Aromatic ring substituents (on aniline)

  • –CH3, –OCH3, –NH2 (o/p): electron-donating → increase basicity (e.g., p-toluidine is more basic than aniline).
  • –NO2, –CN, –COOH, –X (m-directing): electron-withdrawing → decrease basicity (e.g., p-nitroaniline is much less basic than aniline).
  • –NH3+ (protonated): strong –I / –M → very weak base.

Order: Effect of ring substituents on aniline basicity (o/p)

p-CH3O–C6H4NH2 > p-CH3–C6H4NH2 > C6H5NH2 > p-Cl–C6H4NH2 > p-NO2–C6H4NH2

Chemical Reactions of Amines

The lone pair on N drives most of the reactions of amines — alkylation, acylation, carbylamine formation, diazotisation, and electrophilic substitution on aromatic amines.

1. Acetylation (Reaction with Acid Chlorides / Anhydrides)

Reaction — Acetylation

Both 1° and 2° amines react with acetyl chloride or acetic anhydride to form amides (N-substituted acetamides). Tertiary amines do NOT react.

R–NH2 + (CH3CO)2O → CH3–CO–NH–R + CH3COOH
C6H5NH2 + (CH3CO)2O → C6H5NH–COCH3 + CH3COOH (Acetanilide)

Why acetylation? Acetanilide is less activated than aniline → protects the ring during nitration/sulphonation (Friedel-Crafts type reactions still don't work, but mono-substitution is easier on the deactivated acetanilide). The free –NH2 is then regenerated by acidic/alkaline hydrolysis.

CBSE PYQ — Standard

2. Alkylation with Alkyl Halides

Hofmann Elimination / Exhaustive Methylation

Treatment with excess CH3I gives a quaternary ammonium salt.

When the quaternary salt is treated with AgOH then heat, it undergoes Hofmann elimination to give a tertiary amine and an alkene (used in structure determination of amines).

3. Carbylamine Test (Isocyanide Test) ⭐ Board Favourite

Carbylamine Reaction

Test for 1° amines only. Reagents: CHCl3 + alcoholic KOH + heat. Product: an isocyanide (R–NC) with a foul, obnoxious smell.

R–NH2 + CHCl3 + 3 KOH → R–NC + 3 KCl + 3 H2O

2° and 3° amines do NOT give this test. This is the only test in the chapter that is specific to 1° amines along with HNO2.

See Solved Q 13.10

4. Hinsberg Test — Separation of Amines ⭐ High Yield

Reagent: Benzenesulphonyl chloride (C6H5SO2Cl) in aqueous KOH (Schotten-Baumann conditions).

5. Reaction with Nitrous Acid (HNO2)

Reagent: NaNO2 + HCl at 0–5 °C. Behaviour differs by class.

AmineProductObservable

1° aromatic (Ar–NH2) Ar–N2+Cl− (diazonium salt, 0–5 °C) Stable only at low temperature — see Diazonium section

Libermann Nitroso Reaction The yellow N-nitrosoamine from 2° amines gives a red → blue → green colour when warmed with phenol and conc. H2SO4. This is the Liebermann test for distinguishing 2° amines.

6. Reactions of Aromatic Amines (Ring Substitutions)

The –NH2 group is a strong activator and an o/p-director on the benzene ring (because of resonance donation from N's lone pair).

(a) Bromination

C6H5NH2 + 3 Br2 (aq) → 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (white ppt)

To get mono-bromo product, the –NH2 is first acetylated to acetanilide (less activating), then brominated, then hydrolysed.

(b) Nitration

Direct nitration of aniline with HNO3/H2SO4 oxidises the amine. Aniline is first protected as acetanilide → nitrated → hydrolysed to get p-nitroaniline cleanly.

(c) Sulphonation

Aniline + fuming H2SO4 → p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid (sulphanilic acid). The product exists as an internal salt zwitterion (H2N–C6H4–SO3−).

Important Exception Aniline does NOT undergo Friedel-Crafts alkylation or acylation because AlCl3 (a Lewis acid) coordinates to the lone pair of N, deactivating both the catalyst and the ring. CBSE PYQ — Frequently Asked

7. Diazonium Salts — Preparation & Structure

Diazonium salt: Ar–N2+X− — formed when a primary aromatic amine is treated with nitrous acid (NaNO2 + HCl) at 0–5 °C.
Preparation (Diazotisation)
C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2 HCl ──0–5 °C──▶ C6H5N2+Cl− + NaCl + 2 H2O

Key conditions:

  • Temperature must be 0–5 °C — diazonium salts of aromatic amines are stable only at low temperature. Above 5 °C they decompose to phenols + N2.
  • Aliphatic diazonium salts (R–N2+) are unstable even at 0 °C and decompose to alcohols/alkenes + N2.

8. Reactions of Diazonium Salts

Two main pathways:

  • (A) Substitution reactions — N2 replaced by other groups (with loss of N2).
  • (B) Coupling reactions — N2 retained, the salt acts as an electrophile.

Sandmeyer Reaction ⭐ Board Favourite

Catalysed by Cu(I) salts (CuCl, CuBr) for –Cl, –Br. For –CN, KCN/Cu is used. The Cu(I) catalyst facilitates the loss of N2 and formation of an aryl radical/anion that captures the halide.

See Solved Q 13.13

8.2 Coupling Reactions (Diazonium as Electrophile)

Aryl diazonium salts are weak electrophiles and attack highly activated aromatic rings (phenols, aromatic amines) to form azo compounds (R–N=N–R').

9. Azo Coupling & Azo Dyes ⭐ Board Favourite

Coupling Conditions
  • With phenol: weakly basic medium (pH ≈ 9–10), coupling at para position.
  • With aniline: mildly acidic medium (pH ≈ 4–5), coupling at para position of the aniline ring.
  • With β-naphthol: coupling at α-position (C-1) to give 1-phenylazo-2-naphthol (red dye).
⚠️ pH-Dependence In highly acidic medium, the diazonium salt forms Ar–N2+ and the aniline is protonated to –NH3+ (deactivated) → no coupling.
In highly basic medium, the diazonium salt converts to Ar–N=N–O− (diazotate), which is not electrophilic → no coupling.
Hence a weakly acidic or weakly basic medium is essential.

9.1 Common Azo Dyes

Diazonium componentCouplerDye nameColour

C6H5N2+ Aniline p-H2N-C6H4-N=N-C6H5 (p-aminoazobenzene, aniline yellow) Yellow
C6H5N2+ β-Naphthol 1-Phenylazo-2-naphthol Red
p-NO2-C6H4N2+ β-Naphthol Para Red Red

9.2 Why the colour? (Conceptual hook)

The extended conjugation of the –N=N– azo bridge with the aromatic rings absorbs in the visible region. Substituents that extend conjugation (e.g., –OH, –N(CH3)2) shift the absorption to longer wavelengths → bathochromic shift.

In-Text 13.3Why are amines basic in nature? Compare basicity of methylamine and aniline.

Why amines are basic: Amines have a lone pair of electrons on nitrogen which can be donated to a proton (H+) or to a Lewis acid, making them Lewis bases.

R–NH2 + H2O ⇌ R–NH3+ + OH−

Comparison — CH3NH2 vs C6H5NH2:

  • Methylamine is a stronger base (pKb = 3.38) than aniline (pKb = 9.38).
  • Reason: In aniline, the lone pair on N is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance, making it less available for protonation. Methylamine has no such delocalisation; the +I effect of –CH3 actually increases electron density on N.
In-Text 13.4Arrange the following in increasing order of basicity: (i) C2H5NH2, (C2H5)2NH, (C2H5)3N, NH3 (gas phase and aqueous phase)

In gas phase (only +I effect, no solvation):

NH3 < C2H5NH2 < (C2H5)2NH < (C2H5)3N

In aqueous phase (+I, solvation, steric effects):

NH3 < (C2H5)3N < C2H5NH2 < (C2H5)2NH

Explanation: In aqueous phase, the secondary amine is the most basic due to a balance of +I effect, solvation, and minimal steric hindrance. The tertiary amine is destabilised by poor solvation of its cation (only 1 N–H bond) and steric crowding.

In-Text 13.6Why is aniline a weaker base than cyclohexylamine?

Cyclohexylamine (C6H11NH2) has the –NH2 group on an sp3 carbon; the lone pair on N is fully available. In aniline, the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance, reducing its availability for protonation. Hence aniline is a much weaker base.

In-Text 13.7Give the structures of A, B and C in the following: C6H5NH2 --(Ac2O / Py)→ A --(HNO3 / H2SO4)→ B --(H+ / H2O)→ C
  • A = C6H5NH–COCH3 (Acetanilide) — acetylation protects the –NH2 group from oxidation.
  • B = p-O2N–C6H4–NH–COCH3 (p-Nitroacetanilide) — nitration occurs at the para position.
  • C = p-O2N–C6H4–NH2 (p-Nitroaniline) — acid hydrolysis regenerates the free amine.

Why acetylate first? Direct nitration of aniline would oxidise the –NH2 group and give a mixture of products. Acetanilide is less activating → clean mono-nitration at para.

In-Text 13.8Write the chemical reaction to convert aniline into p-nitroaniline. Why does direct nitration not work?

Step 1 — Acetylation:

C6H5NH2 + (CH3CO)2O → C6H5NH–COCH3 + CH3COOH

Step 2 — Nitration:

C6H5NH–COCH3 + HNO3/H2SO4 → p-O2N–C6H4–NH–COCH3

Step 3 — Hydrolysis:

p-O2N–C6H4–NH–COCH3 + H+/H2O → p-O2N–C6H4–NH2 + CH3COOH

Why not direct? Aniline is strongly activating — direct nitration gives a mixture of mono- and poly-nitro products and oxidises the amine.

In-Text 13.9Why is the pKb of aniline (9.38) much higher than that of methylamine (3.38)?

Higher pKb = weaker base. In aniline, the –NH2 lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance (5 resonance structures) and is therefore less available for protonation. In methylamine, the lone pair is fully available and is further enriched by the +I effect of –CH3. Hence CH3NH2 is a much stronger base.

In-Text 13.10Give one test that distinguishes between 1°, 2° and 3° amines.

Carbylamine test (or Isocyanide test) — only 1° amines give this test.

R–NH2 + CHCl3 + 3 KOH(alc.) → R–NC + 3 KCl + 3 H2O (foul smell)

2° and 3° amines do not give this test.

Alternatively, the Hinsberg test using C6H5SO2Cl can distinguish all three — see Tab 2 §4.

In-Text 13.11Why is it difficult to prepare pure 1° amine by ammonolysis of alkyl halides?

Ammonolysis of an alkyl halide gives a mixture of primary, secondary, tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salt. This is because the product amine is also a nucleophile and reacts further with the alkyl halide. For example:

R–X + NH3 → R–NH2 (1°)
R–NH2 + R–X → R2NH (2°)
R2NH + R–X → R3N (3°)
R3N + R–X → R4N+X− (quaternary)

Hence a pure 1° amine is hard to obtain — this is why Gabriel phthalimide synthesis and Hofmann bromamide degradation are preferred.

Ex 13.4What is the basicity order of (i) p-toluidine, (ii) aniline, (iii) p-nitroaniline in aqueous solution? Why?

Order (increasing basicity):

p-Nitroaniline < Aniline < p-Toluidine

Reason: –NO2 is a strong electron-withdrawing group (–M and –I) which decreases the electron density on N (and destabilises the conjugate acid) → aniline becomes less basic. –CH3 is an electron-donating group (+I and hyperconjugation) which increases electron density on N → p-toluidine is more basic than aniline.

Quantitatively, pKb(p-toluidine) ≈ 8.92, pKb(aniline) = 9.38, pKb(p-nitroaniline) ≈ 12.0. Lower pKb = stronger base.

Ex 13.8Account for the following: (i) Aniline is a weaker base than cyclohexylamine. (ii) Methylamine in water reacts with FeCl3 to give Fe(OH)3 precipitate. (iii) Aniline does not give Friedel-Crafts reaction.

(ii) Methylamine + FeCl3 → Fe(OH)3: Methylamine is a strong base in water and hydrolyses to give OH−:

CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3+ + OH−

The OH− reacts with FeCl3 to precipitate brown Fe(OH)3. Aniline is too weak a base to do this.

(iii) No Friedel-Crafts in aniline: The AlCl3 (Lewis acid) catalyst coordinates with the –NH2 lone pair, forming a salt. This deactivates the ring (positive charge on N) and consumes the catalyst, preventing EAS.

Ex 13.10Why does the pKb of aniline (9.38) become 13.0 when a strong electron-withdrawing group (e.g. –NO2) is introduced at the para position?

A para –NO2 group withdraws electron density from the N of –NH2 through both the –M (resonance) and –I (inductive) effects. This drastically reduces the availability of the N lone pair for protonation, making the amine an even weaker base. The +M effect of –NH2 that normally pushes electron density onto the ring is counteracted, and the electron-withdrawing –NO2 further depletes the lone pair.

Quantitatively, pKb increases from 9.38 (aniline) to ~13.0 (p-nitroaniline) → much weaker base.

Ex 13.11Arrange the following in increasing order of basicity: aniline, p-toluidine, p-nitroaniline.
p-Nitroaniline < Aniline < p-Toluidine

Reason: –CH3 in p-toluidine is electron-donating (+I) → increases basicity. –NO2 in p-nitroaniline is electron-withdrawing (–I, –M) → decreases basicity. pKb values: p-NO2 = 12.0 > aniline = 9.38 > p-CH3 = 8.92 (so reverse order: p-NO2 < aniline < p-CH3 in basicity).

Ex 13.12Give plausible explanation for each of the following: (i) Gabriel phthalimide synthesis is preferred for preparing 1° aliphatic amines. (ii) Aniline does not undergo Friedel-Crafts reaction. (iii) Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than aromatic amines.

(i) Gabriel synthesis preferred for 1° aliphatic amines: It avoids the over-alkylation problem of ammonolysis. The phthalimide nitrogen is not very nucleophilic after one alkylation (steric + electronic), so the reaction stops cleanly at the mono-alkyl stage. Hydrolysis (acidic or hydrazine) gives a pure 1° aliphatic amine.

(ii) Aniline does not give Friedel-Crafts reaction: Aniline is a strong Lewis base — the –NH2 lone pair coordinates with AlCl3, forming anilinium-aluminate complex. The N now bears a positive charge, which is strongly electron-withdrawing (deactivator). Hence the ring is deactivated toward EAS, and the AlCl3 catalyst is also consumed.

(iii) Aliphatic > aromatic in basicity: Alkyl groups in aliphatic amines donate electron density via +I effect, making the N lone pair more available. In aromatic amines, the N lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance, reducing its availability for protonation. Hence aliphatic amines are stronger bases (pKb ≈ 3–4) compared to aniline (pKb ≈ 9.4).

Ex 13.14Describe a method for the separation of a mixture of 1°, 2° and 3° amines (Hinsberg method).

Take the mixture and treat it with benzenesulphonyl chloride (C6H5SO2Cl) in excess aqueous KOH (Schotten-Baumann conditions). Three distinct outcomes arise:

  1. 1° amine forms a precipitate of N-alkyl benzenesulphonamide (C6H5SO2NHR), which is soluble in excess KOH (its N–H is acidic). On acidification, it re-precipitates. Filter and collect the 1° sulphonamide → hydrolyse (H+ or OH−, heat) → 1° amine.
  2. 2° amine forms a precipitate of N,N-dialkyl benzenesulphonamide (C6H5SO2NR2) which is insoluble in KOH (no N–H). Filter → hydrolyse → 2° amine.
  3. 3° amine does not react. It remains in the alkaline filtrate. On acidification, it precipitates as the salt or is recovered as the free base.

This single-reagent test cleanly separates all three classes — see Tab 2 §4 for the full table.

Ex 13.15Write short notes on: (i) Hofmann bromamide degradation (ii) Carbylamine reaction (iii) Diazotisation

(i) Hofmann bromamide degradation: Conversion of an amide (R–CONH2) to a 1° amine (R–NH2) with one fewer carbon using Br2/NaOH. The mechanism goes through an isocyanate intermediate (R–N=C=O) which is hydrolysed to amine + CO2. Clean, no over-alkylation, useful for both aliphatic and aromatic amides.

(ii) Carbylamine reaction: A diagnostic test for 1° amines. The 1° amine is heated with chloroform and alcoholic KOH to give an isocyanide (R–NC), recognised by its extremely foul odour. 2° and 3° amines do not give this reaction. Used in detection of 1° amines in the lab.

(iii) Diazotisation: Treatment of a 1° aromatic amine (Ar–NH2) with NaNO2 + HCl at 0–5 °C to form an aryl diazonium salt (Ar–N2+Cl−). Aliphatic 1° amines under the same conditions give unstable diazonium salts that decompose to alcohols + N2. Aryl diazonium salts are versatile — they undergo substitution (Sandmeyer, Balz-Schiemann) and coupling (azo dyes).

Ex 13.19Why does an alkylamine dissolve in HCl while an arylamine does not (or does so only partially)?

Alkylamines (R–NH2) are strong bases (pKb ≈ 3–4) and readily accept a proton from HCl to form water-soluble ammonium salts (R–NH3+Cl−):

R–NH2 + HCl → R–NH3+Cl− (water-soluble)

Arylamines (Ar–NH2) are weak bases (pKb ≈ 9) because the N lone pair is delocalised into the ring → much less readily protonated. The hydrochloride salt of aniline (anilinium chloride) is somewhat water-soluble but reverts to aniline easily.

Hence alkylamines dissolve readily in dilute HCl, whereas arylamines require concentrated HCl or special conditions.

Ex 13.20Why is the basicity of a 1° amine greater than a 3° amine in aqueous solution, even though +I effect predicts the reverse?

This is the classic anomalous basicity order in the aqueous phase. The 1° amine benefits from:

  1. Strong solvation of the conjugate acid by water through H-bonding. A 1° ammonium ion (R–NH3+) has three N–H bonds → maximum H-bonding with water. A 3° ammonium ion (R3NH+) has only one N–H bond → poor solvation.
  2. Less steric hindrance for the small 1° amine to approach H+ and to interact with solvent.

These two factors override the +I effect (which would predict 3° > 1°). The 3° amine is destabilised in water despite having the most electron-rich N.

Net order in water: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3 (for methyl/ethyl series).

Ex 13.25Why are quaternary ammonium salts more stable than primary ammonium salts?

Quaternary ammonium salts (R4N+X−) have all four hydrogens of NH4+ replaced by alkyl/aryl groups. They cannot lose a proton to form a neutral amine (no N–H bond), so they are permanently charged and cannot undergo deprotonation. They are stable up to high temperatures and do not exhibit amine-like properties (no basic N to accept another proton, no N–H for H-bonding).

Primary ammonium salts (R–NH3+X−) can revert to the free amine R–NH2 by deprotonation, and they are also susceptible to oxidation of the N–H bond. Hence quaternary salts are more thermally and chemically stable.

Ex 13.28Give the reaction scheme for the conversion of aniline to sulphanilic acid. Why does it exist as a zwitterion?

Ex 13.29Why is HNO2 not stored in pure form? How is it generated in situ for diazotisation?

Nitrous acid (HNO2) is unstable and decomposes readily:

3 HNO2 → HNO3 + 2 NO ↑ + H2O

It cannot be stored in pure form — it must be generated in situ by treating sodium nitrite (NaNO2) with dilute HCl at 0–5 °C:

NaNO2 + HCl → HNO2 + NaCl

The freshly generated HNO2 then reacts with the aromatic 1° amine to form the diazonium salt in the same reaction vessel.

Ex 13.30Give reasons: (i) Aniline is a weaker base than ammonia. (ii) Ethylamine is more basic than aniline. (iii) Diethylamine is more basic than triethylamine in aqueous solution.

(i) Aniline < Ammonia: The lone pair on N in aniline is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance → less available for protonation. In NH3, the lone pair is fully available. Hence aniline (pKb ≈ 9.4) is a much weaker base than NH3 (pKb = 4.74).

(ii) Ethylamine > Aniline: The +I effect of the ethyl group pushes electron density onto N (making lone pair more available), and there is no resonance delocalisation. In aniline, resonance drains the lone pair. Hence CH3CH2NH2 (pKb = 3.33) is much more basic than C6H5NH2 (pKb = 9.38).

(iii) (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N in water: The 2° amine benefits from a balance of +I effect and good solvation of its conjugate acid (R2NH2+ has 2 N–H bonds for H-bonding with water). The 3° amine, despite having the largest +I effect, is destabilised by poor solvation of R3NH+ (only 1 N–H) and by steric hindrance to protonation. Hence 2° wins in aqueous solution.

Ex 13.31Why does the coupling of benzene diazonium chloride with aniline require a weakly acidic medium (pH ≈ 4–5) while coupling with phenol requires a weakly basic medium (pH ≈ 9–10)?

With aniline: Aniline is a weak base. In strongly basic medium, it remains as the free amine — but the diazonium salt (Ar–N2+) is unstable in base and converts to the non-electrophilic diazotate (Ar–N=N–O−). In strongly acidic medium, aniline is protonated to the deactivated anilinium ion (–NH3+), which does not couple. Hence a weakly acidic medium (pH ≈ 4–5) keeps aniline in the reactive free-amine form and the diazonium as a cation — both reactive, coupling occurs at the para position.

With phenol: Phenol is a weak acid. In acidic medium, it remains as PhOH (less activated for EAS). In basic medium, it forms the phenoxide ion (PhO−), which is strongly activated (–O− is a much stronger activator than –OH). A weakly basic medium (pH ≈ 9–10) gives enough phenoxide for EAS while keeping the diazonium cation intact.

Or the parent compound sulphanilamide (p-H2N–C6H4–SO2NH2) — a powerful antibacterial agent.

Synthesis (key step from aniline): Acetanilide is chlorosulphonated (ClSO3H) → acetanilide-p-sulphonyl chloride → condensed with NH3 or an amine (pyridine) → hydrolysis → sulphanilamide.

C6H5NHCOCH3 + ClSO3H → p-ClO2S–C6H4–NHCOCH3
→ + NH3 / base → p-H2NO2S–C6H4–NHCOCH3
→ + H2O / H+ → p-H2N–C6H4–SO2NH2 (Sulphanilamide)
Ex 13.39Write the structure and use of (i) Saccharin (ii) Sulphanilamide (iii) Sulpha drugs

(i) Saccharin — 2-Benzisothiazol-3(2H)-one 1,1-dioxide; an artificial sweetener (much sweeter than sucrose). Synthesised from toluene by sulphonation and then oxidation/cyclisation with NH3.

(ii) Sulphanilamide (p-H2N–C6H4–SO2NH2) — a powerful antibacterial drug (the first sulpha drug, used in WWII).

(iii) Sulpha drugs — a class of antibacterial agents with the general structure H2N–C6H4–SO2NH–R. They act as antimetabolites by mimicking p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), blocking folic acid synthesis in bacteria. Examples: sulphapyridine, sulphadiazine, sulphathiazole.

Ex 13.40Why is the pKa of anilinium ion (~4.6) lower than that of the conjugate acid of methylamine (~10.6)?

The pKa of the conjugate acid reflects the strength of the parent base: higher pKa of conjugate acid = stronger base.

  • CH3NH3+ has pKa ≈ 10.6 → CH3NH2 is a relatively strong base.
  • C6H5NH3+ has pKa ≈ 4.6 → C6H5NH2 is a much weaker base.

The anilinium ion is a stronger acid (i.e., the equilibrium lies more towards deprotonation) because the free aniline is stabilised by resonance of the N lone pair with the benzene ring — this stabilisation does not exist in the protonated form. Hence the conjugate acid readily loses its proton → lower pKa → aniline is a weaker base than methylamine.
