1. Classification of Haloalkanes & Haloarenes

💡 Definition

Haloalkanes are compounds obtained by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms of an open-chain (aliphatic) hydrocarbon by halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, I).
Haloarenes are compounds obtained by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms of an aromatic hydrocarbon (benzene ring) by halogen atoms.

1.2 On the basis of the carbon bearing the halogen

⭐ Classification is super-important for reactions Q 6.1, 6.13, 6.30, 6.31

(a) Alkyl halides (haloalkanes)

ClassCarbon bearing X is attached to…Example

(b) Allylic, Benzylic, Vinylic & Aryl halides

  • Allylic halide — halogen attached to the carbon next to a C=C double bond.
    CH₂=CH–CH₂–Br (3-Bromopropene / Allyl bromide)
  • Benzylic halide — halogen attached to a carbon that is directly attached to the benzene ring.
    C₆H₅–CH₂–Cl (Benzyl chloride / 1-Phenylchloromethane)
  • Vinylic halide — halogen directly attached to a sp² carbon of a C=C double bond.
    CH₂=CH–Cl (Chloroethene / Vinyl chloride)
  • Aryl halide — halogen directly attached to a sp² carbon of the benzene ring.
    C₆H₅–Cl (Chlorobenzene)
⚠️ Watch out: allyl ≠ vinyl!
In allyl halides the X is on the sp³ carbon next to the C=C, so the C–X is normal and reactive. In vinyl halides the X is on the sp² carbon of the C=C, so the C–X has partial double-bond character and is very unreactive.

2.2 IUPAC rules Q 6.2, 6.3

⭐ Steps to name a haloalkane/haloarene
  1. Find the longest carbon chain (parent alkane).
  2. Number to give the halogen the lowest locant; if tie with substituent, halide wins by older IUPAC rule, but as per NCERT examples we give lowest locant overall.
  3. Replace the “-e” of alkane with the halide prefix: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo.
  4. For haloarenes, name the parent as halobenzene (e.g., chlorobenzene) or use the substituted alkyl chain when the chain is larger.
  5. List substituents alphabetically: fluoro → chloro → bromo → iodo (ignore “di/tri” prefixes for ordering).
🧬 Worked examples
  • CH₃–CH(Br)–CH₃ → 2-Bromopropane
  • CH₂=CH–CH(Br)–CH₂–CH₃ → 3-Bromopent-1-ene
  • p-Cl–C₆H₄–CH₂–CH(CH₃)₂ → 1-Chloro-4-(2-methylpropyl)benzene (or p-chloroisobutylbenzene)
  • (CH₃)₂CHCH(Cl)CH₃ → 2-Chloro-3-methylbutane

3. Nature of the C–X Bond

💡 Key idea
Carbon and halogen differ greatly in electronegativity and size, so the C–X bond is polarised (C^δ+–X^δ–). This polarity makes haloalkanes/haloarenes prone to nucleophilic substitution.
BondBond length (pm)Bond enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹)Dipole moment (D)
C–F1394851.51
C–Cl1783391.56
C–Br1932761.48
C–I2142381.29
⭐ Reactivity order for alkyl halides (nucleophilic substitution)

RI > RBr > RCl > RF

Reason: as size of X increases and bond strength decreases, the C–X bond becomes weaker and easier to break, so reactivity rises from F to I.

💡 C–X bond in haloarenes
In chlorobenzene, the C–Cl bond is shorter than in alkyl chlorides because the halogen is attached to a sp²-hybridised carbon (more s-character → shorter bond). The lone pair on Cl is also delocalised into the benzene ring, giving the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character — making haloarenes far less reactive.

4. Methods of Preparation Q 6.4, 6.14, 6.16

4.1 From Alcohols (the most common method)

R–OH + HX → R–X + H₂O    (HX = HCl, HBr, HI)
R–OH + NaX + H₂SO₄ → R–X + NaHSO₄ + H₂O    (X = Cl, Br)
3R–OH + PX₃ → 3R–X + H₃PO₃
R–OH + PCl₅ → R–Cl + POCl₃ + HCl
R–OH + SOCl₂ (thionyl chloride) → R–Cl + SO₂ + HCl
⚠️ Why not H₂SO₄ + NaI?
NaI + H₂SO₄ produces HI, but concentrated H₂SO₄ oxidises HI to I₂ (2HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O), so the yield of alkyl iodide falls drastically. We use KI + H₃PO₄ instead. Q 6.20

4.2 Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes

CH₄ + Cl₂ ⟶ hv → CH₃Cl + HCl (further chlorination gives CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄)
CH₃–CH₃ + Br₂ ⟶ hv → CH₃CH₂Br + HBr
⚙️ Mechanism: Free Radical Halogenation (3 steps) Q 6.15
1Initiation: Cl–Cl ⟶ hv → 2 Cl•
2Propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → •CH₃ + HCl    then    •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
3Termination: Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂   or   •CH₃ + Cl• → CH₃Cl   or   •CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆
⭐ Order of reactivity of halogens with alkanes

F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂

Order of selectivity (H abstraction): 3° > 2° > 1°; Bromine is more selective than chlorine.

4.3 Allylic & Benzylic Halogenation (NBS / high temperature)

CH₃–CH=CH₂ + Cl₂ ⟶ 500°C → CH₂=CH–CH₂–Cl (allyl chloride) + HCl
C₆H₅–CH₃ + Cl₂ ⟶ hv or heat → C₆H₅–CH₂Cl (benzyl chloride) + HCl
💡 Why allylic/benzylic?
The intermediate allyl / benzyl radical is resonance-stabilised, so halogenation at these positions is favoured over the vinylic/aryl position.

4.4 Vinylic & Aryl Halogenation — NOT direct

Aryl and vinylic halides cannot be prepared by direct halogenation of arenes/alkenes; they are obtained by special methods such as the Sandmeyer reaction (diazonium salt + CuCl/HCl) or the BHC/Balard preparation of chlorobenzene from benzene + Cl₂ + uv (gives addition, not substitution).

4.5 From Alkenes — Addition Reactions

(a) Addition of HX (Markovnikov)

CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃–CH(Br)–CH₃ (2-Bromopropane, Markovnikov product)
CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HI → CH₃–CH(I)–CH₃ (2-Iodopropane)

(b) Anti-Markovnikov (peroxide effect / Kharash effect)

CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HBr ⟶ (C₆H₅COO)₂ (peroxide) → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–Br (1-Bromopropane)
⚠️ Peroxide effect works ONLY with HBr!
HCl and HI do not show the peroxide effect (HCl: H–Cl bond too strong; HI: peroxide reduces I₂).

(c) Addition of X₂ (halogen)

CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ ⟶ CH₂Br–CH₂Br (1,2-Dibromoethane, vic-dibromide)
CH₃–CH=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₃–CHBr–CH₂Br (1,2-Dibromopropane)
⚙️ Mechanism: Electrophilic addition of Br₂ to alkene
1The π-electrons attack Br₂, displacing Br⁻ and forming a cyclic bromonium ion.
2Br⁻ attacks the more substituted carbon from the opposite face → anti addition, trans product.

5. Physical Properties Q 6.4, 6.5, 6.6, 6.24

5.1 Boiling Point

💡 Factors affecting B.P.
  1. Intermolecular forces — larger molecules have stronger van der Waals forces.
  2. Polar C–X bond — adds dipole-dipole attraction.
  3. Size/mass of halogen — I > Br > Cl > F for boiling point (molar mass dominates).
Series trendReason
For same alkyl group, BP: RI > RBr > RCl > RFIncreasing molar mass / surface area
For same halogen, BP: 1° < 2° < 3° (branching reduces surface contact)Van der Waals forces decrease with branching
More halogens → higher BPCH₄ (–161 °C) < CH₃Cl (–24 °C) < CH₂Cl₂ (40 °C) < CHCl₃ (61 °C) < CCl₄ (77 °C)

5.2 Density

Monohaloalkanes (except fluoro) are denser than water. Density increases with the number and mass of halogen atoms: CHCl₃ (1.49) > CCl₄ (1.59).

5.3 Solubility

💡 Haloalkanes/Haloarenes are insoluble in water Q 6.23
Although C–X is polar, haloalkanes cannot form H-bonds with water (no O–H or N–H), so they are immiscible with water but freely soluble in organic solvents like ether, CCl₄, benzene. The hydrocarbon part dominates the interaction.

5.4 Dipole moment Q 6.6, 6.24

⭐ Why μ(cyclohexyl chloride) > μ(chlorobenzene)
In chlorobenzene, the C–Cl σ bond has a dipole, but the lone pair on Cl is delocalised into the ring (resonance) which produces a dipole in the opposite direction. These two dipoles partially cancel, giving a smaller net dipole moment (≈ 1.69 D). In cyclohexyl chloride, no such resonance exists, so the dipoles add up (≈ 2.1 D).

6. Chemical Reactions of Haloalkanes Q 6.10 – 6.13, 6.17, 6.18

6.1 Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

💡 General picture

Nu:⁻ + R–X → R–Nu + X:⁻

The nucleophile (Nu⁻) attacks the δ+ carbon and displaces the halide ion. Two mechanisms operate: SN2 (one-step, bimolecular) and SN1 (two-step, unimolecular).

(a) SN2 Mechanism (Bimolecular, Concerted)

⚙️ Mechanism: SN2 — single concerted step

For primary (and most secondary) alkyl halides, e.g., hydrolysis of CH₃–CH₂–Br with OH⁻.

1. Back-side attack: Nucleophile approaches the carbon opposite to the leaving group.

2. A transition state is formed in which the C–Nu bond is partially formed and C–X is partially broken — carbon is sp²-like (trigonal bipyramidal) with Nu and X in axial positions.

3. Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) — the product has the opposite stereochemistry.

Rate = k [R–X][Nu⁻] — second order, bimolecular.

⭐ SN2 reactivity order (for R–X with same halogen)

Methyl > Primary (1°) > Secondary (2°) >> Tertiary (3°)

Tertiary halides are sterically blocked — backside attack is impossible.

(b) SN1 Mechanism (Unimolecular, Two-step)

⚙️ Mechanism: SN1 — two steps via carbocation

For tertiary (and some secondary) alkyl halides, e.g., hydrolysis of (CH₃)₃C–Cl in water.

Step 1 (slow): The C–X bond ionises to form a carbocation (R⁺) and X⁻. This is the rate-determining step.

Step 2 (fast): The nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation from either face → racemisation (50 : 50 retention + inversion if the carbon was a stereocentre).

Rate = k [R–X] — first order, unimolecular.

⭐ SN1 reactivity order (for R–X with same halogen)

Tertiary (3°) > Secondary (2°) > Primary (1°) >> Methyl

Stability of carbocation: 3° > 2° > 1° (hyperconjugation / +I effect).

💡 KCN vs AgCN — who attacks from where? Q 6.10
  • KCN is ionic (K⁺ CN⁻). The C atom of CN⁻ is more nucleophilic than N → attack from C → alkyl cyanide R–C≡N.
  • AgCN is largely covalent (Ag–C bond). The N end is the only free donor → attack from N → isocyanide R–N≡C.
The major product with KCN is the cyanide (nitrile); with AgCN it is the isocyanide. Cyanide ion is ambidentate (two donor atoms).
💡 KNO₂ vs AgNO₂ — same trick, different anion Q 6.10
NO₂⁻ is also ambidentate (N and O). In KNO₂ the O is more nucleophilic → nitroalkane; in AgNO₂ the Ag–O bond makes N the donor → alkyl nitrite (R–O–N=O).

6.3 Elimination Reactions Q 6.11, 6.17(xi)–(xv)

💡 β-elimination — a nucleophile acts as a base
Haloalkanes + strong base (alc. KOH) → alkene + HX. Two mechanisms: E2 (one step, anti-periplanar) and E1 (two step, via carbocation).
CH₃–CH(Br)–CH₃ + alc. KOH → CH₃–CH=CH₂ (propene) + KBr + H₂O
CH₃–CH₂–CH(Br)–CH₃ + alc. KOH → CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ (but-2-ene, Saytzeff) + KBr + H₂O
⭐ Saytzeff (Zaitsev) Rule
In dehydrohalogenation, the preferred product is the more highly substituted alkene (more stable). For 2-bromobutane the major product is but-2-ene (CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃) rather than but-1-ene.
⚙️ Mechanism: E2 (bimolecular, anti-periplanar)

1. Base removes a β-H while the leaving group X departs from the opposite side.

2. Anti-periplanar geometry: H–C–C–X must be in the same plane but on opposite sides.

3. Two new π-bonds form between Cα and Cβ; C–X breaks; C–H breaks. Concerted — single step.

Rate = k[RX][base].

⚙️ Mechanism: E1 (unimolecular)

1. Slow: C–X ionises → carbocation (R⁺) + X⁻.

2. Fast: Base removes a β-H from the carbocation → alkene.

Rate = k[RX]. Favoured for 3° halides, polar protic solvents, weak bases.

Dehalogenation (vicinal dihalides)

CH₃–CH(Br)–CH(Br)–CH₃ + Zn dust ⟶ Δ → CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ (but-2-ene) + ZnBr₂

6.4 Reaction with Metals Q 6.12, 6.17

(a) Wurtz Reaction (R–X with Na in dry ether)

2 R–X + 2 Na ⟶ dry ether → R–R + 2 NaX
🧬 Example
2 CH₃–CH₂–Br + 2 Na → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (n-butane) + 2 NaBr

(b) Corey–House Synthesis (R₂CuLi — lithium dialkylcuprate)

2 R–Li + CuI → R₂CuLi (lithium dialkylcuprate)
R₂CuLi + R'–X → R–R' + R–Cu + LiX
🧬 Example
(CH₃)₂CuLi + CH₃CH₂Cl → CH₃–CH₂–CH₃ (propane)

(c) Grignard Reagent Formation

R–X + Mg ⟶ dry ether → R–Mg–X (Grignard reagent)
🧬 Examples
CH₃–CH₂–Br + Mg → CH₃–CH₂–MgBr (ethylmagnesium bromide)
C₆H₅–Br + Mg → C₆H₅–MgBr (phenylmagnesium bromide)
⚠️ Why anhydrous conditions? Q 6.19, 6.27
Grignard reagents are strong nucleophiles / bases. They react violently with any protic source — water, alcohols, even the –OH of glass is enough.
R–MgX + H₂O → R–H + Mg(OH)X
Hence, the reaction must be carried out in anhydrous ether with dry apparatus.

7. Chemical Reactions of Haloarenes Q 6.21, 6.22, 6.25, 6.26

💡 Haloarenes are LESS reactive than haloalkanes

Three reasons:

  1. Resonance: Lone pair on Cl is delocalised into the ring → C–Cl acquires partial double bond character → shorter and stronger.
  2. sp² carbon: The C–X is on sp², more s-character → shorter, stronger bond.
  3. Instability of phenyl cation: An SN1 route would need an aryl cation, which is highly unstable (the empty orbital is in the ring plane and cannot overlap).

7.1 Nucleophilic Substitution (very slow; activated ring → fast)

⭐ Order: p-Nitrochlorobenzene >> Chlorobenzene Q 6.22
Strong electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –CN, –COCH₃) at ortho / para positions stabilise the anionic Meisenheimer intermediate, making SNAr much faster.
p-NO₂–C₆H₄–Cl + aq. NaOH ⟶ 443 K, 300 atm → p-NO₂–C₆H₄–OH + NaCl
⚙️ Mechanism: SNAr — addition–elimination (Benzyne for unactivated)

1. Nu⁻ attacks the carbon bearing X → Meisenheimer complex (sp³, carbanion stabilised by –M groups at o/p).

2. X⁻ leaves, restoring aromaticity → substituted product.

7.2 Electrophilic Substitution (favoured because Cl is o/p-directing)

C₆H₅Cl + HNO₃ ⟶ conc. H₂SO₄ → o-NO₂–C₆H₄–Cl + p-NO₂–C₆H₄–Cl (mainly para)
C₆H₅Cl + Cl₂ ⟶ FeCl₃ → o-Cl–C₆H₄–Cl + p-Cl–C₆H₄–Cl (mainly para)
C₆H₅Cl + CH₃Cl ⟶ anh. AlCl₃ → o- and p-CH₃–C₆H₄–Cl
🧬 Real products
Chlorobenzene + fuming HNO₃ / H₂SO₄ → mainly 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene (p-nitrochlorobenzene).
Chlorobenzene + Cl₂ / FeCl₃ → mainly 1,4-dichlorobenzene.
Chlorobenzene + CH₃Cl / AlCl₃ → mainly 1-chloro-4-methylbenzene (p-chlorotoluene).
⭐ Halogens are o/p-directing but deactivating
The lone pair on halogen (+M, ortho/para director) increases ring electron density, but the –I effect of the strongly electronegative halogen reduces overall reactivity. Net: ring is deactivated but o/p substitution dominates.

7.3 Reaction with Metals

(a) Wurtz–Fittig Reaction (R–X + Ar–X + Na)

C₆H₅–Br + CH₃–Br + 2 Na ⟶ dry ether → C₆H₅–CH₃ (toluene) + 2 NaBr
🧬 Real molecules
Bromobenzene + iodomethane + Na → toluene
Chlorobenzene + 1-chloropropane + Na → propylbenzene

(b) Fittig Reaction (R–X + R–X + Na → R–R)

The classical Fittig reaction is for two alkyl halides only. In NCERT, the Wurtz–Fittig couples an aryl and an alkyl halide.

(c) Ullmann Reaction (biaryl synthesis)

2 C₆H₅–I + 2 Cu ⟶ Δ → C₆H₅–C₆H₅ (biphenyl) + 2 CuI

7.4 Reactions of the Side Chain (when aryl halide has a benzylic C–X)

Benzylic halides behave like primary alkyl halides — fast SN1, SN2, and E1/E2.

C₆H₅–CH₂Cl + alc. KCN → C₆H₅–CH₂–C≡N (phenylacetonitrile) — SN2
C₆H₅–CH₂Cl + alc. KOH → C₆H₅–CH=CH₂? No! → C₆H₅–CH₂–OH (excess? no — actually elimination requires β-H, which is on the ring or absent, so substitution dominates giving benzyl alcohol under aqueous KOH, and stilbene from PhCH₂Cl with PhCHCl is via Wurtz; with strong base PhCH=CHPh forms trans-stilbene if two benzyl halides couple)

(Benzyl chloride: aq. KOH → benzyl alcohol C₆H₅CH₂OH. With alc. KOH, elimination to give stilbene requires a separate benzylic coupling — studied in Wurtz.)

8. Polyhalogen Compounds Q 6.25, 6.31

8.1 Dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂) — Methylene chloride

Colourless, sweet-smelling liquid, BP 40 °C. Used as a solvent for paint strippers, aerosol propellants, in food processing (decaffeination of coffee).

8.2 Trichloromethane (CHCl₃) — Chloroform

Colourless, dense (1.49 g/cm³), sweet-smelling liquid, BP 61 °C. Once used as an anaesthetic, now replaced (causes liver damage and is carcinogenic). Used as a laboratory solvent. On exposure to light and air it forms the poisonous phosgene (COCl₂), so it is stored in dark bottles filled to the brim.

2 CHCl₃ + O₂ ⟶ hv → 2 COCl₂ + 2 HCl

8.3 Triiodomethane (CHI₃) — Iodoform

Yellow solid, antiseptic. Formed in the iodoform test for the –COCH₃ group (methyl ketone) or CH₃CH(OH)– (ethanol / 2° alcohol).

CH₃–CO–CH₃ + 3 I₂ + 4 NaOH → CHI₃↓ (yellow) + CH₃COONa + 3 NaI + 3 H₂O

8.4 Tetrachloromethane (CCl₄) — Carbon tetrachloride

Colourless, non-inflammable liquid. Once used as a fire-extinguisher fluid and dry-cleaning agent, but is now banned because it damages the ozone layer and is highly toxic (liver damage). When heated to 773 K, it gives the toxic phosgene:

CCl₄ + H₂O ⟶ Δ → COCl₂ + 2 HCl

8.5 Freons Q 6.25

💡 Freons (CFC) — banned!
Freon-12: CCl₂F₂ (dichlorodifluoromethane). Was used as a refrigerant and aerosol propellant. Banned under the Montreal Protocol (1987) because chlorine radicals catalyse ozone destruction:
CF₂Cl₂ ⟶ hv → •CF₂Cl + Cl•;   Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂;   ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂
Replacement: HFCs (e.g. CH₂FCF₃ — HFC-134a) which contain no Cl and don't harm ozone.

8.6 p,p′-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT)

Insecticide used against mosquitoes (malaria) and crop pests. Made by:

2 C₆H₅Cl + CCl₃CHO ⟶ con. H₂SO₄ → (p-Cl–C₆H₄)₂CH–CCl₃ (DDT) + H₂O

DDT is banned in many countries because it is non-biodegradable, accumulates in food chains, and harms birds (egg-shell thinning).

9. NCERT Solved Examples (with steps)

❓ NCERT Example 6.1 — IUPAC name of a haloalkane

Q. Give the IUPAC name of the compound: (CH₃)₂CH–CH(Cl)–CH₃.

Step 1. Longest chain = 4 carbons → butane.

Step 2. Substituents: chloro at C-2, methyl at C-3.

Step 3. Alphabetical: chloro before methyl.

Answer: 2-Chloro-3-methylbutane. (Type: 2°, alkyl)

❓ NCERT Example 6.2 — Nomenclature of haloarene

Q. Name: p-Cl–C₆H₄–CH₂–CH(CH₃)₂.

Step. The ring is the parent (benzene), with a chloro substituent. The chain is a 2-methylpropyl (isobutyl) group. Chlorine is on the ring carbon para to the side chain attachment.

Answer: 1-Chloro-4-(2-methylpropyl)benzene (or p-chloro-(2-methylpropyl)benzene).

❓ NCERT Example 6.3 — Boiling-point comparison

Q. Which has higher BP — 1-bromopropane or 2-bromopropane?

Reason. Both have the same molar mass and dipole. 1-Bromopropane is a straight chain (more surface contact, stronger van der Waals forces); 2-bromopropane is branched. Answer: 1-Bromopropane has the higher BP.

❓ NCERT Example 6.4 — Boiling-point comparison (halogen size)

Q. Which has higher BP — CH₃Cl or CH₃I?

Reason. Larger halogen → larger electron cloud → stronger London dispersion. Answer: CH₃I has higher BP (CH₃Cl = –24 °C, CH₃I = 42.4 °C).

❓ NCERT Example 6.5 — Finkelstein reaction

Q. Write the reaction: 1-bromopropane with NaI in dry acetone.

Answer: CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + NaI → CH₃CH₂CH₂I + NaBr↓ (NaBr is insoluble in acetone, so equilibrium shifts forward).

Mechanism: SN2; works only for 1° (and 2°) halides — 3° and aryl chlorides do not react.

❓ NCERT Example 6.6 — Wurtz reaction stoichiometry

Q. How would you synthesise n-hexane from a haloalkane?

Answer: 2 CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + 2 Na ⟶ dry ether → CH₃(CH₂)₄CH₃ (n-hexane) + 2 NaBr. Wurtz reaction works best for symmetric alkanes; for unsymmetric alkanes, use the Corey–House synthesis instead.

❓ NCERT Example 6.7 — Williamson synthesis

Q. Starting from CH₃CH₂OH, prepare CH₃CH₂OCH(CH₃)₂.

Steps:

  1. CH₃CH₂OH + Na → CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ (sodium ethoxide) + ½H₂
  2. (CH₃)₂CHOH + PBr₃ → (CH₃)₂CHBr (2-bromopropane)
  3. CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + (CH₃)₂CHBr → CH₃CH₂OCH(CH₃)₂ (ethyl isopropyl ether, SN2)

Why 2° alkyl halide? Williamson with 3° alkyl halide is impossible — elimination wins. With 1° and 2° halides, SN2 is fine.

❓ NCERT Example 6.8 — Conversion problem

Q. Convert: Ethanol → Ethane nitrile (CH₃CH₂CN).

Steps:

  1. CH₃CH₂OH + PCl₅ → CH₃CH₂Cl + POCl₃ + HCl (chlorination)
  2. CH₃CH₂Cl + alc. KCN → CH₃CH₂CN + KCl (SN2 with KCN — gives cyanide, not isocyanide)

Total yield is good; ethanol is the starting material.

❓ NCERT Example 6.9 — Conversion (propene → 1-nitropropane)

Q. Propene → 1-nitropropane.

Steps:

  1. CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HBr (peroxide) → CH₃–CH₂–CH₂Br (anti-Markovnikov, 1-bromopropane)
  2. CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + AgNO₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NO₂ (1-nitropropane) + AgBr

❓ Q 6.5 — Arrange in increasing order of boiling point

❓ Q 6.6 — Dipole moment of chlorobenzene is lower than cyclohexyl chloride

In chlorobenzene, the C–Cl bond dipole is partially opposed by the dipole arising from the delocalisation of the chlorine lone pair into the π-system of the ring (resonance). The two effects cancel partially, lowering the net dipole moment.

In cyclohexyl chloride, the chlorine is on an sp³ carbon; no such resonance exists, so the C–Cl bond is the only contributor, giving a higher net dipole.

❓ Q 6.10 — KCN vs AgCN

CN⁻ is an ambident nucleophile (can attack from C or N). In KCN (ionic, K⁺ CN⁻), the C end is the more nucleophilic site → attack from C → alkyl cyanide (R–C≡N).

In AgCN, the Ag–C bond is largely covalent, so the only free donor is the N end → attack from N → alkyl isocyanide (R–N≡C).

Per Saytzeff, pent-2-ene is the major product.

❓ Q 6.13 — Reactivity comparisons (Explain)

(i) Cyclohexyl chloride more reactive than chlorobenzene — chlorobenzene has resonance (Cl lone pair into ring) and sp² C, both making C–Cl shorter and stronger. Cyclohexyl chloride has a normal sp³ C–Cl which ionises/reacts easily.

(ii) 1-Chloropropane more reactive than 2-chloropropane — SN2 is sterically hindered at 2° (a methyl group on each side of the C–X); 1° halide has free backside attack.

(iii) tert-Butyl chloride more reactive than n-butyl chloride in SN1 — 3° carbocation intermediate is more stable (hyperconjugation, +I) than a 1° carbocation would be; the rate-determining ionisation is faster.

(iv) Chloroethane less reactive than benzyl chloride — benzyl cation is resonance-stabilised; benzyl chloride reacts faster by SN1 (and SN2, due to sp³ benzylic C).

(v) Vinyl chloride less reactive than ethyl chloride — C–X is on sp² carbon (more s-character → shorter and stronger bond); vinylic cation is also highly unstable.

(vi) tert-Butyl chloride with KCN gives cyanide; with AgCN gives isocyanide — KCN is ionic, C-attack gives tert-butyl cyanide; AgCN is covalent, N-attack gives tert-butyl isocyanide. (Note: t-Bu is so sterically hindered that even with KCN the yield of tert-butyl cyanide is poor; elimination dominates. But the question is about the duality of CN⁻ attack site.)

(iv) Cyclohexane → Cyclohexylmethanol: Cyclohexane + Cl₂/hv → chlorocyclohexane (C₆H₁₁Cl). C₆H₁₁Cl + Mg/ether → C₆H₁₁MgCl. C₆H₁₁MgCl + HCHO (formaldehyde) → C₆H₁₁CH₂OMgCl → H₂O/H⁺ → C₆H₁₁CH₂OH (cyclohexylmethanol).

(v) Bromobenzene → 1-Phenylethanol (C₆H₅CH(OH)CH₃): C₆H₅Br + Mg/ether → C₆H₅MgBr. C₆H₅MgBr + CH₃CHO (acetaldehyde) → C₆H₅CH(OMgBr)CH₃ → H₂O/H⁺ → C₆H₅CH(OH)CH₃ (1-phenylethanol).

(vi) 2-Methylpropene → (CH₃)₃COH (tert-butanol): (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ + H₂O/H⁺ (Markovnikov hydration) → (CH₃)₃COH (tert-butyl alcohol).

(vii) Chlorobenzene → 4-Nitroaniline: C₆H₅Cl + conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → p-NO₂–C₆H₄–Cl (1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene). + aq. NaOH, 443 K → p-NO₂–C₆H₄–OH (4-nitrophenol). + Sn/HCl (or Fe/HCl) reduction of NO₂ to NH₂ → p-NH₂–C₆H₄–OH (4-aminophenol). Hmm — that's not 4-nitroaniline. Better path: C₆H₅Cl → (conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄) → p-Cl–C₆H₄–NO₂ → (NH₃, Cu, high T) → p-H₂N–C₆H₄–NO₂ (4-nitroaniline). Yes — direct amination of the C–Cl by NH₃ in the presence of copper (or via the SNAr route) at high T/pressure.

(viii) Styrene (C₆H₅CH=CH₂) → 2-Phenylethanol (C₆H₅CH₂CH₂OH): Anti-Markovnikov hydration via HBr then SN2: (a) + HBr (peroxide) → C₆H₅CH₂CH₂Br; (b) + aq. KOH → C₆H₅CH₂CH₂OH. Or (a) + B₂H₆ (hydroboration) then H₂O₂/OH⁻ → anti-Mark. hydration directly to C₆H₅CH₂CH₂OH (NCERT doesn't cover B₂H₆ in detail but it's standard).

(x) Bromobenzene → 1,1-Diphenylethylene (C₆H₅)₂C=CH₂: (a) C₆H₅Br + Mg/ether → C₆H₅MgBr. (b) 2 C₆H₅MgBr + CH₃COCH₃ (acetone) → (C₆H₅)₂C(OMgBr)CH₃ (after one addition, the alkoxide cannot react further). (c) Hydrolysis → (C₆H₅)₂C(OH)CH₃. (d) H₂SO₄, heat → dehydration → (C₆H₅)₂C=CH₂.

(xii) 1-Phenylpentane → 2-Bromo-1-phenylpentane: C₆H₅CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ + NBS or Br₂/hv (benzylic bromination) → C₆H₅CH(Br)CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ (2-bromo-1-phenylpentane). Selectivity is for the benzylic C–H because the resulting radical is resonance-stabilised.

❓ Q 6.15 — Free-radical chlorination of methane (mechanism)

Step 1 — Initiation: Cl–Cl ⟶ hv → 2 Cl• (homolysis).

Step 2 — Propagation:

(a) Cl• + CH₄ → CH₃• + HCl

(b) CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•

Step 3 — Termination (any of):

(a) Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂

(b) CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆ (ethane)

(c) CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl

Chain continues with the new Cl•. The product CH₃Cl can react further to give CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄.

❓ Q 6.16 — Equations

(i) Free radical chlorination of methane: CH₄ + Cl₂ ⟶ hv → CH₃Cl + HCl.

(ii) Markovnikov addition of HCl to propene: CH₃–CH=CH₂ + HCl → CH₃–CHCl–CH₃ (2-chloropropane).

(iii) Fittig reaction (bromobenzene + chloromethane + Na): C₆H₅Br + CH₃Cl + 2 Na → C₆H₅–CH₃ (toluene) + 2 NaX (Wurtz–Fittig).

(iv) Wurtz–Fittig (chlorobenzene + 1-chloropropane + Na): C₆H₅Cl + CH₃CH₂CH₂Cl + 2 Na → C₆H₅–CH₂CH₂CH₃ (propylbenzene) + 2 NaCl.

(v) Ullmann reaction (iodobenzene + Cu): 2 C₆H₅I + 2 Cu ⟶ Δ → C₆H₅–C₆H₅ (biphenyl) + 2 CuI.

(vi) Sandmeyer reaction (diazonium salt + CuCl/HCl): C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ ⟶ CuCl/HCl → C₆H₅Cl + N₂.

(vii) Addition of HBr to buta-1,3-diene: CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₂=CH–CHBr–CH₃ (1,2-addition) + CH₂Br–CH=CH–CH₃ (1,4-addition). At –80 °C, 1,2 dominates; at 40 °C, 1,4 dominates.

(viii) Allylation of benzene (allyl chloride + benzene + AlCl₃): C₆H₆ + CH₂=CH–CH₂Cl ⟶ AlCl₃ → C₆H₅–CH₂–CH=CH₂ (allylbenzene) + HCl.

(ix) Alkylation of anisole (CH₃OC₆H₅ + C₆H₅CH₂Cl + AlCl₃): p-CH₃O–C₆H₄–CH₂C₆H₅ (para-benzylanisole) + HCl. Anisole's –OCH₃ is a strong o/p director.

❓ Q 6.19 — Why anhydrous conditions for Grignard?

Grignard reagents (R–MgX) contain a highly polar C–Mg bond, with the carbon being strongly nucleophilic and basic. Even a trace of water reacts violently:

R–MgX + H₂O → R–H + Mg(OH)X

This destroys the Grignard, and the alkane R–H (useless for further reaction) is formed. Hence, the reaction must be carried out in anhydrous diethyl ether (or THF) with a dry apparatus.

❓ Q 6.20 — Why not H₂SO₄ with KI for alkyl iodides?

Concentrated H₂SO₄ oxidises HI (the HX produced) to I₂:

2 HI + H₂SO₄ → I₂ + SO₂ + 2 H₂O

This drastically reduces the yield of alkyl iodide. Instead, we use KI + H₃PO₄ (H₃PO₄ is non-oxidising).

❓ Q 6.21 — Why aryl halides are less reactive to nucleophilic substitution

Three reasons:

  1. Resonance: Lone pair on the halogen is delocalised into the benzene ring, giving the C–X bond partial double-bond character — shorter and stronger.
  2. Hybridisation: The C bearing X is sp² (33% s) — more s-character → shorter, stronger bond than sp³ (25% s).
  3. Instability of aryl cation: SN1 would require a phenyl cation (C₆H₅⁺), which is highly unstable because the empty orbital is in the ring plane and cannot be stabilised by resonance.
  4. Difficulty of benzyne mechanism: SN2 requires backside attack, but backside of an sp² C in a ring is blocked by the ring itself.
❓ Q 6.22 — Why p-nitrochlorobenzene is more reactive than chlorobenzene

The –NO₂ group is a strong –M (electron-withdrawing) group at the para position. It stabilises the anionic Meisenheimer intermediate (the σ-complex) formed when the nucleophile adds to the ring, by delocalising the negative charge onto the oxygen atoms of the –NO₂ group. This lowers the activation energy, so the SNAr reaction of p-nitrochlorobenzene is much faster than that of chlorobenzene.

❓ Q 6.23 — Haloarenes insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents

Although haloarenes are polar (C–X dipole), they cannot form hydrogen bonds with water because there is no –OH or –NH on them. The hydrophobic benzene ring also disfavours water solvation. They dissolve readily in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether, CHCl₃, CCl₄) where weak van der Waals and dipole-dipole interactions suffice.

❓ Q 6.24 — μ(chlorobenzene) < μ(cyclohexyl chloride) (Repeat of 6.6)

See Q 6.6 above. In short: in chlorobenzene, resonance delocalisation of Cl lone pair creates a dipole opposite to C–Cl, partially cancelling it.

❓ Q 6.25 — Why are freons banned?

Freons like CF₂Cl₂ release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere under UV light. Cl• catalytically destroys ozone:

CF₂Cl₂ ⟶ hv → •CF₂Cl + Cl•;   Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂;   ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂ (chain)

One Cl atom can destroy ~10⁵ O₃ molecules. This thins the ozone layer, increasing UV-B reaching Earth's surface → skin cancer, crop damage, ecological harm. Hence the Montreal Protocol (1987) banned CFCs. Replacement: HFCs (e.g., HFC-134a, CH₂FCF₃) and HCFCs that contain no Cl and have shorter atmospheric lifetimes.

❓ Q 6.31 — Why dehydrohalogenation gives more substituted alkene (Saytzeff)

The more substituted alkene is more stable because of:

  1. Hyperconjugation — more α-hydrogens on the C=C.
  2. +I effect of alkyl groups donating electron density to the sp² carbons.

Hence, E2 / E1 prefer the formation of but-2-ene (more substituted) over but-1-ene (less substituted) when 2-bromobutane is treated with alc. KOH.

⭐ Williamson rule (when to use Williamson vs elimination)

For ethers via Williamson: alkyl halide MUST be 1° (or 2°). Never use 3° halide — elimination wins.

Conversely, to make a 3° ether, take the 3° alcohol and add conc. H₂SO₄ at low T (SN1 path).

💡 "Haloalkanes love bases" — three fates
  • Strong nucleophile + 1° halide + polar aprotic → SN2 (substitution)
  • Strong base + 2°/3° halide + heat → E2 (elimination)
  • Weak nucleophile + 3°/benzyl/allyl + polar protic → SN1 (substitution via carbocation)
⚠️ Common pitfalls
  • AgCN → isocyanide; KCN → cyanide. Don't mix them up.
  • AgNO₂ → alkyl nitrite (R–O–N=O); KNO₂ → nitroalkane (R–NO₂).
  • CHCl₃ stored in dark bottles to prevent phosgene formation.
  • CCl₄ is toxic and a fire extinguisher, but it forms phosgene on heating — banned.
  • Free-radical halogenation: Cl is non-selective, Br is selective (3° > 2° > 1°).
  • Aryl halides don't give AgX with AgNO₃, do not give Wurtz with Na alone.
  • Saytzeff's rule → more substituted alkene is the major E2 product.
