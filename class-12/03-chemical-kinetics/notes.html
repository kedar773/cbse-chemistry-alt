1.1 Rate of a Chemical Reaction

The rate of reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant (or product) per unit time. For a general reaction:

REACTION aA + bB → cC + dD
AVG RATE Rateav = − 1a Δ[A]Δt = − 1b Δ[B]Δt = + 1c Δ[C]Δt = + 1d Δ[D]Δt
INSTANTANEOUS Rate = − 1a d[A]dt = + 1c d[C]dt

Units of rate: mol L−1 s−1 (or atm s−1 for gases).

1.2 Rate Law, Order & Molecularity ⚡ CBSE PYQ

Rate Law (Rate Equation)

RATE LAW Rate = k [A]x [B]y

where k = rate constant (specific reaction rate), and x, y are the orders w.r.t. A and B (determined experimentally, NOT from stoichiometry).

Order of Reaction

ORDER n = x + y (sum of powers of concentrations in rate law)
  • Order can be 0, 1, 2, 3 or fractional; it may be negative w.r.t. one species.
  • Order is experimentally determined — cannot be predicted from balanced equation.

Molecularity

  • Molecularity is the number of reacting species (atoms/ions/molecules) that must simultaneously collide in an elementary step.
  • Values: unimolecular (1), bimolecular (2), termolecular (3). Molecularity > 3 is extremely rare.
  • Molecularity is a theoretical concept, applies only to elementary reactions. For a complex reaction, we speak of the molecularity of the slowest (rate-determining) step.
⚠️ Key Exam Distinction:
Order → experimental, can be 0/fractional, applies to overall reaction.
Molecularity → theoretical, always a whole number ≥1, applies only to elementary steps.

1.3 Zero-Order Reaction — Full Derivation ⚡ CBSE PYQ

Definition: A reaction whose rate is independent of the concentration of the reactant.

RATE LAW Rate = k [A]0 = k
📘 Derivation of Integrated Rate Equation (Zero-Order)
Consider: R → Products, with rate = k
By definition: −d[R]/dt = k
Rearranging: d[R] = −k · dt
Integrate both sides: ∫ d[R] = −k ∫ dt
⇒ [R] = −kt + I    (where I = constant of integration)
At t = 0, [R] = [R]0  ⇒  [R]0 = −k(0) + I ⇒ I = [R]0
Substituting I:
ZERO-ORDER INTEGRATED [R] = [R]0 − kt    ⇔    k = [R]0 − [R]t

Half-Life Derivation (Zero-Order) ⚡ CBSE PYQ

At t = t1/2, [R] = [R]0/2
Substitute into integrated equation: [R]0/2 = [R]0 − k · t1/2
⇒ k · t1/2 = [R]0 − [R]0/2 = [R]0/2
ZERO-ORDER HALF-LIFE t1/2 = [R]02k    ∝ [R]0

1.4 First-Order Reaction — Full Derivation 🔥 MOST TESTED

RATE LAW Rate = k [R]1
📘 Derivation of Integrated Rate Equation (First-Order)
Consider: R → Products, with −d[R]/dt = k[R]
Separate variables: d[R]/[R] = −k · dt
Integrate: ∫ d[R]/[R] = −k ∫ dt
⇒ ln[R] = −kt + I
At t = 0, [R] = [R]0 ⇒ ln[R]0 = I
∴ ln[R] = −kt + ln[R]0
Rearranging: ln([R]/[R]0) = −kt   ⇒   [R] = [R]0 · e−kt
FIRST-ORDER INTEGRATED k = 2.303t log10 [R]0[R]

Half-Life Derivation (First-Order) ⚡ CBSE PYQ

At t = t1/2, [R] = [R]0/2
Substitute: k = (2.303 / t1/2) log([R]0 / ([R]0/2))
⇒ k = (2.303 / t1/2) log(2)
Since log(2) = 0.3010, we get k = 2.303 × 0.3010 / t1/2 = 0.693 / t1/2
FIRST-ORDER HALF-LIFE t1/2 = 0.693k    (independent of [R]0)
📌 Exam Tip: For a first-order reaction, t99.9% = 10 × t1/2 (since 210 ≈ 1000 ⇒ 10 half-lives reduces [R] to 0.1%). 📌 See Solved Q 4.10

Proof: t99.9% = 10 × t50% for First-Order ⚡ CBSE PYQ — Expected

For t99.9%, [R] = [R]0 − 0.999[R]0 = 0.001 [R]0
k = (2.303 / t99.9%) log([R]0 / 0.001[R]0) = (2.303 / t99.9%) log(1000)
Since log(1000) = 3:   k = (2.303 × 3) / t99.9% = 6.909 / t99.9%   …(i)
Also, k = 0.693 / t1/2   …(ii)
Equating (i) and (ii): 6.909 / t99.9% = 0.693 / t1/2
∴ t99.9% = (6.909 / 0.693) · t1/2 ≈ 10 · t1/2   ∎

Proof: t99% = 2 × t90% for First-Order ⚡ CBSE PYQ

For t90%: [R] = 0.1[R]0 ⇒ k = (2.303/t90%) log(10) = 2.303 / t90%
For t99%: [R] = 0.01[R]0 ⇒ k = (2.303/t99%) log(100) = 4.606 / t99%
Equating: 2.303 / t90% = 4.606 / t99%
∴ t99% = 2 · t90%   ∎

1.5 Second-Order Reaction (Brief)

RATE LAW Rate = k [A]2 (or k[A][B])
Derivation
−d[A]/dt = k[A]2 ⇒ d[A]/[A]2 = −k dt
∫ d[A]/[A]2 = −k ∫ dt ⇒ −1/[A] = −kt + I
At t=0: −1/[A]0 = I
SECOND-ORDER INTEGRATED 1[A] − 1[A]0 = kt   ⇒   t1/2 = 1k[A]0

1.6 Pseudo-First-Order Reactions ⚡ CBSE PYQ

A reaction that is not truly first-order but behaves as first-order under specific conditions (usually when one reactant is in large excess, so its concentration remains practically constant).

EXAMPLE Hydrolysis of ethyl acetate: CH3COOC2H5 + H2O → CH3COOH + C2H5OH

Actual rate = k[CH3COOC2H5][H2O]. Since water is in large excess, [H2O] ≈ constant = k', so:

PSEUDO Rate = k'[CH3COOC2H5]   where k' = k[H2O]

Another classic example: Inversion of cane sugar (sucrose + H2O → glucose + fructose) catalyzed by H+.

1.7 Method to Determine Order

  1. Initial Rate Method (Ostwald's Isolation): Vary one reactant at a time while keeping others constant; find ratio of rates to get order.
  2. Integrated Rate Law Method (Hit-and-Trial): Substitute [A], t into zero, first, second-order integrated equations. The equation giving a constant k identifies the order.
  3. Graphical Method: Plot [A] vs t, ln[A] vs t, 1/[A] vs t. The one yielding a straight line determines the order.
  4. Half-Life Method: If t1/2 ∝ a1−n, compare half-lives at different initial concentrations.
HALF-LIFE METHOD t1/2(1)t1/2(2) = [A]0(2)[A]0(1)n−1

2.1 Effect of Temperature on Rate — Arrhenius Equation 🔥 CORE

An increase of 10 °C (or 10 K) in temperature typically doubles the rate of a reaction (temperature coefficient ≈ 2).

TEMP COEFF μ = k(T+10)kT ≈ 2 to 3

Arrhenius Equation ⚡ CBSE PYQ

ARRHENIUS k = A · e−Ea / RT

where A = frequency factor (pre-exponential factor), Ea = activation energy (J mol−1), R = 8.314 J K−1 mol−1, T = temperature (K).

Logarithmic form
Take ln: ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT)
Convert to log10: log k = log A − Ea/(2.303 RT)
ARRHENIUS LOG FORM log k = log A − Ea2.303 RT

A plot of log k vs 1/T gives a straight line with slope = −Ea/(2.303R) and intercept = log A.

Two-Temperature Form ⚡ CBSE PYQ — Expected

At T1: log k1 = log A − Ea/(2.303 R T1) …(i)
At T2: log k2 = log A − Ea/(2.303 R T2) …(ii)
Subtract (i) from (ii):
TWO-TEMP FORM log k2k1 = Ea2.303 R · T2 − T1T1T2

This is the single most tested numerical formula in CBSE board exams. 📌 See Solved Q 4.17, 4.18, 4.20

2.2 Activation Energy & Energy Profile ⚡ CBSE PYQ

Activation energy (Ea) is the minimum extra energy required by a reacting molecule to get converted into product. It equals the difference between the threshold energy and the average energy of the reactants:

DEFINITION Ea = Threshold Energy − Average Energy of Reactants
Fig. 2.1 — Energy profile showing Ea, threshold energy, and catalyzed (dashed red) path.
  • Catalyst effect: Provides an alternate pathway of lower activation energy Ea(cat) < Ea, thereby increasing the rate. It does NOT change ΔH or the equilibrium constant.
  • A 10 K rise in T nearly doubles the rate mainly because it doubles the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea (see Maxwell-Boltzmann).

2.3 Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution ⚡ CBSE PYQ

The distribution of kinetic energies among gas molecules is given by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. Only molecules with energy ≥ Ea (threshold) can react.

Fig. 2.2 — At T+10 K, the peak shifts right & flattens; the shaded area (fraction with E ≥ Ea) approximately doubles, doubling the rate.
Key conclusions from the graph:
  • Most probable kinetic energy increases with temperature.
  • Fraction of molecules with E ≥ Ea (shaded region) increases sharply with T.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea (shifts the vertical line left), increasing the shaded fraction.

2.4 Collision Theory ⚡ CBSE PYQ

Based on the kinetic theory of gases, a reaction occurs when molecules collide with sufficient energy and proper orientation.

RATE Rate = P · ZAB · e−Ea/RT
  • ZAB = collision frequency (number of collisions per second per unit volume between A and B)
  • e−Ea/RT = fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea
  • P = steric (probability/orientation) factor
  • P · ZAB = A (frequency factor of Arrhenius equation)

Hence: Rate = A · e−Ea/RT

Conditions for effective collision:

  1. Molecules must have energy ≥ Ea (threshold energy).
  2. Molecules must have proper orientation at the moment of collision.

Q 4.1 (In-Text) — For the reaction R → P, concentration of R changes from 0.03 M to 0.02 M in 25 min. Calculate average rate using units of (i) min, (ii) s. ✓ Easy

Given: [R]1 = 0.03 M, [R]2 = 0.02 M, Δt = 25 min

Formula: Rateav = −Δ[R]/Δt

Δ[R] = 0.02 − 0.03 = −0.01 M

(i) Rate = −(−0.01) / 25 = 4 × 10−4 M min−1

(ii) Δt = 25 × 60 = 1500 s ⇒ Rate = 0.01 / 1500 = 6.67 × 10−6 M s−1

Q 4.2 — In reaction 2A → Products, [A] decreases from 0.5 to 0.4 mol L−1 in 10 min. Calculate rate during this interval. ✓ Easy

Rate = −(1/2)·Δ[A]/Δt = −(1/2)·(0.4 − 0.5)/10 = (1/2)·(0.1/10) = 5 × 10−3 mol L−1 min−1

Q 4.3 — For reaction X2 + 2Y2 + 2Z → XY2Z2, doubling [Y2] quadruples rate; changing [X2] or [Z] has no effect. (i) Write rate law. (ii) Find rate if [X2] doubled and [Y2] tripled. ⚡ CBSE PYQ

(i) Doubling [Y2] ⇒ rate × 4, so order w.r.t. Y2 = 2 (since 22 = 4).

Changing [X2] or [Z] has no effect ⇒ order w.r.t. both = 0.

Rate law: Rate = k[Y2]2

(ii) If [Y2] tripled, new rate = k(3[Y2])2 = 9 × original rate. (Doubling [X2] has no effect.)

Q 4.4 — Conversion of cyclopropane to propene is first order, k = 2.714 × 10−5 s−1, [cyclopropane]0 = 0.290 M. Find [cyclopropane] after 100 s.

k = (2.303/t) log(a/(a−x))

2.714 × 10−5 = (2.303/100) log(0.290/[A])

log(0.290/[A]) = 2.714 × 10−5 × 100 / 2.303 = 1.178 × 10−3

0.290/[A] = 100.001178 = 1.00272

[A] = 0.289 M

Q 4.5 — Decomposition of N2O5: 2N2O5 → 4NO2 + O2 is first order with k = 6.2 × 10−4 min−1. Calculate t1/2.

t1/2 = 0.693/k = 0.693 / (6.2 × 10−4) = 1117.7 min ≈ 18.63 hours

Q 4.6 — Rate constant for first order reaction is 60 s−1. How much time to reduce 1 g to 1/16th? ⚡ CBSE PYQ

t = (2.303/k) log([R]0/[R]) = (2.303/60) log(16)

= (2.303/60) × 1.2041 = 0.0462 s = 4.62 × 10−2 s

Alternate: 16 = 24, so 4 half-lives. t1/2 = 0.693/60 = 0.01155 s. Total = 4 × 0.01155 = 0.0462 s ✓

Q 4.7 — Rate quadruples when [A] increases by 10 times. Find order.

Rate ∝ [A]n. 4 = 10n ⇒ n = log(4)/log(10) = 0.602/1 ≈ 0.6 (≈ 3/5)

Q 4.8 — Ea = 104.4 kJ mol−1, A = 6.0 × 1014 s−1 at 25 °C. Find k.

k = A · e−Ea/RT

Ea/RT = 104400 / (8.314 × 298) = 42.14

e−42.14 = 5.01 × 10−19

k = 6.0 × 1014 × 5.01 × 10−19 = 3.0 × 10−4 s−1

Q 4.9 — A first-order reaction has k = 1.15 × 10−3 s−1. How long will 5 g take to become 3 g?

t = (2.303/k) log([R]0/[R]) = (2.303/1.15×10−3) log(5/3)

= 2002.6 × 0.2218 = 444 s ≈ 7.4 min

Example 4.4 — In a pseudo-first-order hydrolysis of ester, data: t = 30, 60, 90 s; [ester] = 0.31, 0.17, 0.085 M. Show it is first order and find average k.

Use k = (2.303/t) log(a/(a−x)) with a = 0.55 M (initial).

At t=30: k = (2.303/30) log(0.55/0.31) = 0.0767 × 0.2484 = 1.907 × 10−2 s−1

At t=60: k = (2.303/60) log(0.55/0.17) = 0.0384 × 0.5098 = 1.957 × 10−2 s−1

At t=90: k = (2.303/90) log(0.55/0.085) = 0.02559 × 0.8108 = 2.074 × 10−2 s−1

Since k values are nearly constant, reaction is first order.

kav = (1.907 + 1.957 + 2.074) × 10−2 / 3 = 1.98 × 10−2 s−1

Example 4.5 — t1/2 = 5 × 104 s. What % of N2O5 reacts in 2 hrs?

k = 0.693 / (5×104) = 1.386 × 10−5 s−1

t = 2 hr = 7200 s

log([R]0/[R]) = 0.04337 ⇒ [R]0/[R] = 1.105 ⇒ [R]/[R]0 = 0.905

∴ % reacted = (1 − 0.905) × 100 = 9.5%

Number of half-lives n = 240/60 = 4

[A] = [A]0/24 = [A]0/16 = 0.0625 [A]0

∴ % remaining = 6.25%

Example 4.10 — Show that t99.9% = 10 × t1/2 for first-order. 🔥 Board Favorite

For 99.9% completion: [A] = 0.001 [A]0

t99.9% = (2.303/k) log([A]0/0.001[A]0) = (2.303/k) log(1000) = (2.303 × 3)/k = 6.909/k

Q 4.1 — Reaction rate doubles when temperature rises from 298 K to 308 K. Find Ea. ⚡ CBSE PYQ — Expected

k2/k1 = 2, T1 = 298 K, T2 = 308 K

log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2.303R) · (T2−T1)/(T1T2)

log 2 = (Ea / (2.303 × 8.314)) · (10 / (298 × 308))

0.3010 = (Ea / 19.147) × 1.089 × 10−4

Ea = 0.3010 × 19.147 / 1.089 × 10−4 = 52,898 J mol−1 ≈ 52.9 kJ mol−1

d[NO2]/dt = 4 × 4.2 × 10−7 = 1.68 × 10−6 M s−1

d[O2]/dt = 4.2 × 10−7 M s−1

Q 4.18 — First-order: 10% completion at 298 K equals 25% completion at 308 K. Find Ea. ⚡ CBSE PYQ — Expected

At 298 K: k1 = (2.303/t1) log(100/90) = 0.1054/t1

At 308 K: k2 = (2.303/t2) log(100/75) = 0.2877/t2

Since times are equal (t1 = t2): k2/k1 = 0.2877/0.1054 = 2.7296

Q 4.19 — A reaction is first order in A and second order in B. (i) Write rate law. (ii) Effect of tripling [B]. (iii) Effect of doubling both.

(i) Rate = k[A][B]2

(ii) Tripling [B]: rate becomes k[A](3B)2 = 9k[A][B]2 ⇒ rate × 9

(iii) Doubling both: k(2A)(2B)2 = 8k[A][B]2 ⇒ rate × 8

Q 4.22 — First-order decomposition: A → B + C. Initial pressure of A = Pi, total pressure at time t = Pt. Derive k in terms of Pi and Pt. ⚡ CBSE PYQ

At t=0: PA = Pi. At t=t: let x decompose.

PA = Pi − x, PB = x, PC = x.

Total: Pt = (Pi − x) + x + x = Pi + x ⇒ x = Pt − Pi

PA at t = Pi − (Pt − Pi) = 2Pi − Pt

k = (2.303/t) log[ Pi / (2Pi − Pt) ]

Q 4.23 — Define: (i) order, (ii) activation energy. State two differences between order and molecularity.

(i) Order = sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law; determined experimentally.

(ii) Ea = minimum extra energy required by reactant molecules to form the activated complex.

Differences:

  • Order can be 0/fractional/negative; molecularity is always a positive integer ≥1.
  • Order is experimental; molecularity is theoretical (from mechanism).
Q 4.24 — Explain: (i) Why rate changes with temperature? (ii) Why explosion rate is very high?

(i) ↑T increases the fraction of molecules having energy ≥ Ea (Maxwell-Boltzmann). Hence more effective collisions per unit time → rate ↑.

(ii) In an explosion, large number of activated molecules + highly exothermic reaction → rapid self-sustaining chain reaction → very high rate.

Q 4.25 — For 2A + B → C + D, find rate law and k from data: [A]=0.1,[B]=0.1,rate=6×10−3; [A]=0.3,[B]=0.2,rate=7.2×10−2; [A]=0.3,[B]=0.4,rate=2.88×10−1; [A]=0.4,[B]=0.1,rate=2.4×10−2.

Let rate = k[A]x[B]y.

Compare (ii) & (iii): [A] same, [B] doubled ⇒ rate × 4 ⇒ y = 2.

Compare (i) & (iv): [B] same, [A] × 4 ⇒ rate × 4 ⇒ x = 1.

Rate = k[A][B]2 (Order = 3)

k = 6×10−3 / (0.1 × 0.12) = 6×10−3 / 10−3 = 6.0 L2 mol−2 min−1
