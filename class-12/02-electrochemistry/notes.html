1. Electrochemical Cells & Nernst Equation

1.1 What is Electrochemistry?

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy through redox reactions. All processes at electrodes are governed by the transfer of electrons between a chemical system and an electrical circuit.

  • Galvanic (Voltaic) cell — spontaneous redox → electrical energy (e.g., Daniell cell, batteries).
  • Electrolytic cell — electrical energy → non-spontaneous chemical change (e.g., electroplating, extraction of metals).

⚖️ Sign & Flow Conventions — Galvanic vs. Electrolytic

A common board trap! Memorise the sign convention carefully. The anode is always the site of oxidation and the cathode is always the site of reduction — but the electrical sign of each electrode flips between a galvanic and an electrolytic cell.

🔋 Galvanic Cell (Spontaneous, ΔG < 0)

Anode (−)   Oxidation: Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−

Cathode (+)   Reduction: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu

• Electrons flow externally: Anode → Cathode through the wire.
• Conventional current flows: Cathode → Anode externally.
• Cations in solution move toward the cathode; anions move toward the anode (via salt bridge).

⚡ Electrolytic Cell (Non-spontaneous, ΔG > 0)

Anode (+)   Oxidation: 2Cl− → Cl2(g) + 2e−

Cathode (−)   Reduction: Na+ + e− → Na

• External battery drives electrons: Cathode ← Anode through the wire (electrons enter at the cathode from battery).
• Cations move toward the cathode (−); anions toward the anode (+) — same ion-movement rule as galvanic.
• Sign convention flips: anode is + (connected to + of battery) and cathode is − (connected to − of battery).

📌 Mnemonic: “Red Cat” — Reduction always occurs at the Cathode (in both cells). The sign attached to the cathode is what changes.

1.2 Daniell Cell — A Classic Galvanic Cell

Set-up: a Zn rod dipped in 1 M ZnSO4 (left half-cell) and a Cu rod dipped in 1 M CuSO4 (right half-cell). The two solutions are connected by a salt bridge (usually KCl or KNO3 in agar-agar gel), and the electrodes are joined by a metal wire with a voltmeter.

Half-cell reactions

Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−   (Anode, oxidation, E° = −0.76 V)
Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s)   (Cathode, reduction, E° = +0.34 V)
Net: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

Salt Bridge — why is it needed?

  • Completes the circuit by allowing ion flow without mixing the two solutions.
  • Maintains electrical neutrality: K+ flows into the cathode compartment (where Cu2+ is being consumed), Cl− (or NO3−) flows into the anode compartment (where Zn2+ is being produced).
  • Without it, charge builds up and the cell stops within seconds.

1.3 Cell Notation (IUPAC Convention) CBSE PYQ – Expected

A cell is represented in the form:

Anode  |  Anode solution (conc.)  ‖  Cathode solution (conc.)  |  Cathode
  • Single vertical bar ( | ) → phase boundary (e.g., solid | aqueous, or | for a single electrode interface).
  • Double vertical bar ( ‖ ) → salt bridge between the two half-cells.
  • By convention: anode is written on the LEFT, cathode on the RIGHT.
  • If a gas is involved, an inert electrode (usually Pt) is added.

Worked Examples

Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s)   (Daniell cell)
Pt(s) | H2(g, 1 bar) | H+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s)   (Hydrogen–Copper cell)
Mg(s) | Mg2+(0.001 M) ‖ Ag+(0.0001 M) | Ag(s)   (Concentration cell-like setup)

1.4 Standard Electrode Potential

The standard electrode potential (E°) of an electrode is its potential relative to the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE), measured at 1 M concentration, 1 bar pressure, 298 K. By convention, E°SHE = 0.00 V.

Properties:

  • Reduction potential is the standard convention. The more positive E°, the greater the tendency to get reduced.
  • For oxidation, E°oxidation = −E°reduction.
  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode   (both as reduction potentials). CBSE PYQ – Expected
  • If E°cell > 0 → cell is spontaneous (galvanic). If E°cell < 0 → non-spontaneous (electrolytic).

Electrochemical Series — Useful Mnemonics

Top (strongest reducing agents) → Bottom (strongest oxidising agents):

Li · K · Ba · Ca · Na · Mg · Al · Zn · Fe · Ni · Sn · Pb · H · Cu · Hg · Ag · Au · F2

Species above H reduce H+ to H2; species below H+ are reduced by H2.

1.5 Nernst Equation CBSE PYQ – Expected Derivation

For a general cell reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

The Nernst equation relates the cell EMF to concentrations:

Ecell = E°cell − RT nF ln Q  =  E°cell − 2.303 RT nF log Q

At 298 K:

Ecell = E°cell − 0.0591 n log [C]c [D]d [A]a [B]b

where n = number of electrons transferred, F = 96485 C mol−1, Q = reaction quotient.

Derivation (Gibbs Energy → EMF)

  1. For a reversible cell, the maximum work is electrical: Wmax = nFEcell
  2. For a non-standard reaction, change in Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q
  3. Since ΔG = −nFEcell and ΔG° = −nFE°cell
  4. Substituting: −nFEcell = −nFE°cell + RT ln Q
  5. Divide by −nF: Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q

📌 See Solved Q 3.3, 3.5 for board-style Nernst calculations.

1.6 Gibbs Energy & Equilibrium Constant

ΔrG° = −nFE°cell   ·   ΔrG = −nFEcell

At equilibrium, ΔG = 0 and Ecell = 0, so:

0 = ΔrG° + RT ln K  ⟹  ln K = nFE°cell RT  ⟹  log K = nE°cell 0.0591
  • Large positive E°cell → very large K → products strongly favoured.
  • ΔG° > 0 ⟺ E° < 0 ⟺ K < 1 ⟺ reaction non-spontaneous under standard conditions.

1.7 Concentration Cells

A concentration cell has identical electrodes on both sides but with different ion concentrations. Since the standard potentials cancel, E°cell = 0 and the EMF is driven purely by the concentration difference.

Ecell = − 0.0591 n log [Anode] [Cathode]   (at 298 K)

2.1 Resistance, Conductance, Resistivity

For a conductor of uniform cross-section, Ohm's law analogously gives:

R = ρ · lA
  • R = resistance (Ω)
  • ρ = resistivity (Ω m) — also called specific resistance
  • l = length (m), A = cross-sectional area (m2)
  • Conductance G = 1/R (S = siemens or Ω−1)
  • Conductivity κ (kappa) = 1/ρ (S m−1) — also called specific conductance
  • Cell constant G* = l/A (m−1), measured using a standard KCl solution.

2.2 Molar Conductivity (Λm) CBSE PYQ – Expected

Molar conductivity is the conducting power of all the ions produced by 1 mole of electrolyte in a given volume of solution.

Λm = κ × 1000 c   (S cm2 mol−1)   or   Λm = κ c (S m2 mol−1)

where c is concentration in mol L−1 (or mol m−3).

Variation with Dilution

  • For strong electrolytes: Λm increases gently with dilution and approaches a limiting value Λ°m (Kohlrausch's limiting law).
  • For weak electrolytes: Λm rises sharply on dilution (degree of dissociation α increases sharply).
Λm = Λ°m − A √c   (Debye–Hückel–Onsager, strong electrolytes)

2.3 Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions CBSE PYQ – Expected

At infinite dilution, the molar conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of the limiting molar conductivities of its constituent ions, each multiplied by the number of ions produced per formula unit:

Λ°m(AxBy) = x · λ°(Ay+) + y · λ°(Bx−)

Where λ° is the limiting molar conductivity of an individual ion.

Application 1: Degree of Dissociation (α)

α = Λm Λ°m

Application 2: Dissociation Constant Ka of a Weak Acid

Ka = c α2 1 − α = c Λm2 Λ°m2 (Λ°m − Λm)

Application 3: Λ°m of a Weak Electrolyte from Strong-Electrolyte Data

Example — Λ°m(CH3COOH):

Λ°m(CH3COOH) = Λ°m(CH3COONa) + Λ°m(HCl) − Λ°m(NaCl)

2.4 Electrolytic Cells & Electrolysis CBSE PYQ – Expected

An electrolytic cell uses external electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. The key components are:

  • Two electrodes immersed in an electrolyte (molten or aqueous solution of an ionic compound).
  • An external DC source (battery) connected across the electrodes.
  • On passing current, ions migrate: cations to the cathode (−), anions to the anode (+).

Preferential Discharge Theory (selectivity of electrodes)

When more than one ion is present, the ion with the higher reduction potential (i.e., easier to be reduced) is discharged at the cathode. Likewise, the species with the higher oxidation potential is discharged at the anode.

Active vs. Inert Electrodes

Inert Electrode (e.g., Pt, Graphite)

Does not participate in the reaction — only provides a surface for electron transfer.

E.g., electrolysis of aqueous NaCl with Pt electrodes → Cl2 at anode, H2 at cathode.

Active Electrode (e.g., Cu, Ag)

Participates in the reaction — the electrode itself may dissolve or deposit.

E.g., electroplating of copper onto an iron spoon using a copper anode.

2.5 Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

First Law CBSE PYQ – Expected

The mass of substance liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the total charge passed through the electrolyte.

m = Z · I · t = (E · I · t) / 96485

where Z = electrochemical equivalent (g C−1) = M / (n × 96485) = E / 96485, with E = equivalent weight = M / n.

Second Law

When the same quantity of charge is passed through different electrolytes in series, the masses of substances liberated are proportional to their equivalent weights.

m1m2 = E1E2

Combined Form & Constant (F)

1 F = 96485 C mol−1 = charge of 1 mole of electrons
m = M · I · t n · 96485

2.6 Products of Electrolysis (Board Favourite Table)

ElectrolyteAnode (oxidation)Cathode (reduction)
Molten NaCl (Pt electrodes)Cl2(g)Na(l)
Aqueous NaCl (Pt, conc. NaCl)Cl2(g)H2(g)
Aqueous NaCl (Pt, dilute)O2(g)H2(g)
Aqueous CuSO4 (Pt)O2(g)Cu(s)
Aqueous CuSO4 (Cu anode)Cu dissolves → Cu2+Cu(s) deposits (refining)
Dilute H2SO4 (Pt)O2(g)H2(g)

Reasoning: at cathode, H2O is reduced in preference to Na+ (because E° of H2O/H2 is higher than that of Na+/Na in neutral water). At anode in dilute NaCl, water is oxidised to O2 rather than Cl− being oxidised, because of overvoltage effects.

2.7 Applications of Electrolysis

  • Electrorefining of copper (impure Cu anode, pure Cu cathode, CuSO4 electrolyte).
  • Electroplating (article = cathode, plating metal = anode, salt of plating metal = electrolyte).
  • Electrometallurgy — extraction of Na, Mg, Al from their molten salts.
  • Manufacture of chemicals — Cl2, NaOH (chlor-alkali process), H2, O2.

Intext Questions (3.1 – 3.8)

3.1 How would you determine the standard electrode potential of the system Mg2+/Mg?

You set up a cell in which the Mg/Mg2+ half-cell is coupled with the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) (which is assigned E° = 0.00 V). The emf of this cell, measured using a high-impedance voltmeter, gives the standard electrode potential of the Mg2+/Mg system.

Cell notation:

Mg(s) | Mg2+(1 M) ‖ H+(1 M) | H2(g, 1 bar) | Pt(s)
The measured E°cell = −2.36 V, so E°(Mg2+/Mg) = −2.36 V.
3.2 Can you store copper sulphate solutions in a zinc pot? CBSE PYQ – Expected

No. Zinc is more reactive than copper (E°(Zn2+/Zn) = −0.76 V; E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V). Zinc will be oxidised by Cu2+ ions:

Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)
E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

The reaction is spontaneous because E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V > 0. Zinc dissolves, copper deposits — the solution gets contaminated with Zn2+ and loses Cu2+. (Hence copper sulphate is stored in copper or glass containers, not zinc.)

3.3 Calculate the potential of a Fe3+/Fe2+ half-cell at 298 K when [Fe3+] = 0.01 M and [Fe2+] = 1.0 M. E° = +0.77 V. CBSE PYQ – Expected
Reaction: Fe3+ + e− → Fe2+   (n = 1)

Nernst equation:
E = E° − (0.0591/n) log [Fe2+]/[Fe3+]
E = 0.77 − (0.0591/1) log(1.0 / 0.01)
E = 0.77 − 0.0591 × log(100)
E = 0.77 − 0.0591 × 2 = 0.77 − 0.1182
E = +0.652 V
3.4 Calculate E°cell and ΔG° for the cell: Zn | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu.
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V
n = 2, F = 96485 C mol−1
ΔG° = −nFE°cell = −2 × 96485 × 1.10
ΔG° = −212267 J mol−1 ≈ −212.3 kJ mol−1

Negative ΔG° confirms the cell reaction is spontaneous.

3.5 The cell Pt, H2(1 bar) | HCl(0.5 M) ‖ H+(1 M) | H2(1 bar), Pt has Ecell = 0.111 V at 298 K. Find the pH of the 0.5 M HCl solution. Cross-ref Tab 1 §1.5

This is a hydrogen concentration cell. Cell reaction: H2(1 bar) + 2H+(1 M) → 2H+(0.5 M) + H2(1 bar). E°cell = 0.

For a concentration cell, the Nernst form (anode = 0.5 M HCl, cathode = 1 M HCl, n = 2):

Ecell = −0.05912 log [0.51]2

But here E° ≠ 0 because of activity corrections. We use the SHE convention:

E = 0 − 0.05911 log 1[H+] = 0.0591 × pH
0.111 = 0.0591 × pH ⟹ pH ≈ 1.88

📌 Note: NCERT answer pH = 1.88. (i.e., [H+] ≈ 0.013 M ≈ theoretical 0.5 M before activity correction).

3.6 For a concentration cell, write the Nernst equation. Cross-ref Tab 1 §1.7

For a concentration cell with same electrodes, E°cell = 0:

Ecell = −(0.0591/n) log [Anode] / [Cathode]   (at 298 K)
Ecell = (0.0591/n) log [Cathode] / [Anode]   (equivalently)
3.7 The conductivity of 0.20 M solution of KCl at 298 K is 0.0248 S cm−1. Calculate its molar conductivity.
Λm = (κ × 1000) / c = (0.0248 × 1000) / 0.20 = 24.8 / 0.20
Λm = 124.0 S cm2 mol−1
3.8 The resistance of a conductivity cell containing 0.001 M KCl solution at 298 K is 1500 Ω. Cell constant = 0.50 cm−1. Calculate (a) conductivity, (b) molar conductivity of the solution. CBSE PYQ – Expected Cross-ref Tab 2 §2.1, 2.2
(a) κ = cell constantR = 0.501500 = 3.33 × 10−4 S cm−1

(b) Λm = κ × 1000c = 3.33 × 10−4 × 10000.001 = 0.3330.001
Λm = 333 S cm2 mol−1

NCERT Exercise (Selected)

Ex 3.1 Arrange the following metals in increasing order of reducing power: Ag, Mg, Cu, Fe. Justify.
Reducing power ∝ tendency to lose electrons, which ∝ −(reduction potential). Standard reduction potentials:

Ag+/Ag = +0.80 V
Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V
Fe2+/Fe = −0.44 V
Mg2+/Mg = −2.36 V

Lower (more negative) reduction potential → stronger reducing agent. So increasing reducing power:
Ag < Cu < Fe < Mg
Ex 3.2 Given the standard electrode potentials: K+/K = −2.93 V, Ag+/Ag = +0.80 V, Hg2+/Hg = +0.79 V, Mg2+/Mg = −2.37 V, Cr3+/Cr = −0.74 V. Arrange them in increasing order of reducing power.

Lower reduction potential = stronger reducing agent. Sorted in increasing order of reducing power (i.e., decreasing reduction potential):

Ag < Hg < Cr < Mg < K
Ex 3.3 Depict the galvanic cell in which the reaction: Zn(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + 2Ag(s) takes place. Further show: (i) which electrode is negatively charged, (ii) the carriers of current in the cell, (iii) the individual reactions at each electrode.

Cell representation:

Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) ‖ Ag+(aq) | Ag(s)

(i) Zn is the negative electrode (anode) because it loses electrons.

(ii) Carriers: electrons in the external wire (Zn → Ag); ions in the solution (Zn2+ into solution, Ag+ from solution), and ions in the salt bridge.

(iii) Reactions at each electrode:

Anode (oxidation): Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−
Cathode (reduction): Ag+(aq) + e− → Ag(s) (× 2)
Ex 3.5 The standard electrode potential for Daniell cell is 1.1 V. Calculate the standard Gibbs energy for the reaction: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s).
ΔG° = −nFE°cell = −2 × 96485 × 1.1
ΔG° = −212267 J mol−1 ≈ −212.3 kJ mol−1
Ex 3.7 Why does the cell potential of a mercury cell remain constant during its entire life?
Because the overall cell reaction does not involve any ion in solution whose concentration can change:

Zn(Hg) + HgO(s) → ZnO(s) + Hg(l)

The products and reactants are all pure solids/liquids (activity = 1), so the Nernst equation gives a constant EMF of 1.35 V throughout the lifetime of the cell.
Ex 3.8 Define molar conductivity. How is it related to conductivity?
Molar conductivity (Λm) is the conducting power of all the ions produced by 1 mole of an electrolyte in solution. It is related to conductivity (κ) by:

Λm = (κ × 1000) / M   (S cm2 mol−1)

where M is molarity (mol L−1). Units: S cm2 mol−1.

Ex 3.10 How much charge is required for the following reductions: (i) 1 mol of Al3+ to Al, (ii) 1 mol of Cu2+ to Cu, (iii) 1 mol of MnO4− to Mn2+?

Using Q = nF, with F = 96485 C mol−1:

(i) Al3+ + 3e− → Al  : n = 3, Q = 3 × 96485 = 2.89 × 105 C
(ii) Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu  : n = 2, Q = 2 × 96485 = 1.93 × 105 C
(iii) MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O  : n = 5, Q = 5 × 96485 = 4.82 × 105 C
Ex 3.11 How much electricity in terms of Faraday is required to produce: (i) 20.0 g of Ca from molten CaCl2, (ii) 40.0 g of Al from molten Al2O3?
Using m = M·I·tn·F and Q = nF, the charge required: Q = m × n × FM

(i) Ca: M = 40 g mol−1, n = 2, m = 20 g
Q = 20 × 2 × 9648540 = 96485 C = 1 F

(ii) Al: M = 27 g mol−1, n = 3, m = 40 g
Q = 40 × 3 × 9648527 ≈ 428822 C = ≈ 4.44 F
Ex 3.12 How much electricity is required to produce 2.0 g of H2 from water? (Hint: 2H2O + 2e− → H2 + 2OH−)

From the reaction, 1 mol H2 requires 2 mol e− (n = 2).

Moles of H2 = 2.02 = 1.0 mol

Q = n × F = 2 × 96485 = 1.93 × 105 C ≈ 2 F
Ex 3.14 A solution of CuSO4 is electrolysed for 10 min with a current of 1.5 A. Calculate the mass of Cu deposited at the cathode.
Charge Q = I × t = 1.5 A × (10 × 60) s = 900 C
Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu, n = 2, M(Cu) = 63.5 g mol−1
m = M × I × tn × F = 63.5 × 9002 × 96485
m = 57150192970 ≈ 0.296 g
Ex 3.15 Suggest a way to determine the value of Λ°m for CH3COOH (a weak electrolyte). CBSE PYQ – Expected Cross-ref Tab 2 §2.3

Measure Λm for a series of strong electrolytes: HCl, NaOH, CH3COONa, NaCl. Use Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions:

Λ°m(CH3COOH) = λ°(H+) + λ°(CH3COO−)
= Λ°m(HCl) + Λ°m(CH3COONa) − Λ°m(NaCl)

Or, equivalently:

Λ°m(CH3COOH) = Λ°m(CH3COONa) + Λ°m(HCl) − Λ°m(NaCl)
